    Structure of Atom | Dual Nature of Matter and Uncertainty Principle#

    To overcome the shortcomings of Bohr's model, a more general model of the atom was needed. Two important developments led to it:

    1. Dual behaviour of matter.
    2. Heisenberg uncertainty principle.

    Dual Behaviour of Matter: de Broglie Relation#

    • The French physicist de Broglie (1924) proposed that matter, like radiation, shows dual behaviour, i.e. both particle and wave like properties.
    • Just as a photon has momentum as well as wavelength, an electron should also have momentum as well as wavelength.
    • He gave the following relation between wavelength (λ) and momentum (p) of a material particle:
    \[&lambda; = {h \over mv} = {h \over p}\]

    Where, m is the mass of the particle, v is its velocity and p is its momentum.

    • de Broglie's prediction was confirmed when an electron beam was found to undergo diffraction, a property of waves.
    • This is used in the electron microscope, which uses the wave like behaviour of electrons just as an ordinary microscope uses the wave nature of light. It can magnify about 15 million times.
    • Every moving object has a wave character. But the wavelengths of ordinary objects are so short (because of their large mass) that their wave properties cannot be detected. For electrons and other sub-atomic particles (very small mass), the wavelengths can be detected.

    Useful forms of de Broglie equation

    \[K.E. = {1 \over 2}mv^2 = {p^2 \over 2m}\]
    \[&lambda; = {h \over \sqrt{2m \times K.E.}}\]

    Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle#

    • Werner Heisenberg (1927) stated the uncertainty principle, which is a consequence of the dual behaviour of matter and radiation.
    • It is impossible to determine simultaneously the exact position and exact momentum (or velocity) of an electron.
    \[&Delta;x \times &Delta;p_x &ge; {h \over 4&pi;}\]
    \[&Delta;x \times &Delta;(mv_x) &ge; {h \over 4&pi;}\]
    \[&Delta;x \times &Delta;v_x &ge; {h \over 4&pi;m}\]

    Where, Δx is uncertainty in position and Δpx (or Δvx) is uncertainty in momentum (or velocity).

    • If the position is known very accurately (Δx is small), the velocity will be uncertain (Δvx is large), and vice versa.

    Example

    Suppose you try to measure the thickness of a sheet of paper with an unmarked metrestick. The result will be meaningless. You need an instrument graduated in units smaller than the thickness of the paper.

    Similarly, to locate an electron we must use "light" with a wavelength smaller than the size of the electron. But such light has high momentum (p = h/λ). Its photons collide with the electron and change its energy. We may find the position, but we would know very little about the velocity after the collision.

    Significance of Uncertainty Principle#

    • It rules out the existence of definite paths or trajectories of electrons and similar particles.
    • The trajectory of an object is fixed by its position and velocity at different moments. Since both cannot be known exactly at the same time for an electron, we cannot talk of its trajectory.
    • The effect is significant only for microscopic objects and negligible for macroscopic objects.

    Macroscopic vs microscopic objects

    For an object of mass 1 milligram (10-6 kg):

    \[&Delta;v.&Delta;x = {h \over 4&pi;m} = {6.626 \times 10^{-34} \over 4 \times 3.1416 \times 10^{-6}} &asymp; 10^{-28}\ m^2\ s^{-1}\]

    This is extremely small and of no real consequence.

    For an electron of mass 9.11 × 10-31 kg:

    \[&Delta;v.&Delta;x = {6.626 \times 10^{-34} \over 4 \times 3.1416 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31}} &asymp; 10^{-4}\ m^2\ s^{-1}\]

    If we try to locate the electron within 10-8 m, then:

    \[&Delta;v = {10^{-4}\ m^2\ s^{-1} \over 10^{-8}\ m} &asymp; 10^4\ m\ s^{-1}\]

    This is so large that the classical picture of electrons moving in fixed Bohr orbits cannot hold.

    Conclusion

    The precise statements of position and momentum of electrons have to be replaced by statements of probability that the electron has a given position and momentum. This is the basis of the quantum mechanical model of the atom.

    Reasons for the Failure of Bohr Model#

    • In Bohr's model, the electron is a charged particle moving in a well defined circular orbit. The wave character of the electron is ignored.
    • An orbit is a clearly defined path, which can be defined only if both position and velocity of the electron are known exactly at the same time. This is not possible according to the uncertainty principle.
    • So the Bohr model ignores the dual behaviour of matter and also contradicts Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. There was no point extending it to other atoms.

    Questions#

    What will be the wavelength of a ball of mass 0.1 kg moving with a velocity of 10 m s-1?

    \[&lambda; = {h \over mv} = {6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ J\ s \over 0.1\ kg \times 10\ m\ s^{-1}}\]
    \[&lambda; = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ m\ \ (J = kg\ m^2\ s^{-2})\]

    This wavelength is far too small to be detected.

    The mass of an electron is 9.1 × 10-31 kg. If its K.E. is 3.0 × 10-25 J, calculate its wavelength.

    \[K.E. = {1 \over 2}mv^2\]
    \[v = \sqrt{2 \times K.E. \over m} = \sqrt{2 \times 3.0 \times 10^{-25} \over 9.1 \times 10^{-31}} = 812\ m\ s^{-1}\]
    \[&lambda; = {h \over mv} = {6.626 \times 10^{-34} \over 9.1 \times 10^{-31} \times 812}\]
    \[&lambda; = 8.967 \times 10^{-7}\ m = 896.7\ nm\]

    Calculate the mass of a photon with wavelength 3.6 Å.

    \[&lambda; = 3.6\ Å = 3.6 \times 10^{-10}\ m\]
    \[Velocity\ of\ photon = c = 3 \times 10^8\ m\ s^{-1}\]
    \[m = {h \over &lambda;c} = {6.626 \times 10^{-34} \over 3.6 \times 10^{-10} \times 3 \times 10^8}\]
    \[m = 6.135 \times 10^{-33}\ kg\]

    Calculate the wavelength of an electron moving with a velocity of 2.05 × 107 m s-1.

    \[&lambda; = {h \over mv} = {6.626 \times 10^{-34} \over 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 2.05 \times 10^7}\]
    \[&lambda; = 3.55 \times 10^{-11}\ m\]

    If the velocity of the electron in Bohr's first orbit is 2.19 × 106 m s-1, calculate the de Broglie wavelength associated with it.

    \[&lambda; = {h \over mv} = {6.626 \times 10^{-34} \over 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 2.19 \times 10^6}\]
    \[&lambda; = 3.32 \times 10^{-10}\ m = 332\ pm\]

    A microscope using suitable photons is employed to locate an electron in an atom within a distance of 0.1 Å. What is the uncertainty involved in the measurement of its velocity?

    \[&Delta;x &Delta;p = {h \over 4&pi;}\ \ or\ \ &Delta;x\ m&Delta;v = {h \over 4&pi;}\]
    \[&Delta;v = {h \over 4&pi;&Delta;x\ m}\]
    \[&Delta;v = {6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ J\ s \over 4 \times 3.14 \times 0.1 \times 10^{-10}\ m \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ kg}\]
    \[&Delta;v = 0.579 \times 10^7\ m\ s^{-1} = 5.79 \times 10^6\ m\ s^{-1}\]

    A golf ball has a mass of 40 g and a speed of 45 m/s. If the speed can be measured within an accuracy of 2%, calculate the uncertainty in the position.

    \[&Delta;v = 45 \times {2 \over 100} = 0.9\ m\ s^{-1}\]
    \[&Delta;x = {h \over 4&pi;m&Delta;v} = {6.626 \times 10^{-34} \over 4 \times 3.14 \times 40 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.9}\]
    \[&Delta;x = 1.46 \times 10^{-33}\ m\]

    This is about 1018 times smaller than the diameter of a typical atomic nucleus. So for large particles, the uncertainty principle sets no meaningful limit on the precision of measurement.

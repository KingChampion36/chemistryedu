    Surface Chemistry | Emulsions and Colloids Around Us#

    Emulsions#

    Emulsions are liquid-liquid colloidal systems, i.e., the dispersion of finely divided droplets of one liquid in another liquid.

    • When a mixture of two immiscible or partially miscible liquids is shaken, a coarse dispersion of one liquid in the other is obtained. This is called an emulsion.
    • Generally, one of the two liquids is water.

    Types of Emulsions#

    1. Oil in water (O/W type)#

    • Oil is the dispersed phase and water is the dispersion medium.
    • Examples: milk (liquid fat dispersed in water) and vanishing cream.

    2. Water in oil (W/O type)#

    • Water is the dispersed phase and oil is the dispersion medium.
    • Examples: butter and cream.
    Property Oil in water (O/W) Water in oil (W/O)
    Dispersed phase Oil Water
    Dispersion medium Water Oil
    Examples Milk, vanishing cream Butter, cream
    Emulsifying agents Proteins, gums, natural and synthetic soaps Heavy metal salts of fatty acids, long chain alcohols, lampblack

    Emulsifying Agents#

    • Emulsions of oil in water are unstable and sometimes separate into two layers on standing.
    • To stabilise an emulsion, a third component called an emulsifying agent (emulsifier) is added.
    • The emulsifying agent forms an interfacial film between the suspended droplets and the medium.
    • Principal emulsifying agents for O/W emulsions are proteins, gums, natural and synthetic soaps, etc. For W/O emulsions, they are heavy metal salts of fatty acids, long chain alcohols, lampblack, etc.
    • The process of making an emulsion is called emulsification.

    Properties of Emulsions#

    • Emulsions can be diluted with any amount of the dispersion medium. But if the dispersed liquid is added, it forms a separate layer.
    • The droplets in emulsions are often negatively charged and can be precipitated by electrolytes.
    • Emulsions show Brownian movement and Tyndall effect.
    • Emulsions can be broken into their constituent liquids by heating, freezing, centrifuging, etc. This process is called demulsification.

    Colloids Around Us#

    Most substances we come across in daily life are colloids: the food we eat, the clothes we wear, wooden furniture, houses and newspapers are largely made of colloids.

    1. Blue colour of the sky#

    Dust particles along with water suspended in air scatter blue light, which reaches our eyes, so the sky looks blue.

    2. Fog, mist and rain#

    • When a large mass of air containing dust particles is cooled below its dew point, moisture condenses on the surfaces of these particles, forming fine droplets. These colloidal droplets float in air as mist or fog.
    • Clouds are aerosols of small water droplets suspended in air. In the upper atmosphere, the droplets grow bigger by condensation until they fall as rain. Sometimes rain occurs when two oppositely charged clouds meet.
    • Artificial rain can be caused by throwing electrified sand or spraying a sol carrying charge opposite to that on the clouds from an aeroplane.

    3. Food articles#

    Milk, butter, halwa, ice creams, fruit juices, etc. are all colloids in one form or the other.

    4. Blood#

    Blood is a colloidal solution of an albuminoid substance. The styptic action of alum and ferric chloride solution is due to coagulation of blood, forming a clot which stops further bleeding.

    5. Soils#

    Fertile soils are colloidal in nature, with humus acting as a protective colloid. Due to their colloidal nature, soils adsorb moisture and nourishing materials.

    6. Formation of delta#

    River water is a colloidal solution of clay. Sea water contains many electrolytes. When river water meets sea water, these electrolytes coagulate the clay, which gets deposited to form a delta.

    Applications of Colloids#

    1. Electrical precipitation of smoke#

    • Smoke is a colloidal solution of solid particles such as carbon, arsenic compounds, dust, etc. in air.
    • Before it leaves the chimney, smoke is passed through a chamber containing plates with a charge opposite to that of the smoke particles. A high voltage electrode (30000 volts or more) is used for this.
    • The particles lose their charge on contact, get precipitated and settle on the floor of the chamber. Gases free from carbon particles come out.
    • This precipitator is called the Cottrell precipitator.

    2. Purification of drinking water#

    Water from natural sources often contains suspended impurities. Alum is added to coagulate these impurities and make the water fit for drinking.

    3. Medicines#

    • Most medicines are colloidal in nature. Colloidal medicines are more effective because they have a large surface area and are easily assimilated.
    • Examples: argyrol (a silver sol) is used as an eye lotion; colloidal antimony is used in curing kalaazar; colloidal gold is used for intramuscular injection; milk of magnesia, an emulsion, is used for stomach disorders.

    4. Tanning#

    Animal hides are colloidal, with positively charged particles. When a hide is soaked in tannin, which has negatively charged colloidal particles, mutual coagulation takes place and the leather hardens. This is called tanning. Chromium salts are also used in place of tannin.

    5. Cleansing action of soaps and detergents#

    Soaps and detergents clean by forming micelles around oil and grease droplets and emulsifying them in water.

    6. Photographic plates and films#

    These are prepared by coating an emulsion of light sensitive silver bromide in gelatin over glass plates or celluloid films.

    7. Rubber industry#

    Latex is a colloidal solution of negatively charged rubber particles. Rubber is obtained by coagulation of latex.

    8. Industrial products#

    Paints, inks, synthetic plastics, rubber, graphite lubricants, cement, etc. are all colloidal solutions.

    Questions#

    What are emulsions? What are their different types? Give example of each type.

    Emulsions are liquid-liquid colloidal systems in which fine droplets of one liquid are dispersed in another liquid. They are of two types:

    • Oil in water (O/W): Oil is dispersed in water. Example: milk, vanishing cream.
    • Water in oil (W/O): Water is dispersed in oil. Example: butter, cream.

    What is demulsification? Name two demulsifiers.

    The process of breaking an emulsion into its constituent liquids is called demulsification. It can be done by heating, freezing, centrifuging, etc. Two common demulsifiers are:

    • Heating (or freezing)
    • Addition of an electrolyte, which neutralises the charge on the droplets

    Give four uses of emulsions.

    • Cleansing action of soaps and detergents is based on emulsification of oil and grease.
    • Milk, butter and cream are emulsions used as food.
    • Milk of magnesia, an emulsion, is used as a medicine for stomach disorders.
    • Photographic films are coated with an emulsion of silver bromide in gelatin.

    How is a delta formed at the mouth of a river?

    River water is a colloidal solution of negatively charged clay particles. Sea water contains many electrolytes like NaCl and MgCl2. When river water meets sea water, the ions from these electrolytes neutralise the charge on the clay particles, causing coagulation. The coagulated clay settles down, and over time this deposit forms a delta.

    Why does alum or ferric chloride stop bleeding from a cut?

    Blood is a colloidal solution of negatively charged albuminoid particles. Alum and FeCl3 provide Al3+ and Fe3+ ions, which have high coagulating power (Hardy-Schulze rule). They coagulate the blood and form a clot, which stops further bleeding.

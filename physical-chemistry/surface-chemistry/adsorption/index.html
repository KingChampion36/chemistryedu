    Surface Chemistry | Adsorption#

    Surface chemistry deals with phenomena that occur at the surfaces or interfaces. The accumulation of molecular species at the surface rather than in the bulk of a solid or liquid is termed adsorption.

    Surface Chemistry#

    • An interface (or surface) is the boundary that separates two bulk phases. It is written with a hyphen or a slash between the phases, e.g., solid-gas or solid/gas.
    • Gases mix completely with each other, so there is no interface between two gases.
    • An interface is usually only a few molecules thick, but its area depends on the size of the particles of the bulk phases.
    • Many important processes take place at interfaces: corrosion, electrode processes, heterogeneous catalysis, dissolution and crystallisation.
    • To study surfaces properly, a really clean surface is needed. Under very high vacuum (10-8 to 10-9 pascal), ultra clean metal surfaces can be obtained. Such surfaces must be stored in vacuum, otherwise they get covered by O2 and N2 from air.
    • The three main topics of surface chemistry are adsorption, catalysis and colloids.

    Adsorption#

    • The surface of a solid has a tendency to attract and retain molecules of the phase with which it comes into contact. These molecules stay only at the surface and do not go deeper into the bulk.
    • The substance which concentrates or accumulates at the surface is called the adsorbate.
    • The material on whose surface adsorption takes place is called the adsorbent.
    • Solids in finely divided state have a large surface area. Hence, charcoal, silica gel, alumina gel, clay, colloids and finely divided metals are good adsorbents.
    • The process of removing an adsorbed substance from the surface is called desorption.

    Adsorption in action

    • When a gas like O2, H2, CO, Cl2, NH3 or SO2 is kept in a closed vessel with powdered charcoal, the pressure of the gas decreases because gas molecules get adsorbed on the charcoal surface.

    • When animal charcoal is shaken with a solution of an organic dye like methylene blue, the filtrate turns colourless. The dye molecules are adsorbed on the charcoal.

    • An aqueous solution of raw sugar becomes colourless when passed over beds of animal charcoal, as the colouring substances are adsorbed.

    • Air becomes dry in the presence of silica gel because water molecules get adsorbed on the surface of the gel.

    Adsorption vs Absorption#

    • In adsorption, the substance is concentrated only at the surface and does not penetrate into the bulk of the adsorbent.
    • In absorption, the substance is uniformly distributed throughout the bulk of the solid.

    Examples

    • Chalk dipped in ink: The surface retains the colour of the ink due to adsorption of coloured molecules, while the solvent goes deeper into the stick due to absorption. On breaking the chalk, it is white from inside.

    • Water vapour: Water vapours are absorbed by anhydrous calcium chloride but adsorbed by silica gel.

    • Both adsorption and absorption can take place simultaneously. The term sorption is used to describe both processes together.

    Mechanism of Adsorption#

    • Inside the adsorbent, every particle is surrounded by similar particles on all sides, so all the forces acting on it are balanced.
    • Particles at the surface are not surrounded on all sides. They have unbalanced or residual attractive forces.
    • These residual forces attract the adsorbate particles onto the surface.
    • The extent of adsorption increases with the increase of surface area per unit mass of the adsorbent at a given temperature and pressure.

    Thermodynamics of adsorption#

    • During adsorption, the residual forces of the surface decrease, i.e., the surface energy decreases. This energy appears as heat. Hence, adsorption is always exothermic, i.e., ΔH is always negative.
    • When a gas is adsorbed, the freedom of movement of its molecules becomes restricted. So the entropy decreases, i.e., ΔS is negative.
    • For a spontaneous process at constant temperature and pressure, ΔG must be negative:
    \[&Delta;G = &Delta;H - T&Delta;S\]
    \[&Delta;H < 0,\ &Delta;S < 0\ (so,\ -T&Delta;S > 0)\]
    • ΔG becomes negative only when ΔH is sufficiently negative to outweigh the positive -TΔS term.
    • As adsorption proceeds, ΔH becomes less and less negative. Finally ΔH = TΔS and ΔG = 0. At this stage, adsorption equilibrium is attained.

    Types of Adsorption#

    1. Physical adsorption (Physisorption)#

    When a gas accumulates on the surface of a solid due to weak van der Waals' forces, the adsorption is called physical adsorption or physisorption.

    2. Chemical adsorption (Chemisorption)#

    When gas molecules or atoms are held to the solid surface by chemical bonds (covalent or ionic), the adsorption is called chemical adsorption or chemisorption. It needs high activation energy, so it is also called activated adsorption.

    • Sometimes both processes occur together. Physisorption at low temperature may pass into chemisorption as temperature increases.
    • Example: H2 is first adsorbed on nickel by van der Waals' forces. The H2 molecules then dissociate into hydrogen atoms, which are held on the surface by chemisorption.

    Characteristics of Physisorption#

    • Lack of specificity: van der Waals' forces are universal, so a surface does not prefer any particular gas.
    • Nature of adsorbate: Easily liquefiable gases (higher critical temperature) are adsorbed more, because van der Waals' forces are stronger near the critical temperature.

    Example

    1 g of activated charcoal adsorbs more SO2 (critical temperature 630 K) than CH4 (critical temperature 190 K), which in turn is adsorbed more than H2 (critical temperature 33 K, about 4.5 mL).

    • Reversible nature: Physisorption is generally reversible:
    \[Solid + Gas &#8652; Gas/Solid + Heat\]
    • By Le Chatelier's principle, increasing pressure (which decreases the volume of gas) increases adsorption, and decreasing pressure removes the gas.
    • Since the process is exothermic, physisorption occurs readily at low temperature and decreases with increasing temperature.
    • Surface area: Adsorption increases with surface area. Finely divided metals and porous substances are good adsorbents.
    • Enthalpy of adsorption: It is low, 20–40 kJ mol-1, because only weak van der Waals' forces are involved.

    Characteristics of Chemisorption#

    • High specificity: It occurs only if chemical bonding is possible between adsorbent and adsorbate. Example: O2 is adsorbed on metals due to oxide formation, and H2 is adsorbed by transition metals due to hydride formation.
    • Irreversibility: As a compound is formed, chemisorption is usually irreversible.
    • Effect of temperature: It is exothermic, but very slow at low temperature due to high activation energy. Like most chemical changes, it often increases with rise in temperature. High pressure is also usually favourable.
    • Surface area: Like physisorption, it increases with increase of surface area.
    • Enthalpy of adsorption: It is high, 80–240 kJ mol-1, as chemical bonds are formed.

    Comparison of Physisorption and Chemisorption#

    Physisorption Chemisorption
    Arises due to van der Waals' forces Caused by chemical bond formation
    Not specific in nature Highly specific in nature
    Reversible in nature Irreversible
    Depends on nature of gas; easily liquefiable gases adsorbed readily Depends on nature of gas; gases which can react with adsorbent show it
    Enthalpy of adsorption is low (20–40 kJ mol-1) Enthalpy of adsorption is high (80–240 kJ mol-1)
    Favoured by low temperature; decreases with increase of temperature Favoured by high temperature; increases with increase of temperature
    No appreciable activation energy needed High activation energy sometimes needed
    Increases with increase of surface area Also increases with increase of surface area
    Forms multimolecular layers under high pressure Forms a unimolecular layer

    Questions#

    Distinguish between the meaning of the terms adsorption and absorption. Give one example of each.

    In adsorption, the substance concentrates only at the surface of the adsorbent and does not penetrate into the bulk. In absorption, the substance is uniformly distributed throughout the bulk of the solid.

    • Adsorption: Water vapour is adsorbed by silica gel.
    • Absorption: Water vapour is absorbed by anhydrous calcium chloride.

    Why does physisorption decrease with the increase of temperature?

    Physisorption is exothermic and reversible:

    \[Solid + Gas &#8652; Gas/Solid + Heat\]

    According to Le Chatelier's principle, increasing the temperature shifts the equilibrium backward, so gas is desorbed. Also, at higher temperature the molecules have more kinetic energy and the weak van der Waals' forces cannot hold them on the surface.

    Why are powdered substances more effective adsorbents than their crystalline forms?

    Adsorption is a surface phenomenon, and its extent increases with surface area. A powdered (finely divided) substance has a much larger surface area per unit mass than its crystalline form, so it provides more surface for the adsorbate and is a more effective adsorbent.

    Why is adsorption always exothermic?

    The surface particles of an adsorbent have unbalanced residual forces. When adsorbate particles get attached, these residual forces decrease, i.e., surface energy decreases, and the energy is released as heat. So ΔH is negative.

    Also, adsorption decreases the entropy of the adsorbate (ΔS < 0). For adsorption to be spontaneous, ΔG = ΔH - TΔS must be negative, which is possible only when ΔH is negative. Hence, adsorption is always exothermic.

    What do you understand by activation of adsorbent? How is it achieved?

    Activation of an adsorbent means increasing its adsorbing power by increasing its effective surface area. It is achieved by:

    • Subdividing the adsorbent into a fine powder.
    • Making the surface rough or porous.
    • Heating the adsorbent (in vacuum or in steam) to remove gases and impurities already adsorbed on it, so that the free surface becomes available. For example, charcoal is activated by heating it in steam.

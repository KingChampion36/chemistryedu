    Redox Reactions | Types of Redox Reactions#

    Redox reactions can be classified into four types: combination, decomposition, displacement and disproportionation reactions. In the equations below, oxidation numbers are written above the atoms.

    1. Combination Reactions#

    • A combination reaction can be written as:
    \[A + B &rarr; C\]
    • For it to be a redox reaction, either A or B or both must be in the elemental form.
    • All combustion reactions that use elemental dioxygen, and many reactions with other elements, are combination redox reactions.
    \[\overset{0}{C}_{(s)} + \overset{0}{O_2}_{(g)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} \overset{+4}{C}\overset{-2}{O_2}_{(g)}\]
    \[3\overset{0}{Mg}_{(s)} + \overset{0}{N_2}_{(g)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} \overset{+2}{Mg_3}\overset{-3}{N_2}_{(s)}\]
    \[\overset{-4}{C}\overset{+1}{H_4}_{(g)} + 2\overset{0}{O_2}_{(g)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} \overset{+4}{C}\overset{-2}{O_2}_{(g)} + 2\overset{+1}{H_2}\overset{-2}{O}_{(l)}\]
    • Note that the oxidation number of hydrogen does not change in the combustion of methane; only carbon is oxidised.

    2. Decomposition Reactions#

    • Decomposition is the opposite of combination: a compound breaks down into two or more components, at least one of which must be in the elemental state.
    \[2\overset{+1}{H_2}\overset{-2}{O}_{(l)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} 2\overset{0}{H_2}_{(g)} + \overset{0}{O_2}_{(g)}\]
    \[2\overset{+1}{Na}\overset{-1}{H}_{(s)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} 2\overset{0}{Na}_{(s)} + \overset{0}{H_2}_{(g)}\]
    \[2\overset{+1}{K}\overset{+5}{Cl}\overset{-2}{O_3}_{(s)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} 2\overset{+1}{K}\overset{-1}{Cl}_{(s)} + 3\overset{0}{O_2}_{(g)}\]
    • In the decomposition of KClO3, the oxidation number of potassium does not change.
    • Not all decomposition reactions are redox. In the decomposition of calcium carbonate, no element changes its oxidation number:
    \[\overset{+2}{Ca}\overset{+4}{C}\overset{-2}{O_3}_{(s)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} \overset{+2}{Ca}\overset{-2}{O}_{(s)} + \overset{+4}{C}\overset{-2}{O_2}_{(g)}\]

    3. Displacement Reactions#

    • In a displacement reaction, an ion (or atom) in a compound is replaced by an ion (or atom) of another element:
    \[X + YZ &rarr; XZ + Y\]
    • Displacement reactions are of two types: metal displacement and non-metal displacement.

    (a) Metal Displacement#

    • A metal in a compound is displaced by another metal in the uncombined state. These reactions are widely used in metallurgy to obtain pure metals from their ores.
    \[\overset{+2}{Cu}SO_{4(aq)} + \overset{0}{Zn}_{(s)} &rarr; \overset{0}{Cu}_{(s)} + \overset{+2}{Zn}SO_{4(aq)}\]
    \[\overset{+5}{V_2}O_{5(s)} + 5\overset{0}{Ca}_{(s)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} 2\overset{0}{V}_{(s)} + 5\overset{+2}{Ca}O_{(s)}\]
    \[\overset{+4}{Ti}Cl_{4(l)} + 2\overset{0}{Mg}_{(s)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} \overset{0}{Ti}_{(s)} + 2\overset{+2}{Mg}Cl_{2(s)}\]
    \[\overset{+3}{Cr_2}O_{3(s)} + 2\overset{0}{Al}_{(s)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} \overset{+3}{Al_2}O_{3(s)} + 2\overset{0}{Cr}_{(s)}\]
    • In each case, the displacing metal is a better reducing agent than the metal being displaced, i.e. it has a greater tendency to lose electrons.

    (b) Non-metal Displacement#

    • These mainly include hydrogen displacement and, rarely, oxygen displacement.

    Hydrogen displacement from water#

    • All alkali metals and some alkaline earth metals (Ca, Sr, Ba) are very good reductants and displace hydrogen from cold water:
    \[2\overset{0}{Na}_{(s)} + 2\overset{+1}{H_2}O_{(l)} &rarr; 2\overset{+1}{Na}OH_{(aq)} + \overset{0}{H_2}_{(g)}\]
    \[\overset{0}{Ca}_{(s)} + 2\overset{+1}{H_2}O_{(l)} &rarr; \overset{+2}{Ca}(OH)_{2(aq)} + \overset{0}{H_2}_{(g)}\]
    • Less active metals like magnesium and iron react with steam to give dihydrogen:
    \[\overset{0}{Mg}_{(s)} + 2\overset{+1}{H_2}O_{(l)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} \overset{+2}{Mg}(OH)_{2(s)} + \overset{0}{H_2}_{(g)}\]
    \[2\overset{0}{Fe}_{(s)} + 3\overset{+1}{H_2}O_{(l)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} \overset{+3}{Fe_2}O_{3(s)} + 3\overset{0}{H_2}_{(g)}\]

    Hydrogen displacement from acids#

    • Many metals which do not react with cold water (and even some like cadmium and tin which do not react with steam) displace hydrogen from acids:
    \[\overset{0}{Zn}_{(s)} + 2\overset{+1}{H}Cl_{(aq)} &rarr; \overset{+2}{Zn}Cl_{2(aq)} + \overset{0}{H_2}_{(g)}\]
    \[\overset{0}{Mg}_{(s)} + 2\overset{+1}{H}Cl_{(aq)} &rarr; \overset{+2}{Mg}Cl_{2(aq)} + \overset{0}{H_2}_{(g)}\]
    \[\overset{0}{Fe}_{(s)} + 2\overset{+1}{H}Cl_{(aq)} &rarr; \overset{+2}{Fe}Cl_{2(aq)} + \overset{0}{H_2}_{(g)}\]
    • These reactions are used to prepare dihydrogen in the laboratory. The rate of hydrogen evolution reflects reactivity: slowest for Fe and fastest for Mg.
    • Very less active metals like silver and gold, which may occur in the native state, do not react even with hydrochloric acid.

    Halogen displacement#

    • The oxidising power of halogens decreases from fluorine to iodine. Fluorine is so reactive that it even displaces oxygen from water:
    \[2\overset{+1}{H_2}\overset{-2}{O}_{(l)} + 2\overset{0}{F_2}_{(g)} &rarr; 4\overset{+1}{H}\overset{-1}{F}_{(aq)} + \overset{0}{O_2}_{(g)}\]
    • For this reason, displacement reactions of Cl, Br and I using fluorine are generally not carried out in aqueous solution.
    • Chlorine displaces bromide and iodide ions, and bromine displaces iodide ions:
    \[\overset{0}{Cl_2}_{(g)} + 2\overset{-1}{Br^-}_{(aq)} &rarr; 2\overset{-1}{Cl^-}_{(aq)} + \overset{0}{Br_2}_{(l)}\]
    \[\overset{0}{Cl_2}_{(g)} + 2\overset{-1}{I^-}_{(aq)} &rarr; 2\overset{-1}{Cl^-}_{(aq)} + \overset{0}{I_2}_{(s)}\]
    \[\overset{0}{Br_2}_{(l)} + 2\overset{-1}{I^-}_{(aq)} &rarr; 2\overset{-1}{Br^-}_{(aq)} + \overset{0}{I_2}_{(s)}\]
    • Br2 and I2 are coloured and dissolve in CCl4, so they can be identified from the colour of the solution. This is the basis of the Layer Test for Br- and I- in the laboratory.
    • Recovery of halogens from halides requires oxidation:
    \[2X^- &rarr; X_2 + 2e^-\]
    • Cl-, Br- and I- can be oxidised by chemical means. But fluorine is the strongest oxidising agent, so there is no chemical way to convert F- to F2. The only way is to oxidise F- electrolytically.

    4. Disproportionation Reactions#

    • In a disproportionation reaction, an element in one oxidation state is simultaneously oxidised and reduced.
    • The reacting substance always contains an element that can exist in at least three oxidation states. It is present in an intermediate state, and both higher and lower states are formed.
    • Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide is a familiar example, where oxygen goes from -1 to 0 (in O2) and to -2 (in H2O):
    \[2\overset{+1}{H_2}\overset{-1}{O_2}_{(aq)} &rarr; 2\overset{+1}{H_2}\overset{-2}{O}_{(l)} + \overset{0}{O_2}_{(g)}\]
    • Phosphorus, sulphur and chlorine disproportionate in alkaline medium:
    \[\overset{0}{P_4}_{(s)} + 3OH^-_{(aq)} + 3H_2O_{(l)} &rarr; \overset{-3}{P}H_{3(g)} + 3H_2\overset{+1}{P}O^-_{2(aq)}\]
    \[\overset{0}{S_8}_{(s)} + 12OH^-_{(aq)} &rarr; 4\overset{-2}{S^{2-}}_{(aq)} + 2\overset{+2}{S_2}O^{2-}_{3(aq)} + 6H_2O_{(l)}\]
    \[\overset{0}{Cl_2}_{(g)} + 2OH^-_{(aq)} &rarr; \overset{+1}{Cl}O^-_{(aq)} + \overset{-1}{Cl^-}_{(aq)} + H_2O_{(l)}\]
    • The last reaction forms household bleaching agents. The hypochlorite ion (ClO-) oxidises the colour-bearing stains to colourless compounds.
    • Bromine and iodine behave like chlorine. But fluorine reacts with alkali differently:
    \[2F_{2(g)} + 2OH^-_{(aq)} &rarr; 2F^-_{(aq)} + OF_{2(g)} + H_2O_{(l)}\]
    • Being the most electronegative element, fluorine cannot show a positive oxidation state, so it does not disproportionate. (Fluorine will also attack water here to produce some oxygen.)

    Questions#

    Which of the following species do not show disproportionation reaction and why? ClO-, ClO2-, ClO3- and ClO4-. Also write the reaction for each species that disproportionates.

    • ClO4- does not disproportionate because chlorine in it is already in its highest oxidation state, +7. It can only be reduced, not oxidised.

    • The other three disproportionate as follows:

    \[3\overset{+1}{Cl}O^- &rarr; 2\overset{-1}{Cl^-} + \overset{+5}{Cl}O_3^-\]
    \[6\overset{+3}{Cl}O_2^- \xrightarrow{h\nu} 4\overset{+5}{Cl}O_3^- + 2\overset{-1}{Cl^-}\]
    \[4\overset{+5}{Cl}O_3^- &rarr; \overset{-1}{Cl^-} + 3\overset{+7}{Cl}O_4^-\]

    Suggest a scheme of classification of the following redox reactions:
    (a) N2(g) + O2(g) → 2NO(g)
    (b) 2Pb(NO3)2(s) → 2PbO(s) + 4NO2(g) + O2(g)
    (c) NaH(s) + H2O(l) → NaOH(aq) + H2(g)
    (d) 2NO2(g) + 2OH-(aq) → NO2-(aq) + NO3-(aq) + H2O(l)

    • (a) Nitric oxide is formed by combining the elements nitrogen and oxygen: combination redox reaction.

    • (b) Lead nitrate breaks down into three components (one of them elemental O2): decomposition redox reaction.

    • (c) Hydrogen of water is displaced by the hydride ion as dihydrogen gas: displacement redox reaction.

    • (d) Nitrogen in NO2 (+4) changes to NO2- (+3) and NO3- (+5): disproportionation redox reaction.

    Why do the following reactions proceed differently?
    Pb3O4 + 8HCl → 3PbCl2 + Cl2 + 4H2O
    Pb3O4 + 4HNO3 → 2Pb(NO3)2 + PbO2 + 2H2O

    • Pb3O4 is actually a stoichiometric mixture of 2 mol PbO and 1 mol PbO2.

    • In PbO2, lead is in the +4 state, whereas its stable state is +2 (as in PbO). So PbO2 acts as an oxidant and can oxidise Cl- of HCl to Cl2. PbO is a basic oxide and simply undergoes an acid-base reaction.

    • So, the reaction with HCl splits into:

    \[2PbO + 4HCl &rarr; 2PbCl_2 + 2H_2O\ (acid-base\ reaction)\]
    \[\overset{+4}{Pb}O_2 + 4H\overset{-1}{Cl} &rarr; \overset{+2}{Pb}Cl_2 + \overset{0}{Cl_2} + 2H_2O\ (redox\ reaction)\]
    • HNO3 is itself an oxidising agent, so no redox reaction takes place between PbO2 and HNO3. Only the acid-base reaction occurs with PbO:
    \[2PbO + 4HNO_3 &rarr; 2Pb(NO_3)_2 + 2H_2O\]
    • It is the passive nature of PbO2 towards HNO3 that makes the reaction different from the one with HCl.

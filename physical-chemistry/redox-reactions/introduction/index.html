    Redox Reactions | Introduction#

    Redox reactions are reactions in which oxidation and reduction take place simultaneously. The word "redox" itself comes from reduction + oxidation.

    • Redox reactions are everywhere: burning of fuels, extraction of reactive metals by electrolysis, manufacture of chemicals like caustic soda, working of dry and wet batteries and corrosion of metals are all redox processes.
    • Biological processes like respiration and photosynthesis, and environmental topics like the hydrogen economy and the ozone hole, also involve redox chemistry.

    Classical Concept of Oxidation#

    • Originally, oxidation meant only the addition of oxygen to an element or a compound. Since air contains about 20% dioxygen, many elements combine with it, which is why most elements occur on earth as oxides.
    \[2Mg_{(s)} + O_{2(g)} &rarr; 2MgO_{(s)}\]
    \[S_{(s)} + O_{2(g)} &rarr; SO_{2(g)}\]
    \[CH_{4(g)} + 2O_{2(g)} &rarr; CO_{2(g)} + 2H_2O_{(l)}\]
    • In the combustion of methane, hydrogen of CH4 is replaced by oxygen. This led chemists to include the removal of hydrogen as oxidation as well:
    \[2H_2S_{(g)} + O_{2(g)} &rarr; 2S_{(s)} + 2H_2O_{(l)}\]
    • Later, the term was extended to the addition of other electronegative elements (like F, Cl, S) in place of oxygen:
    \[Mg_{(s)} + F_{2(g)} &rarr; MgF_{2(s)}\]
    \[Mg_{(s)} + Cl_{2(g)} &rarr; MgCl_{2(s)}\]
    \[Mg_{(s)} + S_{(s)} &rarr; MgS_{(s)}\]
    • Finally, the removal of an electropositive element was also counted as oxidation. For example, potassium ferrocyanide is oxidised to potassium ferricyanide because electropositive potassium is removed from it:
    \[2K_4[Fe(CN)_6]_{(aq)} + H_2O_{2(aq)} &rarr; 2K_3[Fe(CN)_6]_{(aq)} + 2KOH_{(aq)}\]

    Conclusion

    Oxidation is the addition of oxygen/electronegative element to a substance or removal of hydrogen/electropositive element from a substance.

    Classical Concept of Reduction#

    • Reduction was first defined as the removal of oxygen from a compound. Its scope was later broadened in the same way as oxidation.

    Conclusion

    Reduction is the removal of oxygen/electronegative element from a substance or addition of hydrogen/electropositive element to a substance.

    Examples of reduction

    \[2HgO_{(s)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} 2Hg_{(l)} + O_{2(g)}\ (removal\ of\ oxygen)\]
    \[2FeCl_{3(aq)} + H_{2(g)} &rarr; 2FeCl_{2(aq)} + 2HCl_{(aq)}\ (removal\ of\ chlorine)\]
    \[CH_2 = CH_{2(g)} + H_{2(g)} &rarr; H_3C - CH_{3(g)}\ (addition\ of\ hydrogen)\]
    \[2HgCl_{2(aq)} + SnCl_{2(aq)} &rarr; Hg_2Cl_{2(s)} + SnCl_{4(aq)}\ (addition\ of\ mercury)\]
    • In the last reaction, HgCl2 is reduced, but at the same time SnCl2 is oxidised to SnCl4 by the addition of chlorine.
    • Looking carefully at all such reactions, chemists realised that oxidation and reduction always occur together. Hence the name redox reactions.

    Electronic Concept of Redox Reactions#

    • Consider the reactions of sodium with chlorine, oxygen and sulphur:
    \[2Na_{(s)} + Cl_{2(g)} &rarr; 2NaCl_{(s)}\]
    \[4Na_{(s)} + O_{2(g)} &rarr; 2Na_2O_{(s)}\]
    \[2Na_{(s)} + S_{(s)} &rarr; Na_2S_{(s)}\]
    • Classically, sodium is oxidised (an electronegative element is added to it) and Cl2, O2 and S are reduced (electropositive sodium is added to them).
    • But the products are ionic: Na+Cl-, (Na+)2O2- and (Na+)2S2-. This shows that sodium has lost electrons and the non-metal has gained them.
    • Each reaction can therefore be split into two half reactions, one showing loss of electrons and the other showing gain of electrons. For NaCl:
    \[Oxidation\ half:\ 2Na_{(s)} &rarr; 2Na^+_{(g)} + 2e^-\]
    \[Reduction\ half:\ Cl_{2(g)} + 2e^- &rarr; 2Cl^-_{(g)}\]
    \[Overall:\ 2Na_{(s)} + Cl_{2(g)} &rarr; 2Na^+Cl^-_{(s)}\]
    • Sodium gives electrons to chlorine and reduces it, so sodium acts as a reducing agent. Chlorine accepts electrons from sodium and oxidises it, so chlorine acts as an oxidising agent.

    Electronic definitions

    • Oxidation: Loss of electron(s) by any species.

    • Reduction: Gain of electron(s) by any species.

    • Oxidising agent: Acceptor of electron(s).

    • Reducing agent: Donor of electron(s).

    • A simple way to remember: the oxidising agent is itself reduced, and the reducing agent is itself oxidised.

    Competitive Electron Transfer Reactions#

    1. Zinc in copper nitrate solution#

    • When a zinc strip is placed in aqueous copper nitrate solution for about an hour, it gets coated with reddish metallic copper and the blue colour of the solution (due to Cu2+) disappears.
    • The formation of Zn2+ can be confirmed by passing H2S through the colourless solution (made alkaline with ammonia), which gives white zinc sulphide, ZnS.
    \[Zn_{(s)} + Cu^{2+}_{(aq)} &rarr; Zn^{2+}_{(aq)} + Cu_{(s)}\]
    \[Oxidation:\ Zn &rarr; Zn^{2+} + 2e^-\]
    \[Reduction:\ Cu^{2+} + 2e^- &rarr; Cu\]
    • Zinc loses electrons and is oxidised; Cu2+ gains those electrons and is reduced.
    • The reverse reaction does not occur: a copper strip in zinc sulphate solution shows no change, and even the very sensitive H2S test (black CuS) cannot detect any Cu2+. So the equilibrium greatly favours the products.

    2. Copper in silver nitrate solution#

    • A copper rod dipped in silver nitrate solution slowly turns the solution blue (Cu2+ formed) and silver deposits on the rod.
    \[Cu_{(s)} + 2Ag^+_{(aq)} &rarr; Cu^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2Ag_{(s)}\]
    • Here Cu is oxidised to Cu2+ and Ag+ is reduced to Ag. The equilibrium again greatly favours the products.

    3. Cobalt in nickel sulphate solution#

    \[Co_{(s)} + Ni^{2+}_{(aq)} &#8652; Co^{2+}_{(aq)} + Ni_{(s)}\]
    • At equilibrium, both Ni2+ and Co2+ are present at moderate concentrations. Neither the reactants nor the products are greatly favoured.

    Activity Series#

    • These experiments show that metals compete with each other to release electrons, just as acids compete to release protons.
    • Zinc releases electrons to copper and copper releases electrons to silver. So the electron releasing tendency is:
    \[Zn > Cu > Ag\]
    • Arranging metals in order of their tendency to release electrons gives the metal activity series or electrochemical series.
    • This competition for electrons is the basis of galvanic cells, where a redox reaction becomes a source of electrical energy. See Electrochemical Cells.

    Questions#

    In the reactions given below, identify the species undergoing oxidation and reduction:
    (i) H2S(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g) + S(s)
    (ii) 3Fe3O4(s) + 8Al(s) → 9Fe(s) + 4Al2O3(s)
    (iii) 2Na(s) + H2(g) → 2NaH(s)

    • (i) H2S is oxidised because hydrogen (more electropositive) is removed from sulphur, or equivalently, electronegative chlorine is added to hydrogen. Chlorine is reduced because hydrogen is added to it.

    • (ii) Aluminium is oxidised because oxygen is added to it. Fe3O4 is reduced because oxygen is removed from it.

    • (iii) Using electronegativity, sodium (electropositive) is added to hydrogen, so hydrogen is reduced and sodium is oxidised. Such cases are better explained by electron transfer.

    Justify that the reaction 2Na(s) + H2(g) → 2NaH(s) is a redox change.

    • NaH is an ionic compound, Na+H-. So the reaction can be split into two half reactions:
    \[Oxidation\ half:\ 2Na_{(s)} &rarr; 2Na^+_{(g)} + 2e^-\]
    \[Reduction\ half:\ H_{2(g)} + 2e^- &rarr; 2H^-_{(g)}\]
    • Sodium loses electrons (oxidised) and hydrogen gains electrons (reduced). Hence, the reaction is a redox change.

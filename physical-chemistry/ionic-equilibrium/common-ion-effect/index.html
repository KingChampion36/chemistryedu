    Ionic Equilibrium | Common Ion Effect#

    Common Ion Effect#

    Common ion effect can be defined as the shift in equilibrium on adding a substance that provides more of an ionic species already present in the dissociation equilibrium. It is an application of Le Chatelier's principle.

    • Consider the dissociation of acetic acid:
    \[CH_3COOH_{(aq)} &#8652; H^+_{(aq)} + CH_3COO^-_{(aq)}\]
    \[or\ \ HAc_{(aq)} &#8652; H^+_{(aq)} + Ac^-_{(aq)}\]
    \[K_a = {[H^+][Ac^-] \over [HAc]}\]
    • If acetate ions are added (for example, as sodium acetate, CH3COONa), the concentration of Ac- increases. To keep Ka constant, the equilibrium shifts backward, and [H+] decreases.
    • Similarly, if H+ ions are added from an external source (such as HCl), the equilibrium moves towards undissociated acetic acid, reducing the ionization of the acid.

    Conclusion

    • Addition of a common ion suppresses the ionization of a weak electrolyte.

    • The degree of ionization of a weak acid decreases in presence of a strong acid or its own salt. The degree of ionization of a weak base decreases in presence of a strong base or its own salt.

    pH of 0.05 M acetic acid containing 0.05 M acetate ion#

    Example

    \[HAc_{(aq)} &#8652; H^+_{(aq)} + Ac^-_{(aq)}\]
    \[Initial\ (M):\ \ 0.05\ \ \ \ 0\ \ \ \ 0.05\]

    Let x be the extent of ionization of acetic acid.

    \[Change\ (M):\ \ -x\ \ \ \ +x\ \ \ \ +x\]
    \[Equilibrium\ (M):\ \ 0.05 - x\ \ \ \ x\ \ \ \ 0.05 + x\]
    \[K_a = {[H^+][Ac^-] \over [HAc]} = {(0.05 + x)(x) \over 0.05 - x}\]

    As Ka is small for a very weak acid, x << 0.05. Hence, (0.05 + x) ≈ (0.05 - x) ≈ 0.05.

    \[1.8 \times 10^{-5} = {x(0.05) \over 0.05}\]
    \[x = [H^+] = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}\ M\]
    \[pH = -log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) = 4.74\]
    • Without the added acetate, the same 0.05 M acetic acid has [H+] = √(Kac) ≈ 9.5 × 10-4 M and pH ≈ 3.0. The common ion has reduced [H+] about 50 times.

    General result#

    • For a weak acid HA (concentration c) in presence of a strong acid giving [H+] = C', or its salt giving [A-] = C':
    \[Weak\ acid + strong\ acid:\ \ &alpha; = {K_a \over C'}\]
    \[Weak\ acid + its\ salt:\ \ [H^+] = {K_a \times [HA] \over [A^-]}\]
    • Similarly, for a weak base in presence of a strong base giving [OH-] = C':
    \[&alpha; = {K_b \over C'}\]
    • A mixture of a weak acid and its salt (or a weak base and its salt) resists change in pH. Such mixtures are buffer solutions, discussed in a later page.

    Questions#

    Calculate the pH of a 0.10 M ammonia solution. Calculate the pH after 50.0 mL of this solution is treated with 25.0 mL of 0.10 M HCl. The dissociation constant of ammonia, Kb = 1.77 × 10-5.

    Before neutralization

    \[NH_3 + H_2O &#8652; NH_4^+ + OH^-\]
    \[K_b = {[NH_4^+][OH^-] \over [NH_3]} = 1.77 \times 10^{-5}\]

    Let [NH4+] = [OH-] = x, then [NH3] = 0.10 - x ≈ 0.10.

    \[{x^2 \over 0.10} = 1.77 \times 10^{-5}\]
    \[x = 1.33 \times 10^{-3}\ M = [OH^-]\]
    \[[H^+] = {K_w \over [OH^-]} = {10^{-14} \over 1.33 \times 10^{-3}} = 7.51 \times 10^{-12}\ M\]
    \[pH = -log(7.51 \times 10^{-12}) = 11.12\]

    After adding HCl

    25 mL of 0.1 M HCl = 2.5 mmol HCl. 50 mL of 0.1 M NH3 = 5 mmol NH3. So 2.5 mmol NH3 is neutralized.

    \[NH_3 + HCl &rarr; NH_4^+ + Cl^-\]
    \[Before:\ \ 5\ \ \ \ 2.5\ \ \ \ 0\ \ \ \ 0\ \ (mmol)\]
    \[After:\ \ 2.5\ \ \ \ 0\ \ \ \ 2.5\ \ \ \ 2.5\ \ (mmol)\]

    The resulting 75 mL contains 2.5 mmol NH4+ (0.033 M) and 2.5 mmol unneutralized NH3 (0.033 M). This NH3 ionizes in presence of the common ion NH4+:

    \[NH_4OH &#8652; NH_4^+ + OH^-\]
    \[Equilibrium:\ \ 0.033 - y\ \ \ \ 0.033 + y\ \ \ \ y\]

    As y is small, [NH4OH] ≈ 0.033 M and [NH4+] ≈ 0.033 M.

    \[K_b = {[NH_4^+][OH^-] \over [NH_4OH]} = {y(0.033) \over 0.033} = 1.77 \times 10^{-5}\]
    \[y = [OH^-] = 1.77 \times 10^{-5}\ M\]
    \[[H^+] = {10^{-14} \over 1.77 \times 10^{-5}} = 0.56 \times 10^{-9}\ M\]
    \[pH = 9.24\]

    The ionization constant of phenol is 1.0 × 10-10. What is the concentration of phenolate ion in 0.05 M solution of phenol? What will be its degree of ionization if the solution is also 0.01 M in sodium phenolate?

    \[C_6H_5OH &#8652; H^+ + C_6H_5O^-\]

    In 0.05 M phenol alone

    \[[C_6H_5O^-] = \sqrt{K_a\ c} = \sqrt{1.0 \times 10^{-10} \times 0.05} = 2.24 \times 10^{-6}\ M\]
    \[&alpha; = {2.24 \times 10^{-6} \over 0.05} = 4.47 \times 10^{-5}\]

    In presence of 0.01 M sodium phenolate

    Sodium phenolate is fully ionized, so [C6H5O-] ≈ 0.01 M (the phenol's own contribution is negligible).

    \[[H^+] = {K_a[C_6H_5OH] \over [C_6H_5O^-]} = {1.0 \times 10^{-10} \times 0.05 \over 0.01} = 5.0 \times 10^{-10}\ M\]
    \[&alpha; = {[H^+] \over c} = {5.0 \times 10^{-10} \over 0.05} = 1.0 \times 10^{-8}\]

    The degree of ionization drops from 4.47 × 10-5 to 1.0 × 10-8 because of the common ion.

    Calculate the degree of ionization of 0.05 M acetic acid if its pKa value is 4.74. How is the degree of dissociation affected when its solution also contains (a) 0.01 M (b) 0.1 M in HCl?

    \[K_a = 10^{-4.74} = 1.82 \times 10^{-5}\]

    Without HCl

    \[&alpha; = \sqrt{K_a \over c} = \sqrt{1.82 \times 10^{-5} \over 0.05} = 0.019\]

    With HCl, [H+] ≈ [HCl], and α = Ka/[H+]:

    \[(a)\ &alpha; = {1.82 \times 10^{-5} \over 0.01} = 1.82 \times 10^{-3}\]
    \[(b)\ &alpha; = {1.82 \times 10^{-5} \over 0.1} = 1.82 \times 10^{-4}\]

    The degree of dissociation decreases as more HCl (common ion H+) is added.

    The ionization constant of dimethylamine is 5.4 × 10-4. Calculate its degree of ionization in its 0.02 M solution. What percentage of dimethylamine is ionized if the solution is also 0.1 M in NaOH?

    \[(CH_3)_2NH + H_2O &#8652; (CH_3)_2NH_2^+ + OH^-\]

    In 0.02 M solution

    \[&alpha; = \sqrt{K_b \over c} = \sqrt{5.4 \times 10^{-4} \over 0.02} = 0.164\]

    Since α is more than 5%, solving the full quadratic, 0.02α2 + 5.4 × 10-4α - 5.4 × 10-4 = 0, gives the more exact value α = 0.151.

    In presence of 0.1 M NaOH

    [OH-] ≈ 0.1 M from NaOH, so:

    \[&alpha; = {K_b \over [OH^-]} = {5.4 \times 10^{-4} \over 0.1} = 5.4 \times 10^{-3}\]

    So only 0.54% of dimethylamine is ionized.

    The ionization constant of propanoic acid is 1.32 × 10-5. Calculate the degree of ionization of the acid in its 0.05 M solution and also its pH. What will be its degree of ionization if the solution is 0.01 M in HCl also?

    \[&alpha; = \sqrt{K_a \over c} = \sqrt{1.32 \times 10^{-5} \over 0.05} = 1.63 \times 10^{-2}\]
    \[[H^+] = c&alpha; = 0.05 \times 1.63 \times 10^{-2} = 8.12 \times 10^{-4}\ M\]
    \[pH = -log(8.12 \times 10^{-4}) = 3.09\]

    In presence of 0.01 M HCl:

    \[&alpha; = {K_a \over [H^+]} = {1.32 \times 10^{-5} \over 0.01} = 1.32 \times 10^{-3}\]

    Ionic Equilibrium | Polybasic Acids and Acid Strength#

    Di- and Polybasic Acids#

    • Acids like oxalic acid, sulphuric acid and phosphoric acid have more than one ionizable proton per molecule. Such acids are known as polybasic or polyprotic acids.
    • A polybasic acid ionizes in steps, losing one proton at a time. Each step has its own ionization constant.
    • For a dibasic acid H2X:
    \[H_2X_{(aq)} &#8652; H^+_{(aq)} + HX^-_{(aq)},\ \ K_{a_1} = {[H^+][HX^-] \over [H_2X]}\]
    \[HX^-_{(aq)} &#8652; H^+_{(aq)} + X^{2-}_{(aq)},\ \ K_{a_2} = {[H^+][X^{2-}] \over [HX^-]}\]
    • Ka1 and Ka2 are called the first and second ionization constants of H2X.
    • Similarly, a tribasic acid like H3PO4 has three ionization constants: Ka1, Ka2 and Ka3.

    Ionization of phosphoric acid

    \[H_3PO_4 &#8652; H^+ + H_2PO_4^-,\ \ K_{a_1} = 7.5 \times 10^{-3}\]
    \[H_2PO_4^- &#8652; H^+ + HPO_4^{2-},\ \ K_{a_2} = 6.2 \times 10^{-8}\]
    \[HPO_4^{2-} &#8652; H^+ + PO_4^{3-},\ \ K_{a_3} = 4.2 \times 10^{-13}\]
    • For a polybasic acid, the overall constant is the product of the step-wise constants: for H2X ⇌ 2H+ + X2-, K = Ka1 × Ka2.

    Ionization constants of some common polyprotic acids (298 K)#

    Acid Ka1 Ka2 Ka3
    Oxalic acid 5.9 × 10-2 6.4 × 10-5 -
    Ascorbic acid 7.4 × 10-4 1.6 × 10-12 -
    Sulphurous acid 1.7 × 10-2 6.4 × 10-8 -
    Sulphuric acid Very large 1.2 × 10-2 -
    Carbonic acid 4.3 × 10-7 5.6 × 10-11 -
    Citric acid 7.4 × 10-4 1.7 × 10-5 4.0 × 10-7
    Phosphoric acid 7.5 × 10-3 6.2 × 10-8 4.2 × 10-13

    Why is Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3?#

    • The higher order ionization constants are always smaller than the lower order ones.
    • This is because it is more difficult to remove a positively charged proton from a negative ion, due to electrostatic attraction.
    • Example: Removing a proton from uncharged H2CO3 is easier than removing one from negatively charged HCO3-. Similarly, removing a proton from doubly charged HPO42- is harder than from H2PO4-.

    Conclusion

    • A solution of a diprotic acid contains a mixture of H2A, HA- and A2-.

    • Since Ka1 >> Ka2, the H3O+ in the solution comes mainly from the first dissociation step. The primary reaction is the dissociation of H2A.

    • For a weak diprotic acid, the concentration of the dianion is approximately equal to Ka2: [A2-] ≈ Ka2 (when no other acid is present).

    Factors Affecting Acid Strength#

    • Broadly, the extent of dissociation of an acid H-A depends on the strength and polarity of the H-A bond.
    • When the H-A bond strength decreases (less energy is needed to break it), HA becomes a stronger acid.
    • When the H-A bond becomes more polar (greater electronegativity difference between H and A), there is more charge separation, the bond breaks more easily and acidity increases.

    1. Elements in the same group: bond strength decides#

    • Down a group, the size of A increases and the H-A bond strength decreases. So acid strength increases down the group.
    • Here bond strength is more important than polarity.
    \[Size\ of\ A\ increases\ &rarr;\]
    \[HF \ll HCl \ll HBr \ll HI\]
    \[Acid\ strength\ increases\ &rarr;\]
    • Similarly, H2S is a stronger acid than H2O.

    2. Elements in the same period: bond polarity decides#

    • Across a period, the electronegativity of A increases and the H-A bond becomes more polar. So acid strength increases across the period.
    \[Electronegativity\ of\ A\ increases\ &rarr;\]
    \[CH_4 < NH_3 < H_2O < HF\]
    \[Acid\ strength\ increases\ &rarr;\]

    Questions#

    The first ionization constant of H2S is 9.1 × 10-8. Calculate the concentration of HS- ion in its 0.1 M solution. How will this concentration be affected if the solution is 0.1 M in HCl also? If the second dissociation constant of H2S is 1.2 × 10-13, calculate the concentration of S2- under both conditions.

    (i) In 0.1 M H2S alone

    \[H_2S &#8652; H^+ + HS^-,\ \ K_{a_1} = 9.1 \times 10^{-8}\]

    Let [H+] = [HS-] = x. Since Ka1 is small, [H2S] ≈ 0.1 M.

    \[K_{a_1} = {x^2 \over 0.1} = 9.1 \times 10^{-8}\]
    \[x = [HS^-] = \sqrt{9.1 \times 10^{-9}} = 9.54 \times 10^{-5}\ M\]

    For the second step, [H+] ≈ [HS-], so they cancel:

    \[K_{a_2} = {[H^+][S^{2-}] \over [HS^-]},\ \ [S^{2-}] = K_{a_2} = 1.2 \times 10^{-13}\ M\]

    (ii) In presence of 0.1 M HCl

    HCl is a strong acid, so [H+] = 0.1 M. The common ion H+ suppresses the ionization of H2S.

    \[[HS^-] = {K_{a_1}[H_2S] \over [H^+]} = {9.1 \times 10^{-8} \times 0.1 \over 0.1} = 9.1 \times 10^{-8}\ M\]

    For S2-, use the overall equilibrium H2S ⇌ 2H+ + S2-:

    \[K_{a_1} \times K_{a_2} = {[H^+]^2[S^{2-}] \over [H_2S]}\]
    \[[S^{2-}] = {9.1 \times 10^{-8} \times 1.2 \times 10^{-13} \times 0.1 \over (0.1)^2} = 1.09 \times 10^{-19}\ M\]

    So in 0.1 M HCl, [HS-] falls from 9.54 × 10-5 M to 9.1 × 10-8 M, and [S2-] falls from 1.2 × 10-13 M to 1.09 × 10-19 M.

    Arrange HF, HCl, HBr and HI in increasing order of acid strength and explain.

    \[HF < HCl < HBr < HI\]

    These elements belong to the same group. Down the group the size of the halogen increases, so the H-X bond becomes longer and weaker. A weaker bond releases H+ more easily, so acid strength increases from HF to HI, even though bond polarity decreases.

    Why is HF a stronger acid than H2O, while H2S is a stronger acid than H2O?

    • F and O are in the same period. F is more electronegative than O, so the H-F bond is more polar than the H-O bond. Hence HF is a stronger acid than H2O.

    • O and S are in the same group. S is larger than O, so the H-S bond is weaker than the H-O bond. Hence H2S is a stronger acid than H2O.

    Why is the second ionization constant of carbonic acid (5.6 × 10-11) much smaller than its first ionization constant (4.3 × 10-7)?

    In the first step, a proton is removed from the neutral H2CO3 molecule. In the second step, a proton must be removed from the negatively charged HCO3- ion. The negative charge holds the positive proton by electrostatic attraction, so the second ionization is much more difficult and Ka2 << Ka1.

    Amines | Chemical Reactions of Amines#

    The reactions of amines are governed by the lone pair of electrons on nitrogen and by the number of hydrogen atoms attached to it. That is why primary (-NH2), secondary (>N-H) and tertiary (>N-) amines behave differently in many reactions. The basic character of amines is discussed on the previous page; here we look at their other reactions.

    1. Alkylation#

    • Amines are nucleophiles, so they react with alkyl halides. Each step replaces one H on nitrogen by an alkyl group, finally giving a quaternary ammonium salt.
    \[RNH_2 \xrightarrow{R'X} RNHR' \xrightarrow{R'X} RNR'_2 \xrightarrow{R'X} R\overset{+}{N}R'_3\overset{-}{X}\]

    Example

    With excess methyl iodide (in Na2CO3 solution, which neutralises the HI formed), aniline is fully methylated:

    \[C_6H_5NH_2 + 3CH_3I \xrightarrow{Na_2CO_3} C_6H_5\overset{+}{N}(CH_3)_3\overset{-}{I}\]

    The product is N,N,N-trimethylanilinium iodide, a quaternary ammonium salt.

    2. Acylation#

    • Primary and secondary amines (aliphatic and aromatic) react with acid chlorides, anhydrides and esters by nucleophilic substitution. The H on nitrogen is replaced by an acyl group (RCO-) and an amide is formed.
    • The reaction is carried out in the presence of a base stronger than the amine, like pyridine. It removes the HCl formed and shifts the equilibrium to the right.
    • Tertiary amines have no H on nitrogen, so they do not undergo acylation.
    \[C_2H_5NH_2 + CH_3COCl \xrightarrow{Base} C_2H_5NHCOCH_3 + HCl\]
    \[(C_2H_5)_2NH + CH_3COCl \xrightarrow{Base} (C_2H_5)_2NCOCH_3 + HCl\]
    \[C_6H_5NH_2 + (CH_3CO)_2O &rarr; C_6H_5NHCOCH_3 + CH_3COOH\]

    • The products are N-ethylethanamide (from ethanamine), N,N-diethylethanamide (from N-ethylethanamine) and N-phenylethanamide or acetanilide (from aniline and ethanoic anhydride).

    • The reaction with benzoyl chloride (C6H5COCl) is called benzoylation:

    \[CH_3NH_2 + C_6H_5COCl &rarr; CH_3NHCOC_6H_5\ (N-methylbenzamide) + HCl\]
    • With carboxylic acids, amines simply form salts at room temperature.

    3. Carbylamine Reaction (Isocyanide Test)#

    • Aliphatic and aromatic primary amines on heating with chloroform and ethanolic KOH form isocyanides (carbylamines), which have a very foul smell.
    \[R-NH_2 + CHCl_3 + 3KOH \xrightarrow{Heat} R-NC + 3KCl + 3H_2O\]
    \[C_6H_5NH_2 + CHCl_3 + 3KOH \xrightarrow{Heat} C_6H_5NC\ (phenyl\ isocyanide) + 3KCl + 3H_2O\]
    • Secondary and tertiary amines do not give this reaction. So it is used as a test for primary amines.

    4. Reaction with Nitrous Acid#

    Nitrous acid (HNO2) is unstable, so it is prepared in situ from sodium nitrite and a mineral acid. The three classes of amines react differently with it.

    (a) Primary aliphatic amines#

    • They form aliphatic diazonium salts, which are very unstable. These decompose at once, liberating nitrogen gas quantitatively and forming alcohols.
    • The measured volume of N2 is used in the estimation of amino acids and proteins.
    \[R-NH_2 + HNO_2 \xrightarrow{NaNO_2 + HCl} [R-\overset{+}{N}_2\overset{-}{Cl}] \xrightarrow{H_2O} ROH + N_2 &uarr; + HCl\]

    (b) Primary aromatic amines#

    • At low temperature (273-278 K) they form arenediazonium salts, which are very useful in synthesis (see the next page). This process is called diazotisation.
    \[C_6H_5NH_2 \xrightarrow{NaNO_2 + 2HCl,\ 273-278\ K} C_6H_5\overset{+}{N}_2\overset{-}{Cl} + NaCl + 2H_2O\]

    (c) Secondary and tertiary amines#

    • These react with nitrous acid in a different way and do not give diazonium salts or nitrogen gas.

    5. Reaction with Arylsulphonyl Chloride (Hinsberg Test)#

    Benzenesulphonyl chloride (C6H5SO2Cl), also called Hinsberg's reagent, reacts with primary and secondary amines to form sulphonamides.

    (a) Primary amine#

    \[C_6H_5SO_2Cl + H-NH-C_2H_5 &rarr; C_6H_5SO_2-NH-C_2H_5 + HCl\]
    \[(N-Ethylbenzenesulphonamide,\ soluble\ in\ alkali)\]
    • The H left on nitrogen in the sulphonamide is strongly acidic, because the sulphonyl group is strongly electron withdrawing. So the product dissolves in alkali (KOH/NaOH).

    (b) Secondary amine#

    \[C_6H_5SO_2Cl + H-N(C_2H_5)_2 &rarr; C_6H_5SO_2-N(C_2H_5)_2 + HCl\]
    \[(N,N-Diethylbenzenesulphonamide,\ insoluble\ in\ alkali)\]
    • This sulphonamide has no H on nitrogen, so it is not acidic and is insoluble in alkali.

    (c) Tertiary amine#

    • Tertiary amines have no H on nitrogen and do not react with benzenesulphonyl chloride.

    Conclusion

    Amine Reaction with Hinsberg's reagent Product in alkali
    Primary (RNH2) Forms sulphonamide Soluble (clear solution)
    Secondary (R2NH) Forms sulphonamide Insoluble (precipitate)
    Tertiary (R3N) No reaction Amine remains unreacted

    This difference is used to distinguish 1°, 2° and 3° amines and also to separate a mixture of them. Nowadays p-toluenesulphonyl chloride is used in place of benzenesulphonyl chloride.

    6. Electrophilic Substitution in Aniline#

    • From the resonance structures of aniline, the ortho and para positions carry high electron density.
    • So the -NH2 group is ortho and para directing and a powerful activating group.

    (a) Bromination#

    • Aniline reacts with bromine water at room temperature to give a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromoaniline. Substitution occurs at all three o- and p-positions.
    \[C_6H_5NH_2 + 3Br_2 \xrightarrow{H_2O} 2,4,6-Br_3C_6H_2NH_2 &darr; + 3HBr\]

    Controlling the reactivity: protection of -NH2#

    • Because aniline is so reactive, it is hard to stop at monosubstitution. To make a monosubstituted product, the -NH2 group is first protected by acetylation with acetic anhydride. The substitution is then carried out, and finally the amide is hydrolysed back to the amine.
    \[C_6H_5NH_2 \xrightarrow{(CH_3CO)_2O,\ pyridine} C_6H_5NHCOCH_3 \xrightarrow{Br_2,\ CH_3COOH} p-BrC_6H_4NHCOCH_3 \xrightarrow{OH^-\ or\ H^+} p-BrC_6H_4NH_2\]
    \[(Aniline &rarr; Acetanilide &rarr; 4-Bromoacetanilide\ (major) &rarr; 4-Bromoaniline)\]
    • Why does this work? In acetanilide the lone pair on nitrogen is also in resonance with the C=O group:
    \[>\ddot{N}-C(=O)-CH_3\ \ &harr;\ \ >\overset{+}{N}=C(-\overset{-}{O})-CH_3\]
    • So the lone pair is less available for donation to the ring, and -NHCOCH3 is a weaker activator than -NH2.

    (b) Nitration#

    • Direct nitration of aniline gives tarry oxidation products along with nitro derivatives.
    • In the strongly acidic nitrating mixture, aniline is protonated to the anilinium ion (C6H5NH3+), which is meta directing. So a large amount of the meta product is also formed.
    \[C_6H_5NH_2 \xrightarrow{HNO_3,\ H_2SO_4,\ 288\ K} p-nitroaniline\ (51\%) + m-nitroaniline\ (47\%) + o-nitroaniline\ (2\%)\]
    • By protecting -NH2 through acetylation, nitration can be controlled and p-nitroaniline is obtained as the major product:
    \[C_6H_5NH_2 \xrightarrow{(CH_3CO)_2O,\ pyridine} C_6H_5NHCOCH_3 \xrightarrow{HNO_3,\ H_2SO_4,\ 288\ K} p-O_2NC_6H_4NHCOCH_3 \xrightarrow{OH^-\ or\ H^+} p-O_2NC_6H_4NH_2\]

    (c) Sulphonation#

    • Aniline reacts with concentrated H2SO4 to form anilinium hydrogensulphate. On heating with H2SO4 at 453-473 K, this gives p-aminobenzenesulphonic acid, commonly called sulphanilic acid, as the major product.
    • Sulphanilic acid exists mainly as a zwitter ion (p-H3N+-C6H4-SO3-).
    \[C_6H_5NH_2 \xrightarrow{H_2SO_4} C_6H_5\overset{+}{N}H_3\ HSO_4^- \xrightarrow{453-473\ K} p-H_2NC_6H_4SO_3H &#8652; p-H_3\overset{+}{N}C_6H_4SO_3^-\]

    (d) Friedel-Crafts reaction#

    • Aniline does not undergo Friedel-Crafts alkylation or acylation. Aniline (a Lewis base) forms a salt with the catalyst AlCl3 (a Lewis acid). The nitrogen then carries a positive charge and acts as a strong deactivating group for further reaction.

    Basic Principles of Organic Chemistry | Electronic Effects#

    Electron displacement in an organic molecule may take place in the ground state under the influence of an atom or substituent group, or in the presence of an attacking reagent.

    • Displacements caused by an atom or group present in the molecule cause permanent polarisation of bonds. Examples: inductive effect, resonance effect and hyperconjugation.
    • Displacements seen only when a reagent approaches to attack are temporary. This is called the electromeric effect or polarisability effect.

    1. Inductive Effect#

    • When a covalent bond is formed between atoms of different electronegativity, the electron density shifts towards the more electronegative atom. The bond becomes a polar covalent bond.
    • Consider chloroethane (CH3CH2Cl). The C-Cl bond is polarised so that carbon-1 gets a partial positive charge (δ+) and chlorine a partial negative charge (δ-). The shift is shown by an arrow pointing from the δ+ end to the δ- end.
    \[\underset{2}{\overset{\delta\delta+}{C}H_3} \rightarrow \underset{1}{\overset{\delta+}{C}H_2} \rightarrow \overset{\delta-}{Cl}\]
    • Carbon-1, now having δ+ charge, pulls some electron density from the adjacent C-C bond. So, a smaller positive charge (δδ+) develops on carbon-2 also.
    • Thus, the polar C-Cl bond induces polarity in the adjacent bonds. The polarisation of a σ bond caused by the polarisation of an adjacent σ bond is called the inductive effect.
    • The effect is passed on to further bonds, but it decreases rapidly as the number of intervening bonds increases, and becomes vanishingly small after three bonds.

    Electron-withdrawing and electron-donating groups#

    Based on their ability to withdraw or donate electron density relative to hydrogen, substituents are classified as:

    • Electron-withdrawing groups (-I effect): halogens, nitro (-NO2), cyano (-CN), carboxy (-COOH), ester (-COOR), aryloxy (-OAr, e.g. -OC6H5) etc.
    • Electron-donating groups (+I effect): alkyl groups like methyl (-CH3) and ethyl (-CH2CH3).

    Application

    An electron-withdrawing group stabilises a negative charge. For example, O2NCH2CH2O- is more stable than CH3CH2O-, because the -NO2 group (-I) disperses the negative charge, while the -CH3 group (+I) intensifies it.

    2. Resonance Structures#

    • Many molecules cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure. Benzene is an example.
    • A benzene ring with alternating C-C single and C=C double bonds should have two different bond lengths. But experimentally, all C-C bonds in benzene are of equal length, 139 pm, which is between the C-C single bond (154 pm) and the C=C double bond (134 pm).
    • Benzene is represented equally well by two energetically identical structures I and II (the two Kekule structures, in which the positions of the double bonds are interchanged).
    \[Structure\ I \longleftrightarrow Structure\ II\]
    • According to resonance theory, the actual structure of benzene is a resonance hybrid of I and II. These are called resonance structures (canonical or contributing structures).
    • The resonance structures are hypothetical and individually do not represent any real molecule. They contribute to the actual structure in proportion to their stability.

    Example: Nitromethane (CH3NO2)

    Nitromethane can be written as two Lewis structures, each with one N=O and one N-O bond:

    \[\underset{I}{CH_3-\overset{+}{N}(=O)-O^-} \longleftrightarrow \underset{II}{CH_3-\overset{+}{N}(-O^-)=O}\]

    The two structures differ only in which oxygen is double bonded to nitrogen. Experimentally, both N-O bonds are of the same length (intermediate between N-O and N=O), so the actual structure is a resonance hybrid of I and II.

    Resonance energy#

    • The energy of the actual structure (resonance hybrid) is lower than that of any canonical structure.
    • The difference in energy between the actual structure and the lowest energy resonance structure is called the resonance stabilisation energy or resonance energy.
    • More the number of important contributing structures, more is the resonance energy. Resonance is especially important when the contributing structures are equivalent in energy.

    Rules for writing resonance structures#

    1. Resonance structures must have the same positions of nuclei.
    2. They must have the same number of unpaired electrons.

    A resonance structure is more stable (contributes more) if it has:

    • more number of covalent bonds,
    • all atoms with an octet of electrons (except hydrogen, which has a duplet),
    • less separation of opposite charges,
    • a negative charge, if any, on the more electronegative atom and a positive charge, if any, on the more electropositive atom,
    • more dispersal of charge.

    Example: Acrolein (CH2=CH-CHO)

    \[\underset{I}{CH_2=CH-CH=O} \longleftrightarrow \underset{II}{\overset{+}{C}H_2-CH=CH-O^-} \longleftrightarrow \underset{III}{\overset{-}{C}H_2-CH=CH-\overset{+}{O}}\]
    \[Stability:\ I > II > III\]
    • I is most stable: more covalent bonds, every C and O has an octet and there is no charge separation.
    • II has a negative charge on the more electronegative atom (O) and a positive charge on the more electropositive atom (C).
    • III does not contribute, as O carries a positive charge and C a negative charge.

    3. Resonance Effect (Mesomeric Effect)#

    • The resonance effect is defined as the polarity produced in the molecule by the interaction of two π bonds, or between a π bond and a lone pair of electrons present on an adjacent atom.
    • The effect is transmitted through the chain. It is designated as the R or M effect.

    (i) Positive resonance effect (+R effect)#

    • Electrons are transferred away from the atom or group attached to the conjugated system. This makes certain positions of the molecule electron rich.
    • In aniline, the lone pair on N is delocalised into the ring. Besides the normal structure (I), three more contributing structures (II, III and IV) can be written. In each of them, N forms a double bond with the ring carbon and carries a positive charge (=N+H2), while a negative charge appears on one of the two ortho carbons (II and IV) or on the para carbon (III).
    • Thus, the ortho and para positions of aniline become electron rich.

    (ii) Negative resonance effect (-R effect)#

    • Electrons are transferred towards the atom or group attached to the conjugated system.
    • In nitrobenzene, π electrons of the ring move towards the -NO2 group, In the contributing structures, a ring carbon forms a double bond with N, both oxygens carry a negative charge, and a positive charge develops on an ortho or para carbon of the ring.
    • Thus, the ortho and para positions of nitrobenzene become electron deficient.

    Groups showing +R and -R effects:

    Effect Groups
    +R effect -halogen, -OH, -OR, -OCOR, -NH2, -NHR, -NR2, -NHCOR
    -R effect -COOH, -CHO, >C=O, -CN, -NO2
    • The presence of alternate single and double bonds in an open chain or cyclic system is called a conjugated system, e.g. 1,3-butadiene, aniline and nitrobenzene. In such systems the π electrons are delocalised and the system develops polarity.

    4. Electromeric Effect (E effect)#

    • It is a temporary effect shown by compounds having a multiple bond (double or triple bond), only in the presence of an attacking reagent.
    • It is defined as the complete transfer of a shared pair of π electrons to one of the atoms joined by a multiple bond on the demand of an attacking reagent.
    • The effect disappears as soon as the attacking reagent is removed. It is shown by a curved arrow from the π bond to the atom receiving the electrons.

    (i) Positive electromeric effect (+E effect)#

    The π electrons are transferred to the atom to which the reagent gets attached.

    \[>C=C< + H^+ &rarr; >\overset{+}{C}-C(H)<\]

    (ii) Negative electromeric effect (-E effect)#

    The π electrons are transferred to the atom to which the reagent does not get attached.

    \[>C=O + CN^- &rarr; >C(CN)-O^-\]

    Note

    When inductive and electromeric effects act in opposite directions, the electromeric effect predominates.

    5. Hyperconjugation#

    • Hyperconjugation is a general stabilising interaction. It involves delocalisation of σ electrons of a C-H bond of an alkyl group directly attached to an atom of an unsaturated system or to an atom with an unshared (empty) p orbital.
    • It is a permanent effect.

    Hyperconjugation in carbocations#

    • In the ethyl cation (CH3CH2+), the positively charged carbon has an empty p orbital.
    • One of the C-H bonds of the methyl group can align in the plane of this empty p orbital, and its electrons get delocalised into the empty p orbital.
    • This electron density from the adjacent σ bond helps in dispersing the positive charge, which stabilises the carbocation.
    \[H-CH_2-\overset{+}{C}H_2 \longleftrightarrow H^+\ CH_2=CH_2\]
    • Greater the number of alkyl groups attached to the positive carbon, greater is the number of C-H bonds available for hyperconjugation, and greater is the stability:
    \[(CH_3)_3\overset{+}{C} > (CH_3)_2\overset{+}{C}H > CH_3\overset{+}{C}H_2 > \overset{+}{C}H_3\]

    Hyperconjugation in alkenes#

    • Hyperconjugation is also possible in alkenes and alkylarenes. In propene, the C-H σ electrons of the methyl group are delocalised into the π system:
    \[H-CH_2-CH=CH_2 \longleftrightarrow H^+\ CH_2=CH-\overset{-}{C}H_2\]
    • One way of looking at hyperconjugation is to regard the C-H bond as having partial ionic character due to resonance. Since one contributing structure has no bond between C and H, hyperconjugation is also called no bond resonance.

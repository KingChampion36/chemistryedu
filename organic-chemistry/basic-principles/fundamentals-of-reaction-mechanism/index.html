    Basic Principles of Organic Chemistry | Fundamentals of Reaction Mechanism#

    A sequential account of each step of an organic reaction, describing the details of electron movement, energetics during bond cleavage and bond formation, and the rates of transformation of reactants into products (kinetics), is called the reaction mechanism.

    Substrate and Reagent#

    • In an organic reaction, the organic molecule (substrate) reacts with an attacking reagent to form one or more intermediates and finally the product(s) and by-products.
    \[Substrate \xrightarrow{Attacking\ reagent} [Intermediate] &rarr; Product(s) + By\text{-}products\]
    • The substrate is the reactant which supplies carbon to the new bond. The other reactant is the reagent.
    • If both reactants supply carbon to the new bond, the choice is arbitrary, and the molecule on which attention is focused is called the substrate.
    • In a reaction, a covalent bond between two carbons, or between carbon and another atom, is broken and a new bond is formed. Knowing the mechanism helps us understand reactivity and plan syntheses.

    Fission of a Covalent Bond#

    A covalent bond can be broken in two ways: (i) heterolytic cleavage and (ii) homolytic cleavage.

    1. Heterolytic cleavage#

    • In heterolytic cleavage, the bond breaks in such a way that the shared pair of electrons stays with one of the fragments.
    • After heterolysis, one atom has a sextet of electrons and a positive charge, and the other has an octet with at least one lone pair and a negative charge.
    \[H_3C-Br &rarr; H_3\overset{+}{C} + Br^-\]
    • Reactions which proceed through heterolytic cleavage are called ionic, heteropolar or simply polar reactions.

    Carbocations#

    • A species having a carbon atom with a sextet of electrons and a positive charge is called a carbocation (earlier called carbonium ion). \(CH_3^+\) is the methyl cation.
    • Carbocations are classified as primary, secondary or tertiary depending on whether one, two or three carbons are directly attached to the positively charged carbon.

    Examples

    • CH3CH2+ (ethyl cation): primary carbocation
    • (CH3)2CH+ (isopropyl cation): secondary carbocation
    • (CH3)3C+ (tert-butyl cation): tertiary carbocation
    • Carbocations are highly unstable and reactive. Alkyl groups attached to the positive carbon stabilise it by inductive and hyperconjugation effects. The observed order of stability is:
    \[\overset{+}{C}H_3 < CH_3\overset{+}{C}H_2 < (CH_3)_2\overset{+}{C}H < (CH_3)_3\overset{+}{C}\]
    • Shape: The positively charged carbon is sp2 hybridised, so carbocations are trigonal planar. In \(CH_3^+\), the three sp2 orbitals of carbon overlap with the 1s orbitals of three hydrogens to form three C(sp2)-H(1s) sigma bonds. The remaining unhybridised p orbital is perpendicular to the molecular plane and is empty.

    Carbanions#

    • Heterolytic cleavage can also give a species in which carbon keeps the shared pair of electrons. When a group Z leaves without the electron pair, the methyl anion is formed.
    \[CH_3-Z &rarr; H_3C:^- + Z^+\]
    • A carbon species carrying a negative charge on the carbon atom is called a carbanion. Carbanions are also unstable and reactive.

    2. Homolytic cleavage#

    • In homolytic cleavage, one electron of the shared pair goes with each of the bonded atoms.
    • The movement of a single electron is shown by a half-headed ('fish hook') curved arrow.
    • Homolysis gives neutral species containing an unpaired electron, called free radicals. Like carbocations and carbanions, free radicals are very reactive.
    \[R-X \xrightarrow{Heat\ or\ Light} \underset{Alkyl\ free\ radical}{\dot{R}} + \dot{X}\]
    • Alkyl radicals are classified as primary, secondary or tertiary. Their stability increases from primary to tertiary:
    \[\underset{Methyl}{\dot{C}H_3} < \underset{Ethyl}{\dot{C}H_2CH_3} < \underset{Isopropyl}{\dot{C}H(CH_3)_2} < \underset{tert\text{-}Butyl}{\dot{C}(CH_3)_3}\]
    • Reactions which proceed by homolytic fission are called free radical, homopolar or nonpolar reactions.

    Nucleophiles and Electrophiles#

    Nucleophile#

    • A reagent that brings an electron pair is called a nucleophile (Nu:), i.e. nucleus seeking. The reaction is then called nucleophilic.
    • Examples:
      • Negatively charged ions with lone pairs: hydroxide (HO-), cyanide (NC-) and carbanions (R3C:-).
      • Neutral molecules with lone pairs: H2O:, R3N:, R2NH: etc. (lone pairs on O and N).

    Electrophile#

    • A reagent that takes away an electron pair is called an electrophile (E+), i.e. electron seeking. The reaction is called electrophilic.

    • Examples:

      • Carbocations (\(CH_3^+\)): the carbon has only a sextet, so it is electron deficient and can accept an electron pair.
      • Neutral molecules having polar functional groups like carbonyl (>C=O) or alkyl halides (R3C-X). Due to the polarity of the C-X bond, the carbon gets a partial positive charge and becomes an electrophilic centre.

    • During a polar reaction, a nucleophile attacks the electrophilic centre (electron deficient site) of the substrate. Similarly, an electrophile attacks the nucleophilic centre (electron rich site) of the substrate.

    • The electrophile receives an electron pair from the nucleophile when the two bond together.

    Note

    Electrophiles may be neutral, like BF3 and AlCl3, which have an incomplete octet on the central atom. Nucleophiles may also be neutral, like NH3 and H2O, which have lone pairs.

    Electron Movement in Organic Reactions#

    • The movement of electrons in a reaction is shown by curved-arrow notation.
    • A curved arrow starts from the point from where an electron pair is shifted and ends at the location to which the pair moves.
    • A full-headed curved arrow shows the movement of an electron pair. A single-barbed 'fish hook' (half-headed) arrow shows the movement of a single electron.
    • An electron pair can shift:
      1. from a π bond to an adjacent bond position,
      2. from a π bond to an adjacent atom,
      3. from an atom (lone pair) to an adjacent bond position.

    Example

    In the reaction of hydroxide ion with chloromethane to give methanol, one curved arrow is drawn from a lone pair on O of HO- to the carbon, and a second arrow from the C-Cl bond to Cl, showing Cl leaving as Cl-.

    \[HO^- + CH_3-Cl &rarr; HO-CH_3 + Cl^-\]

    Types of Organic Reactions#

    Organic reactions can be classified into the following categories:

    1. Substitution reactions#

    An atom or group in the substrate is replaced by another atom or group.

    \[CH_3CH_2Br + HS^- &rarr; CH_3CH_2SH + Br^-\]

    2. Addition reactions#

    Two molecules combine to give a single product; this usually happens at a multiple bond.

    \[(CH_3)_2C=CH_2 + HCl &rarr; (CH_3)_2CClCH_3\]

    3. Elimination reactions#

    Atoms or groups are removed from adjacent carbons of the substrate to form a multiple bond.

    \[CH_3CH_2Br + HO^- &rarr; CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O + Br^-\]

    4. Rearrangement reactions#

    The carbon skeleton or the position of atoms/groups in the molecule changes to give an isomeric structure.

    \[(CH_3)_3C-CH_2OH + HBr &rarr; (CH_3)_2CBrCH_2CH_3 + H_2O\]

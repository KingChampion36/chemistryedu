    Alkyl Halides | Nature of C-X Bond and Methods of Preparation#

    In this article, we will discuss the nature of the carbon-halogen bond and the important methods used to prepare haloalkanes and haloarenes.

    Nature of C-X Bond#

    • Halogen atoms are more electronegative than carbon. So, the C-X bond in an alkyl halide is polarised: carbon carries a partial positive charge (δ+) and halogen carries a partial negative charge (δ-).
    \[\overset{\delta+}{C} - \overset{\delta-}{X}\]
    • The size of the halogen atom increases down the group (F is smallest, I is largest). Hence, the C-X bond length increases from C-F to C-I, while the bond enthalpy decreases.
    Bond Bond length (pm) C-X Bond enthalpy (kJ mol-1) Dipole moment (Debye)
    CH3-F 139 452 1.847
    CH3-Cl 178 351 1.860
    CH3-Br 193 293 1.830
    CH3-I 214 234 1.636

    Note

    A longer, weaker C-X bond breaks more easily. This is why the reactivity of alkyl halides follows the order R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F.

    Methods of Preparation#

    1. From Alcohols#

    • Alcohols are easily available, so they are the best starting material for alkyl halides.
    • The -OH group of an alcohol is replaced by a halogen using concentrated halogen acids (HX), phosphorus halides (PX3, PCl5) or thionyl chloride (SOCl2).
    \[R-OH + HX \xrightarrow{ZnCl_2} R-X + H_2O\]
    \[R-OH + NaBr + H_2SO_4 &rarr; R-Br + NaHSO_4 + H_2O\]
    \[3R-OH + PX_3 &rarr; 3R-X + H_3PO_3\ \ (X = Cl,\ Br)\]
    \[R-OH + PCl_5 &rarr; R-Cl + POCl_3 + HCl\]
    \[R-OH \xrightarrow{red\ P/X_2\ (X_2 = Br_2,\ I_2)} R-X\]
    \[R-OH + SOCl_2 &rarr; R-Cl + SO_2 &uarr; + HCl &uarr;\]
    • Thionyl chloride is preferred, because both by-products (SO2 and HCl) are gases that escape, leaving pure alkyl chloride behind.
    • PBr3 and PI3 are usually generated in situ (inside the reaction mixture) by reacting red phosphorus with bromine or iodine.
    • Alkyl chlorides are made either by passing dry HCl gas through the alcohol, or by heating the alcohol with concentrated aqueous HCl.
    • Primary and secondary alcohols need anhydrous ZnCl2 as catalyst with HX. Tertiary alcohols react simply on shaking with concentrated HCl at room temperature.
    • Alkyl bromides are prepared by constant boiling with HBr (48%). Good yields of alkyl iodides are obtained by heating the alcohol with NaI or KI in 95% phosphoric acid.
    • Order of reactivity of alcohols with a given haloacid:
    \[3^0 > 2^0 > 1^0\]

    Note

    This method cannot be used to make aryl halides from phenols. The C-O bond in phenol has partial double bond character (due to resonance), so it is stronger than a single bond and difficult to break.

    2. From Hydrocarbons#

    (a) Free radical halogenation#

    • Chlorination or bromination of alkanes in UV light or heat gives a complex mixture of isomeric mono- and polyhaloalkanes.
    • These are difficult to separate, so the yield of any single product is low.
    \[CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3 \xrightarrow[or\ heat]{Cl_2/UV\ light} CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Cl + CH_3CH_2CHClCH_3\]

    (b) Electrophilic substitution (for haloarenes)#

    • Aryl chlorides and bromides are prepared by treating arenes with Cl2 or Br2 in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst such as iron or FeCl3, in the dark.
    \[C_6H_5CH_3 + X_2 \xrightarrow[dark]{Fe} o-XC_6H_4CH_3 + p-XC_6H_4CH_3\]
    \[(o-Halotoluene\ and\ p-Halotoluene)\]
    • The ortho and para isomers are easily separated because of the large difference in their melting points.
    • Iodination is reversible. An oxidising agent (HNO3 or HIO4) is needed to oxidise the HI formed, so that the reaction moves forward.
    • Fluoroarenes are not made this way because fluorine is too reactive.

    (c) Sandmeyer's reaction (for haloarenes)#

    • A primary aromatic amine in cold aqueous mineral acid is treated with sodium nitrite (NaNO2) at 273-278 K to form a diazonium salt.
    • The freshly prepared diazonium salt is mixed with cuprous chloride or cuprous bromide. The diazonium group is replaced by -Cl or -Br.
    \[C_6H_5NH_2 \xrightarrow[273-278\ K]{NaNO_2\ +\ HX} C_6H_5N_2^+X^-\]
    \[C_6H_5N_2^+X^- \xrightarrow{Cu_2X_2} C_6H_5X + N_2 &uarr;\ \ (X = Cl,\ Br)\]
    • For iodobenzene, cuprous halide is not needed. The diazonium salt is simply shaken with potassium iodide.
    \[C_6H_5N_2^+X^- + KI &rarr; C_6H_5I + N_2 &uarr; + KX\]

    (d) From alkenes#

    (i) Addition of hydrogen halides: An alkene adds HCl, HBr or HI to form an alkyl halide.

    \[>C=C< + HX &rarr; >CH-CX<\]
    • Unsymmetrical alkenes like propene can give two products, but the major one is decided by Markovnikov's rule (the halogen goes to the carbon having fewer hydrogens).
    \[CH_3CH=CH_2 + HI &rarr; \underset{minor}{CH_3CH_2CH_2I} + \underset{major}{CH_3CHICH_3}\]
    • In the presence of peroxide, HBr adds in the anti-Markovnikov manner.

    (ii) Addition of halogens: Bromine in CCl4 adds across a double bond to form a colourless vic-dibromide. The disappearance of the reddish-brown colour of bromine is a laboratory test for unsaturation.

    \[CH_2=CH_2 + Br_2 \xrightarrow{CCl_4} BrCH_2-CH_2Br\]
    \[(vic-Dibromide)\]

    3. Halogen Exchange#

    (a) Finkelstein reaction#

    • Alkyl iodides are prepared by heating alkyl chlorides or bromides with NaI in dry acetone.
    \[R-X + NaI \xrightarrow{dry\ acetone} R-I + NaX\ \ (X = Cl,\ Br)\]
    • NaCl or NaBr formed is insoluble in dry acetone and precipitates out. Removal of a product pushes the equilibrium forward (Le Chatelier's principle).

    (b) Swarts reaction#

    • Alkyl fluorides are best prepared by heating an alkyl chloride or bromide with a metallic fluoride such as AgF, Hg2F2, CoF2 or SbF3.
    \[H_3C-Br + AgF &rarr; H_3C-F + AgBr\]

    Conclusion

    Fluorides and iodides are difficult to make by direct halogenation, so they are best prepared by halogen exchange: Swarts reaction for fluorides and Finkelstein reaction for iodides.

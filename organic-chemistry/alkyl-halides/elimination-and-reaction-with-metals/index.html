    Alkyl Halides | Elimination and Reaction with Metals#

    In this article, we will discuss the elimination reactions of haloalkanes (dehydrohalogenation), the competition between elimination and substitution, and the reactions of haloalkanes with metals.

    Elimination Reactions#

    • When a haloalkane having a β-hydrogen atom is heated with alcoholic KOH, a hydrogen atom is removed from the β-carbon and the halogen atom from the α-carbon.
    • The α-carbon is the one carrying the halogen; the carbon next to it is the β-carbon.
    • As a result, an alkene is formed. Since a β-hydrogen is involved, the reaction is called β-elimination (or dehydrohalogenation).
    \[B^- + H-\overset{\beta}{C}-\overset{\alpha}{C}-X &rarr; >C=C< + B-H + X^-\]
    \[B = Base;\ \ X = Leaving\ group\]

    Example

    \[CH_3CH_2Br + KOH_{(alc)} \xrightarrow{&Delta;} CH_2=CH_2 + KBr + H_2O\]

    Saytzeff (Zaitsev) Rule#

    • If the halide has more than one type of β-hydrogen, more than one alkene can form. Usually, one of them is the major product.
    • In 1875, the Russian chemist Alexander Zaitsev (also written Saytzeff) gave a rule:

    Saytzeff Rule

    In dehydrohalogenation reactions, the preferred product is that alkene which has the greater number of alkyl groups attached to the doubly bonded carbon atoms.

    • In simple words, the more substituted (and more stable) alkene is the major product.

    Example: Dehydrohalogenation of 2-bromopentane

    \[CH_3CH_2CH_2CH(Br)CH_3 \xrightarrow{alc.\ KOH} \underset{Pent-2-ene\ (81\%)}{CH_3CH_2CH=CHCH_3} + \underset{Pent-1-ene\ (19\%)}{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH=CH_2}\]

    Pent-2-ene has two alkyl groups on the double bond carbons while pent-1-ene has only one. So pent-2-ene is the major product.

    Elimination versus Substitution#

    • An alkyl halide with β-hydrogens, when treated with a base or nucleophile, has two competing routes: substitution (SN1 or SN2) and elimination.
    • A reaction is like a race: the route that is easiest (fastest) wins. Which route is taken depends on the nature of the alkyl halide, the strength and size of the base/nucleophile, and the reaction conditions.
    • A bulky nucleophile (e.g. (CH3)3CO-) finds it hard to approach the crowded tetravalent carbon. So it prefers to act as a base and pull off a proton, giving elimination. A small nucleophile (e.g. CH3CH2O-) more easily gives substitution.
    Type of alkyl halide Preferred route
    Primary SN2
    Secondary SN2 or elimination, depending on the strength of the base/nucleophile
    Tertiary SN1 or elimination, depending on the stability of the carbocation or of the more substituted alkene

    Note

    Aqueous KOH gives mainly alcohols, since OH- in water is a good nucleophile (substitution). Alcoholic KOH contains the alkoxide ion RO-, a stronger base, which favours elimination and gives alkenes.

    Reaction with Metals#

    • Most organic chlorides, bromides and iodides react with certain metals to give compounds containing carbon-metal bonds. These are called organometallic compounds.

    1. Grignard Reagents#

    • Alkyl magnesium halides, RMgX, were discovered by Victor Grignard in 1900. They are called Grignard reagents.
    • They are prepared by reacting a haloalkane with magnesium metal in dry ether.
    \[CH_3CH_2Br + Mg \xrightarrow{dry\ ether} CH_3CH_2MgBr\]
    \[(Ethylmagnesium\ bromide,\ a\ Grignard\ reagent)\]
    • In RMgX, the C-Mg bond is covalent but highly polar: carbon pulls electrons from the electropositive magnesium. The Mg-X bond is essentially ionic.
    \[\overset{\delta-}{R}-\overset{\delta+}{Mg}-\overset{\delta-}{X}\]
    • Grignard reagents are highly reactive. They react with any source of proton to give hydrocarbons. Even water, alcohols and amines are acidic enough to destroy them.
    \[RMgX + H_2O &rarr; RH + Mg(OH)X\]
    • So, even traces of moisture must be avoided while preparing a Grignard reagent (anhydrous conditions). On the other hand, this reaction is also a method of converting halides into hydrocarbons.
    • Grignard was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1912, shared with Paul Sabatier.

    2. Wurtz Reaction#

    • Alkyl halides react with sodium in dry ether to give hydrocarbons containing double the number of carbon atoms present in the halide.
    \[2RX + 2Na \xrightarrow{dry\ ether} R-R + 2NaX\]

    Example

    \[2CH_3Br + 2Na \xrightarrow{dry\ ether} CH_3-CH_3 + 2NaBr\]
    \[2CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Cl + 2Na \xrightarrow{dry\ ether} CH_3(CH_2)_6CH_3 + 2NaCl\]

    1-Chlorobutane gives n-octane.

    Hydrocarbons | Chemical Properties of Alkenes#

    Alkenes are a rich source of loosely held π electrons, so they mainly undergo addition reactions in which electrophiles add on to the carbon-carbon double bond.

    • Some reagents add by a free radical mechanism.
    • Oxidation and ozonolysis are also important reactions of alkenes.

    1. Addition of Dihydrogen#

    Alkenes add one molecule of H2 in the presence of finely divided Ni, Pd or Pt to form alkanes.

    \[CH_2=CH_2 + H_2 \xrightarrow{Ni/Pd/Pt} CH_3-CH_3\]

    2. Addition of Halogens#

    • Bromine or chlorine adds to alkenes to give vicinal dihalides. Iodine does not add under normal conditions.
    • The reddish orange colour of bromine in CCl4 disappears when bromine adds to an unsaturated site. This is used as a test for unsaturation.
    • It is an electrophilic addition reaction that proceeds through a cyclic halonium ion.
    \[CH_2=CH_2 + Br-Br \xrightarrow{CCl_4} BrCH_2-CH_2Br\ \ \ (1,2\text{-}Dibromoethane)\]
    \[CH_3-CH=CH_2 + Cl-Cl &rarr; CH_3-CHCl-CH_2Cl\ \ \ (1,2\text{-}Dichloropropane)\]

    3. Addition of Hydrogen Halides#

    • HCl, HBr and HI add to alkenes to form alkyl halides by electrophilic addition.
    • Order of reactivity:
    \[HI > HBr > HCl\]

    Addition to Symmetrical Alkenes#

    When the same groups are attached to both doubly bonded carbons, only one product is possible:

    \[CH_2=CH_2 + H-Br &rarr; CH_3-CH_2-Br\]
    \[CH_3-CH=CH-CH_3 + HBr &rarr; CH_3-CH_2-CHBr-CH_3\]

    Addition to Unsymmetrical Alkenes (Markovnikov Rule)#

    HBr can add to propene in two ways:

    \[CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H-Br &rarr; CH_3-CHBr-CH_3\ \ \ (I:\ 2\text{-}Bromopropane)\]
    \[CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H-Br &rarr; CH_3-CH_2-CH_2Br\ \ \ (II:\ 1\text{-}Bromopropane)\]

    Markovnikov Rule

    The negative part of the addendum (adding molecule) gets attached to that carbon atom which possesses the lesser number of hydrogen atoms.

    According to this rule (framed by the Russian chemist Markovnikov in 1869), product I, 2-bromopropane, is expected, and it is indeed the principal product.

    Mechanism#

    HBr provides the electrophile H+, which attacks the double bond to form a carbocation. Two carbocations are possible:

    \[CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H^+ &rarr; CH_3-CH_2-\overset{+}{C}H_2\ \ \ (a:\ less\ stable\ 1^\circ\ carbocation)\]
    \[CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H^+ &rarr; CH_3-\overset{+}{C}H-CH_3\ \ \ (b:\ more\ stable\ 2^\circ\ carbocation)\]
    • The secondary carbocation (b) is more stable than the primary carbocation (a), so it is formed faster and predominates.
    • Br- then attacks carbocation (b):
    \[CH_3-\overset{+}{C}H-CH_3 + Br^- &rarr; CH_3-CHBr-CH_3\ \ \ (2\text{-}Bromopropane,\ major)\]

    Anti-Markovnikov Addition (Peroxide Effect or Kharash Effect)#

    • In the presence of a peroxide, HBr adds to unsymmetrical alkenes like propene contrary to the Markovnikov rule.
    • This was observed by M.S. Kharash and F.R. Mayo in 1933 at the University of Chicago. It is called the peroxide effect or Kharash effect.
    • It happens only with HBr, not with HCl or HI.
    \[CH_3-CH=CH_2 + HBr \xrightarrow{(C_6H_5CO)_2O_2} CH_3-CH_2-CH_2Br\ \ \ (1\text{-}Bromopropane)\]

    Mechanism (Free Radical Chain)#

    \[(i)\ C_6H_5COO-OOCC_6H_5 \xrightarrow{Homolysis} 2C_6H_5CO\dot{O} &rarr; 2\dot{C}_6H_5 + 2CO_2\]
    \[(ii)\ \dot{C}_6H_5 + H-Br \xrightarrow{Homolysis} C_6H_6 + \dot{B}r\]
    \[(iii)\ CH_3-CH=CH_2 + \dot{B}r &rarr; CH_3-\dot{C}H-CH_2Br\ \ \ (b:\ more\ stable\ 2^\circ\ radical)\]
    \[\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ CH_3-CH=CH_2 + \dot{B}r &rarr; CH_3-CHBr-\dot{C}H_2\ \ \ (a:\ less\ stable\ 1^\circ\ radical)\]
    \[(iv)\ CH_3-\dot{C}H-CH_2Br + H-Br &rarr; CH_3-CH_2-CH_2Br + \dot{B}r\ \ \ (major)\]
    \[(v)\ CH_3-CHBr-\dot{C}H_2 + H-Br &rarr; CH_3-CHBr-CH_3 + \dot{B}r\ \ \ (minor)\]
    • The bromine radical adds to the terminal carbon so that the more stable secondary free radical is formed. This explains why 1-bromopropane is the major product.

    Why no peroxide effect with HCl and HI?

    • The H–Cl bond (430.5 kJ mol-1) is stronger than the H–Br bond (363.7 kJ mol-1), so it is not cleaved by the free radical.
    • The H–I bond (296.8 kJ mol-1) is weaker, but iodine free radicals combine with each other to form I2 instead of adding to the double bond.

    4. Addition of Sulphuric Acid#

    Cold concentrated H2SO4 adds to alkenes according to the Markovnikov rule, forming alkyl hydrogen sulphates by electrophilic addition.

    \[CH_2=CH_2 + H-OSO_2OH &rarr; CH_3-CH_2-OSO_2OH\ \ \ (Ethyl\ hydrogen\ sulphate)\]
    \[CH_3-CH=CH_2 + HOSO_2OH &rarr; CH_3-CH(OSO_2OH)-CH_3\ \ \ (Propyl\ hydrogen\ sulphate)\]

    5. Addition of Water (Hydration)#

    In the presence of a few drops of concentrated sulphuric acid, alkenes react with water to form alcohols, following the Markovnikov rule.

    \[(CH_3)_2C=CH_2 + H_2O \xrightarrow{H^+} (CH_3)_3C-OH\]
    \[2\text{-}Methylpropene &rarr; 2\text{-}Methylpropan\text{-}2\text{-}ol\]

    6. Oxidation#

    (a) With cold dilute KMnO4 (Baeyer's reagent)#

    Alkenes react with cold, dilute aqueous KMnO4 to give vicinal glycols. Decolourisation of the purple KMnO4 solution is used as a test for unsaturation.

    \[CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O + O \xrightarrow[273\ K]{dil.\ KMnO_4} HOCH_2-CH_2OH\ \ \ (Ethane\text{-}1,2\text{-}diol\ or\ glycol)\]
    \[CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H_2O + O \xrightarrow[273\ K]{dil.\ KMnO_4} CH_3CH(OH)CH_2OH\ \ \ (Propane\text{-}1,2\text{-}diol)\]

    (b) With acidic KMnO4 or acidic K2Cr2O7#

    These oxidise alkenes to ketones and/or acids, depending on the alkene and the conditions. The double bond is cleaved.

    \[(CH_3)_2C=CH_2 \xrightarrow{KMnO_4/H^+} (CH_3)_2C=O + CO_2 + H_2O\]
    \[CH_3-CH=CH-CH_3 \xrightarrow{KMnO_4/H^+} 2CH_3COOH\]

    7. Ozonolysis#

    • Ozone adds to the alkene to form an ozonide, which is then cleaved by Zn–H2O into smaller molecules (aldehydes and/or ketones).
    • Each doubly bonded carbon becomes a C=O group. So by identifying the products, the position of the double bond can be located.
    \[CH_3CH=CH_2 + O_3 &rarr; Propene\ ozonide \xrightarrow{Zn + H_2O} CH_3CHO + HCHO\]
    \[(CH_3)_2C=CH_2 + O_3 &rarr; Ozonide \xrightarrow{Zn + H_2O} (CH_3)_2C=O + HCHO\]

    Example

    Propene gives ethanal and methanal, while 2-methylpropene gives propan-2-one (acetone) and methanal.

    8. Polymerisation#

    • A large number of ethene molecules combine at high temperature, high pressure and in the presence of a catalyst to give polythene. The large molecules formed are called polymers, the simple starting molecules are monomers, and the reaction is called polymerisation.
    \[n(CH_2=CH_2) \xrightarrow[Catalyst]{High\ temp./pressure} -(CH_2-CH_2)_n-\ \ \ (Polythene)\]
    \[n(CH_3-CH=CH_2) \xrightarrow[Catalyst]{High\ temp./pressure} -(CH(CH_3)-CH_2)_n-\ \ \ (Polypropene)\]
    • Polythene is used for plastic bags, squeeze bottles, refrigerator dishes, toys, pipes, radio and T.V. cabinets.
    • Polypropene is used for milk crates, plastic buckets and other moulded articles.
    • Excessive use of polythene and polypropylene is a matter of great environmental concern.

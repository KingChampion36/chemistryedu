    Hydrocarbons | Alkenes#

    Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond.

    • With one double bond, an alkene has two hydrogen atoms less than the corresponding alkane. So the general formula is:
    \[C_nH_{2n}\]
    • Alkenes are also called olefins (oil forming), because the first member, ethylene (C2H4), forms an oily liquid with chlorine.

    Structure of the Double Bond#

    • A C=C double bond consists of one strong σ bond (bond enthalpy about 397 kJ mol-1), formed by head-on overlap of sp2 hybrid orbitals, and one weak π bond (about 284 kJ mol-1), formed by sideways overlap of the two unhybridised 2p orbitals.
    • The C=C bond length (134 pm) is shorter than the C–C single bond (154 pm).
    • In ethene, each carbon is sp2 hybridised. The H–C–C angle is 121.7°, the H–C–H angle is 116.6° and the C–H bond length is 110 pm. The π electron cloud lies above and below the plane of the molecule.
    • The C=C bond enthalpy (681 kJ mol-1) is greater than that of the C–C single bond in ethane (348 kJ mol-1).

    Conclusion

    The π bond is weak and its electrons are loosely held. So alkenes act as a source of mobile electrons and are easily attacked by electrophilic reagents (electron seeking species). This makes alkenes less stable than alkanes, and they readily add reagents to become single-bonded compounds.

    Nomenclature#

    • The longest chain containing the double bond is selected.
    • Numbering starts from the end nearer to the double bond.
    • The suffix '-ane' of the alkane is replaced by '-ene'.
    • The first member, CH2 (methene), has a very short life. The first stable member is C2H4, ethylene (common) or ethene (IUPAC).
    Structure IUPAC Name
    CH3–CH=CH2 Propene
    CH3–CH2–CH=CH2 But-1-ene
    CH3–CH=CH–CH3 But-2-ene
    CH2=CH–CH=CH2 Buta-1,3-diene
    CH2=C(CH3)–CH3 2-Methylprop-1-ene
    CH2=CH–CH(CH3)–CH3 3-Methylbut-1-ene

    Isomerism in Alkenes#

    Alkenes show both structural isomerism and geometrical isomerism.

    1. Structural Isomerism#

    Ethene and propene have only one structure each. C4H8 can be written in three ways:

    Isomers of C4H8

    \[I.\ CH_2=CH-CH_2-CH_3\ \ \ (But\text{-}1\text{-}ene)\]
    \[II.\ CH_3-CH=CH-CH_3\ \ \ (But\text{-}2\text{-}ene)\]
    \[III.\ CH_2=C(CH_3)-CH_3\ \ \ (2\text{-}Methylprop\text{-}1\text{-}ene)\]
    • I and III, and II and III, are chain isomers.
    • I and II are position isomers (they differ in the position of the double bond).

    2. Geometrical Isomerism#

    • Rotation about a C=C bond is restricted, because rotating would break the π bond.
    • If each doubly bonded carbon carries two different atoms or groups (type YXC=CXY), two different spatial arrangements are possible. Such stereoisomers are called geometrical isomers.
    • Cis isomer: identical atoms or groups lie on the same side of the double bond.
    • Trans isomer: identical atoms or groups lie on opposite sides of the double bond.
    • Cis and trans isomers have the same structure but different configuration, so they differ in m.p., b.p., dipole moment, solubility, etc.

    Example: But-2-ene

    • cis-But-2-ene: both CH3 groups on the same side; b.p. 277 K; dipole moment 0.33 D.
    • trans-But-2-ene: CH3 groups on opposite sides; b.p. 274 K; dipole moment almost zero.
    • The cis form is more polar. In the trans form, the two C–CH3 bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel each other, making it non-polar.
    • Among solids, the trans isomer has a higher melting point than the cis form (it packs better).
    • Geometrical isomerism is also shown by alkenes of the types XYC=CXZ and XYC=CZW.

    Note

    If either doubly bonded carbon carries two identical groups (for example (CH3)2C=CH–C2H5 or CH2=CBr2), cis-trans isomerism is not possible.

    Preparation of Alkenes#

    1. From Alkynes (Partial Reduction)#

    • Alkynes are partially reduced with a calculated amount of H2 in the presence of palladised charcoal partially deactivated with poisons like sulphur compounds or quinoline. This catalyst is called Lindlar's catalyst. The alkene formed has cis geometry.
    • Reduction with sodium in liquid ammonia gives trans alkenes.
    \[RC \equiv CR' + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd/C} cis\text{-}RCH=CHR'\]
    \[RC \equiv CR' + H_2 \xrightarrow{Na/liquid\ NH_3} trans\text{-}RCH=CHR'\]
    \[CH \equiv CH + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd/C} CH_2=CH_2\]
    \[CH_3-C \equiv CH + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd/C} CH_3-CH=CH_2\]
    • Propene obtained this way does not show geometrical isomerism, because one of its doubly bonded carbons (=CH2) has two identical H atoms.

    2. From Alkyl Halides (Dehydrohalogenation)#

    • Alkyl halides, on heating with alcoholic KOH, eliminate one molecule of halogen acid to form alkenes. This is called dehydrohalogenation.
    • It is a β-elimination reaction, because the H atom is removed from the β-carbon (the carbon next to the one carrying the halogen).
    \[CH_3-CH_2X \xrightarrow[&Delta;]{alc.\ KOH} CH_2=CH_2 + HX\ \ \ (X = Cl,\ Br,\ I)\]
    • Rate depends on the halogen and the alkyl group:
    \[Halogen:\ I > Br > Cl\]
    \[Alkyl\ group:\ tert > sec > primary\]

    3. From Vicinal Dihalides (Dehalogenation)#

    Dihalides with the two halogens on adjacent carbons are called vicinal dihalides. On treatment with zinc metal they lose ZnX2 to form alkenes. This is called dehalogenation.

    \[CH_2Br-CH_2Br + Zn &rarr; CH_2=CH_2 + ZnBr_2\]
    \[CH_3CHBr-CH_2Br + Zn &rarr; CH_3CH=CH_2 + ZnBr_2\]

    4. From Alcohols (Acidic Dehydration)#

    Alcohols, on heating with concentrated sulphuric acid, lose a water molecule to form alkenes. This is also a β-elimination, since the –OH group takes out one H atom from the β-carbon.

    \[CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow[&Delta;]{Conc.\ H_2SO_4} CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O\]

    Physical Properties of Alkenes#

    • Alkenes resemble alkanes in physical properties, except in the types of isomerism and polar nature.
    • The first three members are gases, the next fourteen are liquids and the higher ones are solids.
    • Ethene is a colourless gas with a faint sweet smell. Other alkenes are colourless and odourless.
    • They are insoluble in water but fairly soluble in non-polar solvents like benzene and petroleum ether.
    • Boiling point rises regularly with size: every –CH2 group added increases b.p. by 20–30 K. Straight chain alkenes boil higher than their branched isomers.

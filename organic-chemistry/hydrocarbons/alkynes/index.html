    Hydrocarbons | Alkynes#

    Alkynes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one triple bond between two carbon atoms. They have even fewer hydrogen atoms than alkenes or alkanes.

    \[General\ formula:\ C_nH_{2n-2}\]
    • The first stable member is ethyne, popularly known as acetylene. It is used for arc welding in the form of the oxyacetylene flame (acetylene mixed with oxygen).
    • Alkynes are starting materials for a large number of organic compounds.

    Nomenclature and Isomerism#

    • In the common system, alkynes are named as derivatives of acetylene.
    • In the IUPAC system, the suffix '-ane' of the corresponding alkane is replaced by '-yne'. The position of the triple bond is indicated by the first triply bonded carbon.
    Value of n Formula Structure Common name IUPAC name
    2 C2H2 H–C≡C–H Acetylene Ethyne
    3 C3H4 CH3–C≡CH Methylacetylene Propyne
    4 C4H6 CH3CH2–C≡CH Ethylacetylene But-1-yne
    4 C4H6 CH3–C≡C–CH3 Dimethylacetylene But-2-yne
    • Ethyne and propyne have only one structure each. But-1-yne and but-2-yne differ in the position of the triple bond, so they are position isomers.
    • For C5H8, three structures are possible:

    Isomers of C5H8

    \[I.\ HC \equiv C-CH_2-CH_2-CH_3\ \ \ (Pent\text{-}1\text{-}yne)\]
    \[II.\ H_3C-C \equiv C-CH_2-CH_3\ \ \ (Pent\text{-}2\text{-}yne)\]
    \[III.\ H_3C-CH(CH_3)-C \equiv CH\ \ \ (3\text{-}Methylbut\text{-}1\text{-}yne)\]

    I and II are position isomers. I and III, or II and III, are chain isomers.

    Structure of the Triple Bond#

    • Each carbon atom of ethyne has two sp hybrid orbitals.
    • A C–C σ bond is formed by head-on overlap of one sp orbital from each carbon. The other sp orbital of each carbon overlaps with the 1s orbital of a hydrogen atom to form two C–H σ bonds. The H–C–C bond angle is 180°.
    • Each carbon still has two unhybridised p orbitals, perpendicular to each other and to the C–C axis. These overlap sideways with those of the other carbon to form two π bonds.
    • So ethyne has one C–C σ bond, two C–H σ bonds and two C–C π bonds.
    • The electron cloud between the two carbons is cylindrically symmetrical about the internuclear axis. Thus, ethyne is a linear molecule.
    Bond Bond enthalpy (kJ mol-1) Bond length (pm)
    C≡C 823 120
    C=C 681 133
    C–C 348 154

    Preparation of Alkynes#

    1. From Calcium Carbide#

    On industrial scale, ethyne is prepared by treating calcium carbide with water. Calcium carbide is made by heating quick lime with coke, and quick lime is obtained by heating limestone.

    \[CaCO_3 \xrightarrow{&Delta;} CaO + CO_2\]
    \[CaO + 3C &rarr; CaC_2 + CO\]
    \[CaC_2 + 2H_2O &rarr; Ca(OH)_2 + C_2H_2\]

    2. From Vicinal Dihalides#

    Vicinal dihalides undergo dehydrohalogenation with alcoholic KOH. One molecule of HX is eliminated to give an alkenyl halide, which on treatment with sodamide (NaNH2) gives the alkyne.

    \[CH_2Br-CH_2Br + KOH \xrightarrow{alcohol} CH_2=CHBr + KBr + H_2O\]
    \[CH_2=CHBr + NaNH_2 &rarr; CH \equiv CH + NaBr + NH_3\]

    Physical Properties#

    • Physical properties follow the same trend as alkenes and alkanes.
    • The first three members are gases, the next eight are liquids and the higher ones are solids.
    • All alkynes are colourless. Ethyne has a characteristic odour; other members are odourless.
    • Alkynes are weakly polar, lighter than water and immiscible with it, but soluble in organic solvents like ethers, CCl4 and benzene.
    • Melting point, boiling point and density increase with molar mass.

    Chemical Properties#

    A. Acidic Character of Alkynes#

    Sodium metal and sodamide (NaNH2) are strong bases. They react with ethyne to form sodium acetylide with the liberation of H2. Ethene and ethane do not show such reactions.

    \[HC \equiv CH + Na &rarr; HC \equiv C^-Na^+ + {1 \over 2}H_2\ \ \ (Monosodium\ ethynide)\]
    \[HC \equiv C^-Na^+ + Na &rarr; Na^+\ ^-C \equiv C^-Na^+ + {1 \over 2}H_2\ \ \ (Disodium\ ethynide)\]
    \[CH_3-C \equiv C-H + Na^+NH_2^- &rarr; CH_3-C \equiv C^-Na^+ + NH_3\ \ \ (Sodium\ propynide)\]

    Reason for acidity#

    • In ethyne, the hydrogen atoms are attached to sp hybridised carbon. In ethene they are attached to sp2 and in ethane to sp3 carbon.
    • The sp orbital has the maximum s character (50%), so the sp carbon is the most electronegative. It pulls the shared electron pair of the C–H bond towards itself more strongly than sp2 or sp3 carbon.
    • Hence the H atom of ethyne is released as a proton more easily.
    • Only hydrogens attached to the triply bonded carbon are acidic, not all the hydrogens of an alkyne. So but-1-yne reacts with Na or NaNH2, but but-2-yne does not.

    Order of acidic character

    \[HC \equiv CH > H_2C=CH_2 > CH_3-CH_3\]
    \[HC \equiv CH > CH_3-C \equiv CH >> CH_3-C \equiv C-CH_3\]

    These reactions are used to distinguish alkynes (with terminal H) from alkenes and alkanes.

    B. Addition Reactions#

    Alkynes have a triple bond, so they can add two molecules of H2, halogen, hydrogen halide, etc. Most additions are electrophilic and proceed through a vinylic cation:

    \[-C \equiv C- + H-Z \xrightarrow{H^+} -CH=\overset{+}{C}- + Z^- &rarr; -CH=CZ-\]

    The product depends on the stability of the vinylic cation. In unsymmetrical alkynes, addition follows the Markovnikov rule.

    (i) Addition of Dihydrogen#

    \[HC \equiv CH + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} [H_2C=CH_2] \xrightarrow{H_2} CH_3-CH_3\]
    \[CH_3-C \equiv CH + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} [CH_3-CH=CH_2] \xrightarrow{H_2} CH_3-CH_2-CH_3\]

    (ii) Addition of Halogens#

    \[CH_3-C \equiv CH + Br-Br &rarr; [CH_3CBr=CHBr] \xrightarrow{Br_2} CH_3CBr_2-CHBr_2\]
    \[1,2\text{-}Dibromopropene &rarr; 1,1,2,2\text{-}Tetrabromopropane\]
    • The reddish orange colour of Br2 in CCl4 is decolourised. This is a test for unsaturation.

    (iii) Addition of Hydrogen Halides#

    Two molecules of HCl, HBr or HI add to give gem dihalides (both halogens on the same carbon).

    \[H-C \equiv C-H + H-Br &rarr; [CH_2=CH-Br] &rarr; CH_3CHBr_2\ \ \ (1,1\text{-}Dibromoethane)\]
    \[CH_3-C \equiv CH + H-Br &rarr; [CH_3-CBr=CH_2] &rarr; CH_3-CBr_2-CH_3\ \ \ (2,2\text{-}Dibromopropane)\]

    (iv) Addition of Water#

    Alkynes do not react with water normally. However, on warming with mercuric sulphate and dilute sulphuric acid at 333 K, one molecule of water adds to give an enol, which isomerises to a carbonyl compound.

    \[HC \equiv CH + H-OH \xrightarrow[333\ K]{Hg^{2+}/H^+} [CH_2=CH-OH] \xrightarrow{Isomerisation} CH_3CHO\ \ \ (Ethanal)\]
    \[CH_3-C \equiv CH + H-OH \xrightarrow[333\ K]{Hg^{2+}/H^+} [CH_3-C(OH)=CH_2] \xrightarrow{Isomerisation} CH_3COCH_3\ \ \ (Propanone)\]

    (v) Polymerisation#

    (a) Linear polymerisation: Under suitable conditions, ethyne polymerises to polyacetylene (polyethyne), a high molecular weight polyene with repeating units –(CH=CH–CH=CH)n–. Under special conditions this polymer conducts electricity. Thin films of polyacetylene are used as electrodes in batteries; they are good conductors, lighter and cheaper than metal conductors.

    (b) Cyclic polymerisation: When ethyne is passed through a red hot iron tube at 873 K, three molecules combine to form benzene. This is the best route from aliphatic to aromatic compounds.

    \[3HC \equiv CH \xrightarrow[873\ K]{Red\ hot\ iron\ tube} C_6H_6\]

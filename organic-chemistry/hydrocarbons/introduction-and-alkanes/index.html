    Hydrocarbons | Introduction and Alkanes#

    Hydrocarbons are compounds made up of carbon and hydrogen only. They are the main sources of energy in our daily life.

    • LPG (liquified petroleum gas), CNG (compressed natural gas) and LNG (liquified natural gas) are all hydrocarbon fuels.
    • Petrol, diesel and kerosene are obtained by fractional distillation of petroleum. Coal gas is obtained by destructive distillation of coal.
    • Hydrocarbons are also used to make polymers like polythene, polypropene and polystyrene. Higher hydrocarbons are used as solvents for paints and as starting materials for dyes and drugs.

    Classification of Hydrocarbons#

    Depending upon the type of carbon-carbon bonds present, hydrocarbons are classified into three main categories:

    1. Saturated Hydrocarbons#

    • They contain only carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen single bonds.
    • Open chain saturated hydrocarbons are called alkanes. Example: CH3CH3 (ethane).
    • When the carbon atoms form a closed chain or ring, they are called cycloalkanes. Example: cyclohexane.

    2. Unsaturated Hydrocarbons#

    • They contain carbon-carbon multiple bonds: double bonds, triple bonds or both.
    • Examples: alkenes (CH2=CH2) and alkynes (HC≡CH).

    3. Aromatic Hydrocarbons#

    • These are a special type of cyclic compounds, such as benzene (C6H6) and its derivatives.

    Alkanes#

    Alkanes are saturated open chain hydrocarbons containing carbon-carbon single bonds only.

    • Methane (CH4) is the first member. It is found in coal mines and marshy places.
    • If one H atom of methane is replaced by a –CH3 group, we get ethane (C2H6). Repeating this gives C3H8, C4H10 and so on.
    • Alkanes are quite inert under normal conditions and do not react with acids, bases and other reagents. Hence, they were earlier called paraffins (Latin: parum = little, affinis = affinity).
    • The general formula of the alkane family (homologous series) is:
    \[C_nH_{2n+2}\]

    Where n is the number of carbon atoms.

    Structure of Alkanes#

    • In methane, carbon is sp3 hybridised. The molecule is tetrahedral, with carbon at the centre and four H atoms at the corners of a regular tetrahedron. All H–C–H bond angles are 109.5°.
    • In higher alkanes, such tetrahedra are joined together. C–C and C–H bond lengths are 154 pm and 112 pm respectively.
    • C–C and C–H σ bonds are formed by head-on overlapping of sp3 hybrid orbitals of carbon and 1s orbitals of hydrogen.

    Nomenclature and Isomerism#

    • Methane, ethane and propane have only one possible structure. Higher alkanes can have more than one structure.
    • For C4H10, the four carbon atoms can be joined in a continuous chain or as a branched chain:

    Isomers of C4H10

    \[CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3\ \ \ (Butane\ or\ n\text{-}butane,\ b.p.\ 273\ K)\]
    \[(CH_3)_2CHCH_3\ \ \ (2\text{-}Methylpropane\ or\ isobutane,\ b.p.\ 261\ K)\]

    Isomers of C5H12

    \[CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2CH_3\ \ \ (Pentane\ or\ n\text{-}pentane,\ b.p.\ 309\ K)\]
    \[(CH_3)_2CHCH_2CH_3\ \ \ (2\text{-}Methylbutane\ or\ isopentane,\ b.p.\ 301\ K)\]
    \[C(CH_3)_4\ \ \ (2,2\text{-}Dimethylpropane\ or\ neopentane,\ b.p.\ 282.5\ K)\]
    • Compounds having the same molecular formula but different structures (and hence different properties) are called structural isomers.
    • Structural isomers which differ only in the chain of carbon atoms (straight or branched) are called chain isomers. C4H10 has two and C5H12 has three chain isomers.
    • The number of isomers grows quickly with chain length: C6H14 has 5 isomers, C7H16 has 9, and C10H22 has as many as 75.

    Types of Carbon Atoms#

    Based on the number of carbon atoms attached to a given carbon atom:

    Type of carbon Attached to Example
    Primary (1°) One carbon atom (or none, as in methane) Terminal carbons, both carbons of ethane
    Secondary (2°) Two carbon atoms Middle carbons of n-butane
    Tertiary (3°) Three carbon atoms C-2 of isobutane
    Quaternary (4°) Four carbon atoms C-2 of neopentane

    Alkyl Groups#

    • Groups like –CH3, –C2H5, –C3H7 are derived from alkanes by removal of one hydrogen atom. They are called alkyl groups, with general formula CnH2n+1.

    IUPAC Rules: Some Worked Names#

    The longest carbon chain is chosen as the parent, numbered to give substituents the lowest set of locants, and substituents are written in alphabetical order.

    Structure IUPAC Name Remark
    CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH(C2H5)CH2CH3 4-Ethyl-2-methylhexane Lowest sum and alphabetical arrangement
    CH3CH2CH(C2H5)CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH3 3-Ethyl-5-methylheptane Alphabetical priority order
    Decane with CH(CH3)2 at C-4 and CH(CH3)CH2CH3 at C-5 5-sec-Butyl-4-isopropyldecane sec is ignored in alphabetising; isopropyl is taken as one word
    Nonane with –CH2C(CH3)3 at C-5 5-(2,2-Dimethylpropyl)nonane The side chain is numbered separately

    Writing a Structure from its IUPAC Name#

    To write the structure of 3-ethyl-2,2-dimethylpentane:

    1. Draw the parent chain of five carbon atoms: C–C–C–C–C
    2. Number it: C1–C2–C3–C4–C5
    3. Attach an ethyl group at C-3 and two methyl groups at C-2.
    4. Satisfy the valency of each carbon with hydrogen atoms:
    \[CH_3-C(CH_3)_2-CH(C_2H_5)-CH_2-CH_3\]

    Preparation of Alkanes#

    Petroleum and natural gas are the main sources of alkanes. In the laboratory, they can be prepared by the following methods.

    1. From Unsaturated Hydrocarbons (Hydrogenation)#

    • Dihydrogen adds to alkenes and alkynes in the presence of finely divided Pt, Pd or Ni to form alkanes. This is called hydrogenation.
    • These metals adsorb H2 on their surface and activate the H–H bond. Pt and Pd work at room temperature, while Ni needs relatively higher temperature and pressure.
    \[CH_2=CH_2 + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} CH_3-CH_3\]
    \[CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} CH_3-CH_2-CH_3\]
    \[CH_3-C \equiv CH + 2H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} CH_3-CH_2-CH_3\]

    2. From Alkyl Halides#

    (a) Reduction with zinc and dilute HCl#

    Alkyl halides (except fluorides) are reduced to alkanes by zinc and dilute hydrochloric acid.

    \[CH_3Cl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Zn,\ H^+} CH_4 + HCl\]
    \[C_2H_5Cl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Zn,\ H^+} C_2H_6 + HCl\]
    \[CH_3CH_2CH_2Cl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Zn,\ H^+} CH_3CH_2CH_3 + HCl\]

    (b) Wurtz Reaction#

    • Alkyl halides react with sodium metal in dry ether (free from moisture) to give higher alkanes with double the number of carbon atoms.
    • It is used to prepare higher alkanes containing an even number of carbon atoms.
    \[CH_3Br + 2Na + BrCH_3 \xrightarrow{dry\ ether} CH_3-CH_3 + 2NaBr\]
    \[C_2H_5Br + 2Na + BrC_2H_5 \xrightarrow{dry\ ether} C_2H_5-C_2H_5 + 2NaBr\]

    Note

    If two different alkyl halides are used, a mixture of three alkanes is obtained, which is difficult to separate. That is why the Wurtz reaction is not suitable for alkanes with an odd number of carbon atoms.

    3. From Carboxylic Acids#

    (a) Decarboxylation#

    • Sodium salts of carboxylic acids, on heating with soda lime (NaOH + CaO), give alkanes containing one carbon atom less than the carboxylic acid.
    • This elimination of CO2 from a carboxylic acid is called decarboxylation.
    \[CH_3COO^-Na^+ + NaOH \xrightarrow[&Delta;]{CaO} CH_4 + Na_2CO_3\]

    (b) Kolbe's Electrolytic Method#

    An aqueous solution of the sodium or potassium salt of a carboxylic acid, on electrolysis, gives an alkane with an even number of carbon atoms at the anode.

    \[2CH_3COO^-Na^+ + 2H_2O \xrightarrow{Electrolysis} CH_3-CH_3 + 2CO_2 + H_2 + 2NaOH\]

    The reaction follows a free radical path:

    Mechanism

    \[(i)\ 2CH_3COO^-Na^+ &#8652; 2CH_3COO^- + 2Na^+\]
    \[(ii)\ At\ anode:\ 2CH_3COO^- \xrightarrow{-2e^-} 2CH_3CO\dot{O} &rarr; 2\dot{C}H_3 + 2CO_2 &uarr;\]
    \[(iii)\ \dot{C}H_3 + \dot{C}H_3 &rarr; CH_3-CH_3 &uarr;\]
    \[(iv)\ At\ cathode:\ H_2O + e^- &rarr; OH^- + \dot{H}\ ;\ \ 2\dot{H} &rarr; H_2 &uarr;\]
    • Methane cannot be prepared by this method because two alkyl radicals always join, so the product has at least two carbon atoms.

    Physical Properties of Alkanes#

    • Alkanes are almost non-polar because of the covalent C–C and C–H bonds and the very small electronegativity difference between C and H. They have only weak van der Waals forces.
    • C1 to C4 are gases, C5 to C17 are liquids and C18 onwards are solids at 298 K. They are colourless and odourless.
    • Being non-polar, alkanes are insoluble in water (hydrophobic) but soluble in non-polar solvents (like dissolves like). Petrol is used for dry cleaning because grease (a mixture of higher alkanes) is non-polar.

    Boiling Point#

    • Boiling point increases steadily with molecular mass, because van der Waals forces increase with molecular size or surface area.
    • Among isomers, branching lowers the boiling point. With more branching, the molecule becomes nearly spherical, so the area of contact and hence intermolecular forces are smaller.

    Conclusion

    Among the isomeric pentanes, n-pentane (continuous chain) has the highest boiling point (309.1 K), while the most branched 2,2-dimethylpropane has the lowest (282.5 K).

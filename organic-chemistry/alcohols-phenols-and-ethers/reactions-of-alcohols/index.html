    Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers | Reactions of Alcohols#

    In this article, we will discuss the reactions of alcohols that involve cleavage of the carbon–oxygen (C–O) bond, oxidation of alcohols, and two commercially important alcohols: methanol and ethanol.

    Reactions Involving Cleavage of C–O Bond#

    • Reactions involving cleavage of the C–O bond take place only in alcohols.
    • Phenols show this type of reaction only with zinc (phenol is reduced to benzene on heating with zinc dust). The C–O bond in phenol is too strong because of its partial double bond character.

    1. Reaction with Hydrogen Halides#

    • Alcohols react with hydrogen halides to form alkyl halides.
    \[ROH + HX &rarr; R-X + H_2O\]

    Lucas Test#

    • The three classes of alcohols react with HCl at very different rates. This difference is used to distinguish them (Lucas test).
    • Lucas reagent is a mixture of concentrated HCl and anhydrous ZnCl2.
    • Alcohols are soluble in Lucas reagent, but the alkyl chlorides formed are immiscible and make the solution turbid.
    Alcohol type Observation with Lucas reagent at room temperature
    Tertiary (3°) Turbidity appears immediately (halide forms easily)
    Secondary (2°) Turbidity appears after some time
    Primary (1°) No turbidity at room temperature

    2. Reaction with Phosphorus Trihalides#

    • Alcohols are converted to alkyl bromides by reaction with phosphorus tribromide.
    \[3ROH + PBr_3 &rarr; 3R-Br + H_3PO_3\]

    3. Dehydration#

    • Alcohols lose a molecule of water to form alkenes when treated with a protic acid such as concentrated H2SO4 or H3PO4, or with catalysts such as anhydrous ZnCl2 or alumina.
    \[>CH-C(OH)< \xrightarrow{H^+,\ Heat} >C=C< + H_2O\]
    • Ethanol is dehydrated by heating with concentrated H2SO4 at 443 K.
    \[C_2H_5OH \xrightarrow{H_2SO_4,\ 443\ K} CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O\]
    • Secondary and tertiary alcohols are dehydrated under milder conditions:
    \[CH_3CH(OH)CH_3 \xrightarrow{85\%\ H_3PO_4,\ 440\ K} CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H_2O\]
    \[(CH_3)_3C-OH \xrightarrow{20\%\ H_3PO_4,\ 358\ K} CH_3-C(CH_3)=CH_2 + H_2O\]
    • The relative ease of dehydration is:
    \[Tertiary > Secondary > Primary\]
    • This is because tertiary carbocations are more stable, and hence easier to form, than secondary and primary carbocations.

    Mechanism of dehydration of ethanol#

    • Step 1: Formation of protonated alcohol (ethyl oxonium ion). This step is fast.
    \[CH_3-CH_2-\ddot{O}-H + H^+ \overset{Fast}{&#8652;} CH_3-CH_2-\overset{+}{O}H_2\]
    • Step 2: Formation of carbocation. Water leaves from the protonated alcohol. This is the slowest step and hence the rate determining step.
    \[CH_3-CH_2-\overset{+}{O}H_2 \overset{Slow}{&#8652;} CH_3-\overset{+}{C}H_2 + H_2O\]
    • Step 3: Formation of ethene by elimination of a proton from the carbon next to the positive carbon.
    \[H-CH_2-\overset{+}{C}H_2 &#8652; CH_2=CH_2 + H^+\]
    • The acid used in step 1 is released again in step 3. To drive the equilibrium to the right, ethene is removed as soon as it is formed.

    4. Oxidation#

    • Oxidation of alcohols involves formation of a C=O double bond with cleavage of an O–H bond and a C–H bond (on the carbinol carbon).
    • Since a molecule of dihydrogen is effectively lost, these reactions are also called dehydrogenation reactions.
    \[>C(H)-O-H &rarr; >C=O\ \ (C-H\ and\ O-H\ bonds\ break)\]

    (i) Primary alcohols#

    • A primary alcohol is oxidised to an aldehyde, which is further oxidised to a carboxylic acid.
    \[RCH_2OH \xrightarrow{Oxidation} RCHO \xrightarrow{Oxidation} RCOOH\]
    • Strong oxidising agents such as acidified potassium permanganate give carboxylic acids directly.
    • CrO3 in an anhydrous medium is used to stop the oxidation at the aldehyde stage.
    \[RCH_2OH \xrightarrow{CrO_3} RCHO\]
    • A better reagent for getting aldehydes in good yield is pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC), a complex of CrO3 with pyridine and HCl.
    \[CH_3-CH=CH-CH_2OH \xrightarrow{PCC} CH_3-CH=CH-CHO\]

    (ii) Secondary alcohols#

    • Secondary alcohols are oxidised to ketones by chromic anhydride (CrO3).
    \[R-CH(OH)-R' \xrightarrow{CrO_3} R-CO-R'\]

    (iii) Tertiary alcohols#

    • Tertiary alcohols do not undergo oxidation under normal conditions, as there is no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon.
    • Under strong conditions (strong oxidising agents like KMnO4 and high temperature), various C–C bonds break and a mixture of carboxylic acids with fewer carbon atoms is formed.

    (iv) Dehydrogenation over heated copper#

    • When vapours of a primary or secondary alcohol are passed over heated copper at 573 K, dehydrogenation gives an aldehyde or a ketone. Tertiary alcohols undergo dehydration instead.
    \[RCH_2OH \xrightarrow{Cu,\ 573\ K} RCHO\]
    \[R-CH(OH)-R' \xrightarrow{Cu,\ 573\ K} R-CO-R'\]
    \[(CH_3)_3C-OH \xrightarrow{Cu,\ 573\ K} CH_3-C(CH_3)=CH_2\]

    Biological oxidation of methanol

    In the body, methanol and ethanol are oxidised first to the corresponding aldehyde and then to the acid. Methanol is oxidised to methanal and then to methanoic acid, which can cause blindness and death. Methanol is sometimes present in denatured alcohol, which may be drunk by mistake. A methanol-poisoned patient is treated with intravenous infusions of diluted ethanol: the enzyme that oxidises the aldehyde (HCHO) to acid gets busy with ethanol, giving the kidneys time to excrete methanol.

    Commercially Important Alcohols#

    1. Methanol#

    • Methanol, CH3OH, is also called wood spirit because it was earlier produced by destructive distillation of wood.
    • Today, most methanol is produced by catalytic hydrogenation of carbon monoxide at high pressure and temperature using a ZnO–Cr2O3 catalyst.
    \[CO + 2H_2 \xrightarrow{ZnO-Cr_2O_3,\ 200-300\ atm,\ 573-673\ K} CH_3OH\]
    • Methanol is a colourless liquid with boiling point 337 K.
    • It is highly poisonous. Even small quantities can cause blindness, and large quantities can cause death.
    • It is used as a solvent in paints and varnishes, and chiefly for making formaldehyde.

    2. Ethanol#

    • Ethanol, C2H5OH, is obtained commercially by fermentation of sugars, the oldest method.
    • The sugar in molasses, sugarcane or fruits such as grapes is converted into glucose and fructose (both C6H12O6) by the enzyme invertase.
    • Glucose and fructose are then fermented to ethanol by the enzyme zymase, found in yeast.
    \[C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} + H_2O \xrightarrow{Invertase} C_6H_{12}O_6\ (glucose) + C_6H_{12}O_6\ (fructose)\]
    \[C_6H_{12}O_6 \xrightarrow{Zymase} 2C_2H_5OH + 2CO_2\]
    • In wine making, grapes provide both the sugar and the yeast (which grows on the outer skin of ripening grapes). When grapes are crushed, sugar and enzyme come in contact and fermentation starts.
    • Fermentation takes place in anaerobic conditions (absence of air), and CO2 is released.
    • Zymase stops working once the alcohol content exceeds about 14 percent.
    • If air enters the fermentation mixture, oxygen oxidises ethanol to ethanoic acid, which spoils the taste of alcoholic drinks.
    • Ethanol is a colourless liquid with boiling point 351 K. It is used as a solvent in the paint industry and for preparing many carbon compounds.
    • Denaturation of alcohol: Commercial alcohol is made unfit for drinking by adding some copper sulphate (to give it colour) and pyridine (a foul smelling liquid).
    • Nowadays, large quantities of ethanol are also obtained by the acid catalysed hydration of ethene.
    • Ethanol acts on the central nervous system. Moderate amounts affect judgement and lower inhibitions, higher concentrations cause nausea and loss of consciousness, and still higher amounts interfere with breathing and can be fatal.

    Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids | Reduction, Oxidation and Other Reactions#

    Reduction#

    1. Reduction to alcohols#

    • Aldehydes are reduced to primary alcohols and ketones to secondary alcohols by sodium borohydride (NaBH4), lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH4) or catalytic hydrogenation.
    \[RCHO \xrightarrow{NaBH_4\ or\ LiAlH_4} RCH_2OH\]
    \[R_2CO \xrightarrow{NaBH_4\ or\ LiAlH_4} R_2CHOH\]

    2. Reduction to hydrocarbons#

    The carbonyl group can be reduced completely to a –CH2– group in two ways:

    • Clemmensen reduction: heating with zinc amalgam and concentrated hydrochloric acid.
    • Wolff-Kishner reduction: reaction with hydrazine to form a hydrazone, then heating with KOH or NaOH in a high boiling solvent such as ethylene glycol.
    \[>C=O \xrightarrow{Zn-Hg,\ HCl} >CH_2 + H_2O \qquad (Clemmensen\ reduction)\]
    \[>C=O \xrightarrow[-H_2O]{NH_2NH_2} >C=NNH_2 \xrightarrow[heat]{KOH/ethylene\ glycol} >CH_2 + N_2 \qquad (Wolff-Kishner\ reduction)\]

    Oxidation#

    • Aldehydes are easily oxidised to carboxylic acids by common oxidising agents such as HNO3, KMnO4 and K2Cr2O7. Even mild oxidising agents like Tollens' reagent and Fehling's reagent oxidise them.
    \[RCHO \xrightarrow{[O]} RCOOH\]
    • Ketones are oxidised only under vigorous conditions (strong oxidising agents and high temperature). The C–C bond next to the carbonyl group breaks, giving a mixture of carboxylic acids each having fewer carbon atoms than the ketone.
    \[R-\overset{1}{C}H_2-\overset{2}{C}O-\overset{3}{C}H_2-R' \xrightarrow{[O]} RCOOH + R'CH_2COOH\ \ (C_1-C_2\ cleavage)\]
    \[\qquad \qquad \qquad + \ RCH_2COOH + R'COOH\ \ (C_2-C_3\ cleavage)\]

    The following mild oxidising agents are used to distinguish aldehydes from ketones.

    1. Tollens' test#

    • On warming an aldehyde with freshly prepared ammoniacal silver nitrate solution (Tollens' reagent), a bright silver mirror is formed due to deposition of silver metal.
    • The aldehyde is oxidised to the carboxylate anion. The reaction occurs in alkaline medium.
    \[RCHO + 2[Ag(NH_3)_2]^+ + 3OH^- &rarr; RCOO^- + 2Ag &darr; + 2H_2O + 4NH_3\]

    2. Fehling's test#

    • Fehling's reagent is made by mixing equal amounts of Fehling solution A (aqueous copper sulphate) and Fehling solution B (alkaline sodium potassium tartrate, called Rochelle salt) just before the test.
    • On heating an aldehyde with Fehling's reagent, a reddish brown precipitate of Cu2O is formed.
    • Aromatic aldehydes do not respond to this test.
    \[RCHO + 2Cu^{2+} + 5OH^- &rarr; RCOO^- + Cu_2O &darr; + 3H_2O\]

    3. Haloform reaction#

    • Aldehydes and ketones having at least one methyl group attached to the carbonyl carbon (CH3CO– group, i.e. methyl ketones) are oxidised by sodium hypohalite (NaOX) to the sodium salt of a carboxylic acid having one carbon atom less. The methyl group is converted into a haloform (CHX3).
    • A C=C double bond present in the molecule is not affected.
    \[RCOCH_3 \xrightarrow{NaOX} RCOONa + CHX_3 \qquad (X = Cl,\ Br,\ I)\]
    \[CH_3CH=C(CH_3)COCH_3 \xrightarrow{NaOCl} CH_3CH=C(CH_3)COONa + CHCl_3\]
    • With sodium hypoiodite (I2 + NaOH), yellow iodoform (CHI3) is formed. The iodoform test detects the CH3CO– group, and also the CH3CH(OH)– group, which is oxidised to CH3CO– under the test conditions.

    Reactions due to α-Hydrogen#

    • A hydrogen on the carbon next to the carbonyl group (the α-carbon) is acidic.
    • This acidity is due to the strong electron withdrawing effect of the carbonyl group and the resonance stabilisation of the conjugate base (enolate ion) formed when a base removes the α-hydrogen.
    \[-CO-CH< \ \xrightarrow{:B} \ [-CO-\overset{-}{C}< \ \longleftrightarrow \ -C(O^-)=C<]\]

    1. Aldol condensation#

    • Aldehydes and ketones having at least one α-hydrogen react in the presence of dilute alkali to form β-hydroxy aldehydes (aldol) or β-hydroxy ketones (ketol). This is the aldol reaction. The name aldol comes from the two functional groups in the product: aldehyde and alcohol.
    • The aldol or ketol readily loses water on heating to form an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound. This overall reaction is called aldol condensation.
    • Ketones give ketols, but the reaction is still called aldol condensation because of its similarity to the aldehyde reaction.
    \[2CH_3CHO \overset{dil.\ NaOH}{&#8652;} CH_3CH(OH)CH_2CHO \xrightarrow[-H_2O]{&Delta;} CH_3CH=CHCHO\]
    \[Ethanal \qquad 3-Hydroxybutanal\ (aldol) \qquad But-2-enal\]
    \[2CH_3COCH_3 \overset{Ba(OH)_2}{&#8652;} (CH_3)_2C(OH)CH_2COCH_3 \xrightarrow[-H_2O]{&Delta;} (CH_3)_2C=CHCOCH_3\]
    \[Propanone \qquad 4-Hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one\ (ketol) \qquad 4-Methylpent-3-en-2-one\]

    2. Cross aldol condensation#

    • Aldol condensation between two different aldehydes and/or ketones is called cross aldol condensation.
    • If both of them have α-hydrogens, a mixture of four products is formed. For example, a mixture of ethanal and propanal (1. NaOH, 2. heat) gives:
    Product Formed from
    CH3CH=CHCHO (But-2-enal) Two molecules of ethanal (self aldol)
    CH3CH2CH=C(CH3)CHO (2-Methylpent-2-enal) Two molecules of propanal (self aldol)
    CH3CH=C(CH3)CHO (2-Methylbut-2-enal) Ethanal + propanal (cross aldol)
    CH3CH2CH=CHCHO (Pent-2-enal) Ethanal + propanal (cross aldol)
    • Ketones can also be one component in a cross aldol reaction. Benzaldehyde (no α-hydrogen) and acetophenone give benzalacetophenone as the major product:
    \[C_6H_5CHO + CH_3COC_6H_5 \xrightarrow{OH^-,\ 293\ K} C_6H_5CH=CHCOC_6H_5\]
    \[1,3-Diphenylprop-2-en-1-one\ (Benzalacetophenone)\]

    Other Reactions#

    1. Cannizzaro reaction#

    • Aldehydes which have no α-hydrogen undergo self oxidation and reduction (disproportionation) when treated with concentrated alkali.
    • One molecule of the aldehyde is reduced to an alcohol while another is oxidised to the salt of a carboxylic acid.
    \[2HCHO + conc.\ KOH &rarr; CH_3OH + HCOOK\]
    \[Formaldehyde \qquad Methanol \qquad Potassium\ formate\]
    \[2C_6H_5CHO + conc.\ NaOH &rarr; C_6H_5CH_2OH + C_6H_5COONa\]
    \[Benzaldehyde \qquad Benzyl\ alcohol \qquad Sodium\ benzoate\]

    Conclusion

    • Aldehydes/ketones with α-hydrogen + dilute alkali → aldol condensation.

    • Aldehydes without α-hydrogen (HCHO, C6H5CHO, (CH3)3CCHO) + concentrated alkali → Cannizzaro reaction.

    2. Electrophilic substitution#

    • Aromatic aldehydes and ketones undergo electrophilic substitution on the ring. The carbonyl group is deactivating and meta-directing.
    \[C_6H_5CHO \xrightarrow{HNO_3/H_2SO_4,\ 273-283\ K} m-NO_2C_6H_4CHO\]
    \[Benzaldehyde \qquad \qquad m-Nitrobenzaldehyde\]

    Uses of Aldehydes and Ketones#

    • Aldehydes and ketones are used in the chemical industry as solvents, starting materials and reagents.
    • Formaldehyde: its 40% solution, formalin, is used to preserve biological specimens. It is also used to make bakelite (phenol-formaldehyde resin), urea-formaldehyde glues and other polymers.
    • Acetaldehyde: mainly a starting material for making acetic acid, ethyl acetate, vinyl acetate, polymers and drugs.
    • Benzaldehyde: used in perfumery and in the dye industry.
    • Acetone and ethyl methyl ketone: common industrial solvents.
    • Butyraldehyde, vanillin, acetophenone and camphor are well known for their odours and flavours.

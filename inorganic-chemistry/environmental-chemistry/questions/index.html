    Environmental Chemistry | Questions#

    Short-answer questions with solutions on environmental chemistry covering tropospheric pollution, smog, ozone depletion, water pollution, BOD, pesticides and green chemistry.

    Questions#

    Define environmental chemistry.

    Environmental chemistry is the branch of chemistry which deals with the study of the origin, transport, reactions, effects and fates of chemical species in the environment.

    Explain tropospheric pollution in brief.

    • Tropospheric pollution occurs due to undesirable solid or gaseous particles in the lowest layer of the atmosphere (up to about 10 km), where we live.
    • Gaseous pollutants: oxides of sulphur (SO2), nitrogen (NO, NO2) and carbon (CO, CO2), hydrogen sulphide, hydrocarbons, ozone and other oxidants. They come mainly from burning fossil fuels and automobile exhaust.
    • Particulate pollutants: dust, mist, fumes, smoke and smog.
    • They cause respiratory diseases, eye irritation, damage to plants, acid rain, smog and global warming.

    Carbon monoxide gas is more dangerous than carbon dioxide gas. Why?

    • CO combines with haemoglobin to form carboxyhaemoglobin, which is about 300 times more stable than the oxygen-haemoglobin complex.
    \[Hb + CO &rarr; HbCO\]
    • So haemoglobin can no longer carry oxygen to the organs and tissues. At 3-4 per cent carboxyhaemoglobin in blood, the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood is greatly reduced, causing headache, weak eyesight, nervousness, cardiovascular disorder and even death.
    • CO2 does not bind to haemoglobin in this way. It is harmful mainly by contributing to global warming, not by direct poisoning.

    List the gases which are responsible for the greenhouse effect.

    Carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), water vapour, nitrous oxide (N2O), chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and ozone (O3). CO2 is the major contributor.

    Statues and monuments in India are affected by acid rain. How?

    • SO2 and NO2 released from industries, power plants and domestic fuels get oxidised and react with water to form H2SO4 and HNO3:
    \[2SO_2 + O_2 + 2H_2O &rarr; 2H_2SO_4\]
    \[4NO_2 + O_2 + 2H_2O &rarr; 4HNO_3\]
    • Statues and monuments made of marble (CaCO3) react with these acids:
    \[CaCO_3 + H_2SO_4 &rarr; CaSO_4 + H_2O + CO_2\]
    • The marble slowly gets corroded, discoloured and lustreless. The Taj Mahal at Agra is a well-known example.

    What is smog? How is classical smog different from photochemical smog?

    Smog is a mixture of smoke and fog. It is the most common form of air pollution in cities.

    Classical smog Photochemical smog
    Occurs in cool, humid climate Occurs in warm, dry and sunny climate
    Mixture of smoke, fog and SO2 Contains O3, NO, acrolein, formaldehyde and PAN
    Reducing in nature (reducing smog) Oxidising in nature (oxidising smog)

    Write down the reactions involved during the formation of photochemical smog.

    \[NO_{2(g)} \xrightarrow{h\nu} NO_{(g)} + O_{(g)}\]
    \[O_{(g)} + O_{2(g)} &#8652; O_{3(g)}\]
    \[NO_{(g)} + O_{3(g)} &rarr; NO_{2(g)} + O_{2(g)}\]

    O3 and NO2 then oxidise unburnt hydrocarbons to formaldehyde, acrolein and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN):

    \[3CH_4 + 2O_3 &rarr; 3HCHO + 3H_2O\]

    What are the harmful effects of photochemical smog and how can they be controlled?

    Harmful effects:

    • Ozone and PAN are powerful eye irritants.
    • Ozone and NO irritate the nose and throat; high concentrations cause headache, chest pain, dryness of throat, cough and difficulty in breathing.
    • It cracks rubber, damages plant life and corrodes metals, stones, building materials and painted surfaces.

    Control:

    • Control the primary precursors (NO2 and hydrocarbons) using catalytic converters in automobiles; the secondary precursors (O3 and PAN) then automatically decrease.
    • Plant trees such as Pinus, Juniparus, Quercus, Pyrus and Vitis, which can metabolise nitrogen oxide.

    What are the reactions involved in ozone layer depletion in the stratosphere?

    CFCs are broken down by UV radiation to give chlorine radicals, which destroy ozone in a chain reaction:

    \[CF_2Cl_{2(g)} \xrightarrow{UV} \dot{Cl}_{(g)} + \dot{C}F_2Cl_{(g)}\]
    \[\dot{Cl}_{(g)} + O_{3(g)} &rarr; Cl\dot{O}_{(g)} + O_{2(g)}\]
    \[Cl\dot{O}_{(g)} + O_{(g)} &rarr; \dot{Cl}_{(g)} + O_{2(g)}\]

    The chlorine radical is regenerated, so one CFC molecule can destroy many ozone molecules.

    What do you mean by ozone hole? What are its consequences?

    • The depletion of the ozone layer over the South Pole (Antarctica), first reported in the 1980s, is called the ozone hole. It forms because polar stratospheric clouds in winter convert chlorine sinks (ClONO2, HCl) into HOCl and Cl2, which are photolysed in spring to give chlorine radicals.
    • Consequences: More UV radiation reaches the troposphere, causing ageing of skin, cataract, sunburn, skin cancer, death of phytoplanktons, reduced fish productivity, harmful mutation of cells, loss of soil moisture through increased evaporation from leaves, and fading of paints and fibres.

    What are the major causes of water pollution? Explain.

    • Pathogens: bacteria and other organisms from domestic sewage and animal excreta, e.g., Escherichia coli and Streptococcus faecalis, which cause gastrointestinal diseases.
    • Organic wastes: leaves, grass, trash and excess phytoplankton. Their decomposition by bacteria uses up dissolved oxygen, killing aquatic life.
    • Chemical pollutants: heavy metals (Cd, Hg, Ni), acids from mine drainage, salts, petroleum products, pesticides, PCBs, detergents and fertilizers (phosphates cause eutrophication).

    What do you mean by Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD)?

    • The amount of oxygen required by bacteria to break down the organic matter present in a certain volume of a water sample is called BOD.
    • It measures the amount of organic matter in water. Clean water has BOD less than 5 ppm, while highly polluted water has BOD of 17 ppm or more.

    What are pesticides and herbicides? Explain giving examples.

    • Pesticides are synthetic toxic chemicals used to kill insects, rodents and other pests that damage crops. Examples: DDT, Aldrin, Dieldrin (organo-chlorides), and organo-phosphates and carbamates. Organo-chlorides are non-biodegradable and accumulate in the food chain.
    • Herbicides are chemicals used to kill weeds. Examples: sodium chlorate (NaClO3) and sodium arsinite (Na3AsO3). They are toxic to mammals but decompose within a few months.

    What do you mean by green chemistry? How will it help decrease environmental pollution?

    • Green chemistry is a way of thinking that uses existing knowledge and principles of chemistry and other sciences to reduce the adverse impact on the environment.
    • It helps by: choosing starting materials that give nearly 100% yield of useful products, so little by-product waste forms; using water instead of toxic organic solvents; replacing harmful reagents (e.g., liquid CO2 instead of tetrachloroethene in dry cleaning, H2O2 instead of Cl2 in bleaching paper); and reducing material and energy consumption.

    What would have happened if the greenhouse gases were totally missing in the earth's atmosphere? Discuss.

    • Greenhouse gases trap part of the heat radiated by the earth's surface and keep the earth warm (natural greenhouse effect).
    • Without them, all this heat would escape into space. The earth's surface would become extremely cold, and life as we know it, including plants and animals, could not survive.

    A large number of fish are suddenly found floating dead on a lake. There is no evidence of toxic dumping but you find an abundance of phytoplankton. Suggest a reason for the fish kill.

    • The abundant phytoplankton indicates eutrophication, caused by nutrients such as phosphates from fertilizers or detergents.
    • When the phytoplankton die, bacteria decompose them and use up the dissolved oxygen. The algal bloom also covers the surface, reducing oxygen in water.
    • When DO falls well below 6 ppm, fish cannot survive and die of oxygen deficiency.

    How can domestic waste be used as manure?

    • Domestic waste is separated into biodegradable (vegetable peels, leftover food, leaves etc.) and non-biodegradable (plastic, glass, metal) parts.
    • The biodegradable part is put in a compost pit or land fill, where micro-organisms decompose it into compost, which is used as manure. Non-biodegradable materials are sent for recycling.

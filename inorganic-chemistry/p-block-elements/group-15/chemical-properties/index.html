    Group 15 Elements | Chemical Properties#

    In this article, we will discuss chemical properties of group 15 elements like oxidation states, anomalous behaviour of nitrogen and reactivity towards hydrogen, oxygen, halogens and metals.

    1. Oxidation States and Trends in Chemical Reactivity#

    • The common oxidation states of group 15 elements are -3, +3 and +5.
    • The tendency to show -3 oxidation state decreases down the group due to increase in size and metallic character. Bismuth hardly forms any compound in -3 oxidation state.
    • The stability of +5 oxidation state decreases down the group, while that of +3 oxidation state increases. This is due to the inert pair effect (reluctance of the ns2 electrons to take part in bonding).
    • The only well characterised Bi(V) compound is BiF5.
    • Nitrogen also shows +1, +2 and +4 oxidation states when it combines with oxygen. Phosphorus shows +1 and +4 oxidation states in some oxoacids.

    Disproportionation#

    • In the case of nitrogen, all oxidation states from +1 to +4 tend to disproportionate in acid solution. For example:
    \[3HNO_2 &rarr; HNO_3 + H_2O + 2NO\]
    • In the case of phosphorus, nearly all intermediate oxidation states disproportionate into +5 and -3, both in alkali and in acid.
    • The +3 oxidation state of arsenic, antimony and bismuth becomes increasingly stable with respect to disproportionation.

    Covalency#

    • Nitrogen is restricted to a maximum covalency of 4, because only four orbitals (one s and three p) are available for bonding.
    • The heavier elements have vacant d orbitals in the outermost shell, so they can expand their covalence, as in PF6-.

    2. Anomalous Properties of Nitrogen#

    Nitrogen differs from the rest of the members of the group due to its small size, high electronegativity, high ionisation enthalpy and non-availability of d orbitals.

    • pπ-pπ multiple bonding: Nitrogen can form pπ-pπ multiple bonds with itself and with other small, highly electronegative atoms like C and O. Heavier elements cannot do this because their atomic orbitals are too large and diffuse for effective sideways overlap.
    • Diatomic molecule: Because of this, nitrogen exists as a diatomic molecule with a triple bond (one σ and two π) between the two atoms. Its bond enthalpy is very high (941.4 kJ mol-1).
    • On the other hand, phosphorus, arsenic and antimony form single bonds (P-P, As-As, Sb-Sb), while bismuth forms metallic bonds in the elemental state.
    • Weak catenation: The single N-N bond is weaker than the single P-P bond. Owing to the small bond length, the non-bonding electrons on the two nitrogen atoms repel each other strongly. Hence, the catenation tendency of nitrogen is weaker.
    • No dπ-pπ bonding: Since nitrogen has no d orbitals, it cannot form dπ-pπ bonds as heavier elements do, for example in R3P=O or R3P=CH2 (R = alkyl group).
    • Phosphorus and arsenic can also form dπ-dπ bonds with transition metals when their compounds like P(C2H5)3 and As(C6H5)3 act as ligands.
    • Maximum covalency of 4: Nitrogen cannot expand its octet.

    3. Reactivity towards Hydrogen#

    • All group 15 elements form hydrides of the type EH3 (E = N, P, As, Sb, Bi).
    • The hydrides show a regular gradation in their properties.
    Property NH3 PH3 AsH3 SbH3 BiH3
    Melting point (K) 195.2 139.5 156.7 185 -
    Boiling point (K) 238.5 185.5 210.6 254.6 290
    E-H distance (pm) 101.7 141.9 151.9 170.7 -
    HEH angle (°) 107.8 93.6 91.8 91.3 -
    ΔfH0 (kJ mol-1) -46.1 13.4 66.4 145.1 278
    ΔdissH0 (E-H) (kJ mol-1) 389 322 297 255 -
    • Thermal stability decreases from NH3 to BiH3, as seen from the decreasing E-H bond dissociation enthalpy.
    • Reducing character therefore increases down the group. Ammonia is only a mild reducing agent while BiH3 is the strongest reducing agent amongst all the hydrides.
    • Basicity decreases down the group:
    \[Stability:\ NH_3\ >\ PH_3\ >\ AsH_3\ >\ SbH_3\ >\ BiH_3\]
    \[Reducing\ character:\ NH_3\ <\ PH_3\ <\ AsH_3\ <\ SbH_3\ <\ BiH_3\]
    \[Basicity:\ NH_3\ >\ PH_3\ >\ AsH_3\ >\ SbH_3\ \geq\ BiH_3\]
    • NH3 has an unusually high melting and boiling point because its molecules are associated through hydrogen bonding.

    4. Reactivity towards Oxygen#

    • All these elements form two types of oxides: E2O3 and E2O5.
    • The oxide in the higher oxidation state of an element is more acidic than that in the lower oxidation state.
    • Acidic character of the oxides decreases down the group.
    • The E2O3 oxides of nitrogen and phosphorus are purely acidic, those of arsenic and antimony are amphoteric and those of bismuth are predominantly basic.

    5. Reactivity towards Halogens#

    • These elements react with halogens to form two series of halides: EX3 and EX5.
    • Nitrogen does not form pentahalides because it has no d orbitals in its valence shell.
    • Pentahalides are more covalent than trihalides.
    • All the trihalides are stable except those of nitrogen. In the case of nitrogen, only NF3 is known to be stable.
    • Trihalides, except BiF3, are predominantly covalent in nature.

    6. Reactivity towards Metals#

    • All these elements react with metals to form binary compounds in which they show -3 oxidation state. For example:
    Compound Name
    Ca3N2 Calcium nitride
    Ca3P2 Calcium phosphide
    Na3As2 Sodium arsenide
    Zn3Sb2 Zinc antimonide
    Mg3Bi2 Magnesium bismuthide

    Questions#

    Though nitrogen exhibits +5 oxidation state, it does not form pentahalide. Give reason.

    Nitrogen (n = 2) has only s and p orbitals in its valence shell. It has no d orbitals, so it cannot expand its covalence beyond four. That is why nitrogen does not form a pentahalide.

    PH3 has lower boiling point than NH3. Why?

    In the liquid state, NH3 molecules are associated through hydrogen bonding, but PH3 molecules are not (phosphorus is much less electronegative than nitrogen). Extra energy is needed to break the hydrogen bonds in NH3, so PH3 has a lower boiling point.

    Why are pentahalides more covalent than trihalides?

    In pentahalides the central atom is in the +5 oxidation state, while in trihalides it is in the +3 state. An atom in a higher oxidation state has a higher charge and smaller size, so it has greater polarising power. It distorts the electron cloud of the halide more strongly, which increases covalent character. Hence pentahalides are more covalent than trihalides.

    Why is BiH3 the strongest reducing agent amongst all the hydrides of group 15 elements?

    Down the group, the size of the central atom increases and the E-H bond becomes longer and weaker. BiH3 has the weakest E-H bond, so it is the least stable hydride and gives up hydrogen most easily. Therefore, it is the strongest reducing agent.

    Why does nitrogen exist as N2 while phosphorus exists as P4?

    Nitrogen is small and can form strong pπ-pπ multiple bonds, so two nitrogen atoms join by a triple bond to give N2. Phosphorus atoms are larger and their p orbitals are too diffuse for effective pπ-pπ overlap, so phosphorus forms P-P single bonds instead, giving the tetrahedral P4 molecule.

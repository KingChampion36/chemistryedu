    Group 14 Elements | Chemical Properties#

    In this article, we will discuss chemical properties of group 14 elements like oxidation states, reactivity towards oxygen, water and halogens, and the anomalous behaviour of carbon.

    1. Oxidation States and Trends in Chemical Reactivity#

    • Group 14 elements have four electrons in the outermost shell. Their common oxidation states are +4 and +2. Carbon also exhibits negative oxidation states.
    • The sum of the first four ionization enthalpies is very high, so compounds in the +4 oxidation state are generally covalent.
    • In the heavier members, the tendency to show the +2 oxidation state increases in the order:
    \[Ge\ <\ Sn\ <\ Pb\]
    • This is due to the inability of the ns2 electrons of the valence shell to participate in bonding (inert pair effect).

    Relative stability of +4 and +2 states#

    Element Behaviour
    C, Si Mostly show the +4 oxidation state
    Ge Forms stable compounds in the +4 state and only a few in the +2 state
    Sn Forms compounds in both states; Sn(II) is a reducing agent
    Pb Pb(II) compounds are stable; Pb(IV) compounds are strong oxidising agents

    Electron precise molecules and expansion of covalence#

    • In the tetravalent state, the central atom (e.g., carbon in CCl4) has eight electrons around it. Such electron precise molecules are not normally expected to act as electron acceptors or donors.
    • Carbon cannot exceed a covalence of 4. The other elements of the group can, because they have d orbitals.
    • Due to this, their halides undergo hydrolysis and tend to form complexes by accepting electron pairs from donor species. For example, in SiF62-, [GeCl6]2- and [Sn(OH)6]2-, the central atom is sp3d2 hybridised.

    2. Reactivity Towards Oxygen#

    • All members form oxides when heated in oxygen. There are two main types: monoxides (MO) and dioxides (MO2). SiO exists only at high temperature.
    • Oxides in higher oxidation states are generally more acidic than those in lower oxidation states.
    Type Acidic Amphoteric Neutral
    Dioxides CO2, SiO2, GeO2 SnO2, PbO2 -
    Monoxides GeO SnO, PbO CO

    3. Reactivity Towards Water#

    • Carbon, silicon and germanium are not affected by water.
    • Tin decomposes steam to form the dioxide and dihydrogen:
    \[Sn + 2H_2O \xrightarrow{\Delta} SnO_2 + 2H_2\]
    • Lead is unaffected by water, probably because of a protective oxide film on its surface.

    4. Reactivity Towards Halogens#

    • These elements form halides of formula MX2 and MX4 (X = F, Cl, Br, I).
    • Except carbon, all members react directly with halogens under suitable conditions.
    • Most MX4 are covalent. The central atom is sp3 hybridised and the molecule is tetrahedral. SnF4 and PbF4 are exceptions; they are ionic.
    • PbI4 does not exist, because the energy released in forming Pb-I bonds is not enough to unpair the 6s2 electrons and excite one of them to a higher orbital to give four unpaired electrons.
    • The heavier members Ge to Pb also form dihalides, MX2. The stability of dihalides increases down the group. Considering thermal and chemical stability, GeX4 is more stable than GeX2, whereas PbX2 is more stable than PbX4.

    Hydrolysis of tetrachlorides#

    • Except CCl4, all tetrachlorides are easily hydrolysed by water, because the central atom can accommodate a lone pair from the oxygen of water in its vacant d orbital.
    • For example, SiCl4 first accepts a lone pair from a water molecule in a d orbital of Si and loses HCl. Repetition of this step finally gives silicic acid, Si(OH)4:
    \[SiCl_4 + H_2O &rarr; SiCl_3(OH) + HCl\]
    \[SiCl_3(OH) + 3H_2O &rarr; Si(OH)_4 + 3HCl\]
    \[Overall:\ SiCl_4 + 4H_2O &rarr; Si(OH)_4 + 4HCl\]
    • CCl4 is not hydrolysed because carbon has no d orbitals to accept the lone pair.

    5. Important Trends and Anomalous Behaviour of Carbon#

    Like the first member of other groups, carbon differs from the rest of its group. This is due to its smaller size, higher electronegativity, higher ionisation enthalpy and the unavailability of d orbitals.

    1. Maximum covalence of four#

    • Carbon has only s and p orbitals available for bonding, so it can accommodate only four pairs of electrons. Its maximum covalence is four, whereas the other members can expand their covalence due to d orbitals.

    2. pπ-pπ multiple bonding#

    • Carbon has a unique ability to form pπ-pπ multiple bonds with itself and with other atoms of small size and high electronegativity. Examples: C=C, C≡C, C=O, C=S and C≡N.
    • Heavier elements do not form pπ-pπ bonds because their atomic orbitals are too large and diffuse for effective overlap.

    3. Catenation#

    • The tendency of atoms of an element to link with one another through covalent bonds to form chains and rings is called catenation.
    • Carbon shows catenation to the greatest extent because C-C bonds are very strong.
    • Down the group, size increases and electronegativity decreases, so the tendency to catenate decreases. Lead does not show catenation.
    \[Order\ of\ catenation:\ C >> Si > Ge \approx Sn\]
    Bond Bond enthalpy / kJ mol-1
    C-C 348
    Si-Si 297
    Ge-Ge 260
    Sn-Sn 240
    • Due to catenation and pπ-pπ bond formation, carbon is able to show allotropic forms.

    Questions#

    Select the member(s) of group 14 that
    (i) forms the most acidic dioxide,
    (ii) is commonly found in +2 oxidation state,
    (iii) used as semiconductor.

    (i) Carbon (CO2)

    (ii) Lead

    (iii) Silicon and germanium

    [SiF6]2- is known whereas [SiCl6]2- not. Give possible reasons.

    The main reasons are:

    • Six large chloride ions cannot be accommodated around the small Si4+ ion, whereas six small fluoride ions can.
    • The interaction between a lone pair of the chloride ion and Si4+ is not very strong.

    Discuss the pattern of variation in the oxidation states from C to Pb.

    • All members have the configuration ns2np2 and show +4 and +2 oxidation states. Carbon also shows negative oxidation states (e.g., -4 in CH4).
    • C and Si show mainly +4. Going down the group, the inert pair effect makes the +2 state more stable in the order Ge < Sn < Pb.
    • Thus, Ge(IV) is more stable than Ge(II), Sn shows both states (Sn2+ is reducing), and Pb(II) is more stable than Pb(IV) (Pb4+ is oxidising).

    Rationalise the given statements and give chemical reactions:
    (i) Lead(II) chloride reacts with Cl2 to give PbCl4.
    (ii) Lead(IV) chloride is highly unstable towards heat.
    (iii) Lead is known not to form an iodide, PbI4.

    (i) Because of the inert pair effect, Pb(II) is more stable than Pb(IV), so PbCl2 reacts with Cl2 only with difficulty. PbCl4 formed is unstable:

    \[PbCl_2 + Cl_2 &rarr; PbCl_4\]

    (ii) Pb(IV) tends to change to the more stable Pb(II). On heating, PbCl4 decomposes:

    \[PbCl_4 \xrightarrow{\Delta} PbCl_2 + Cl_2\]

    (iii) Pb4+ is a strong oxidising agent and I- is a strong reducing agent, so they cannot coexist. Also, the Pb-I bond formed does not release enough energy to unpair the 6s2 electrons. Hence PbI4 does not exist.

    What do you understand by catenation?

    Catenation is the ability of atoms of an element to link with one another through covalent bonds to form long chains or rings. Carbon shows it to the maximum extent because of its strong C-C bonds (348 kJ mol-1). The tendency decreases down the group: C >> Si > Ge ≈ Sn, and Pb does not show catenation.

    Elements of group 14
    (a) exhibit oxidation state of +4 only
    (b) exhibit oxidation state of +2 and +4
    (c) form M2- and M4+ ion
    (d) form M2+ and M4+ ions

    (b) exhibit oxidation state of +2 and +4.

    Group 13 Elements | Physical and Atomic Properties#

    Introduction to p-Block Elements#

    • In p-block elements, the last electron enters the outermost p orbital. Since a set of p orbitals can hold a maximum of six electrons, there are six groups of p-block elements, numbered 13 to 18.
    • Boron, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine and helium head these groups.
    • The general valence shell electronic configuration of p-block elements is ns2np1-6 (except helium, which is 1s2).
    • The inner core of the electronic configuration differs from element to element. This difference strongly influences both physical properties (atomic and ionic radii, ionisation enthalpy, etc.) and chemical properties.

    Oxidation states of p-block elements#

    • The maximum oxidation state shown by a p-block element is equal to its total number of valence electrons (sum of s and p electrons). This is called the group oxidation state.
    • Many p-block elements also show oxidation states which differ from the group oxidation state by two units.
    • In the boron, carbon and nitrogen families, the group oxidation state is the most stable state for the lighter elements. For the heavier elements, the oxidation state two units less than the group oxidation state becomes progressively more stable. This is attributed to the inert pair effect.

    Metallic and non-metallic character#

    • Non-metals and metalloids exist only in the p-block. Non-metallic character decreases down a group; the heaviest element of each p-block group is the most metallic.
    • Non-metals have higher ionisation enthalpies and electronegativities than metals. So, non-metals readily form anions while metals form cations.
    • Compounds of highly reactive non-metals with highly reactive metals are generally ionic, whereas compounds formed between non-metals are largely covalent.
    • Non-metal oxides are acidic or neutral, whereas metal oxides are basic.

    How the first member differs#

    The first member of each p-block group differs from the rest of its group in two major ways:

    • Size: It is much smaller, so all size-dependent properties differ.
    • Absence of d orbitals: Second period elements have no d orbitals, so their maximum covalence is four (using one 2s and three 2p orbitals). Third period elements (3s23pn) have vacant 3d orbitals and can expand their covalence above four. For example, boron forms only [BF4]-, whereas aluminium forms [AlF6]3-.
    • pπ-pπ bonding: The first member can form strong pπ-pπ multiple bonds with itself (C=C, C≡C, N≡N) and with other second period elements (C=O, C=N, C≡N, N=O). Heavier elements form π bonds using d orbitals (dπ-pπ or dπ-dπ), which are weaker.
    • Heavier elements can have higher coordination numbers in the same oxidation state. For example, in the +5 state, N forms NO3- (three-coordinate) while P forms PO43- (four-coordinate).

    Introduction to Group 13 Elements#

    • The elements of group 13 are Boron(B), Aluminium(Al), Gallium(Ga), Indium(In) and Thallium(Tl). This group is known as the boron family.
    • The group shows a wide variation in properties. Boron is a typical non-metal, aluminium is a metal with many chemical similarities to boron, and gallium, indium and thallium are almost exclusively metallic.

    Occurrence#

    • Boron is a fairly rare element (less than 0.0001% by mass of the earth's crust). It mainly occurs as orthoboric acid (H3BO3), borax (Na2B4O7.10H2O) and kernite (Na2B4O7.4H2O).
    • In India, borax occurs in Puga Valley (Ladakh) and Sambhar Lake (Rajasthan).
    • Boron has two isotopes: 10B (19%) and 11B (81%).
    • Aluminium is the most abundant metal and the third most abundant element in the earth's crust (8.3% by mass), after oxygen (45.5%) and silicon (27.7%).
    • Bauxite (Al2O3.2H2O) and cryolite (Na3AlF6) are the important minerals of aluminium.
    • Gallium, indium and thallium are less abundant in nature.

    Atomic Properties of Group 13 Elements#

    1. Electronic Configuration#

    • General valence shell electronic configuration: ns2np1
    • Boron and aluminium have a noble gas core. Gallium and indium have a noble gas core plus 10 d-electrons, and thallium has a noble gas core plus 14 f-electrons and 10 d-electrons.
    • These more complex inner cores affect the properties of the heavier members.

    2. Atomic Radii#

    • Atomic radius is expected to increase down the group as one extra shell of electrons is added at each step.
    • Exception: The atomic radius of Ga (135 pm) is less than that of Al (143 pm).

    Reason

    Gallium has 10 additional d-electrons in its inner core. d-electrons have poor screening effect, so the outer electrons of gallium experience a larger effective nuclear charge. As a result, the atomic radius of gallium is smaller than that of aluminium.

    3. Ionization Enthalpy#

    • Ionization enthalpy does not decrease smoothly down the group.
    • The decrease from B to Al is due to increase in atomic size.
    • The discontinuity between Al and Ga, and between In and Tl, is due to the poor screening effect of d- and f-electrons, which fail to compensate for the increase in nuclear charge.
    • As expected, \(Δ_iH_1 < Δ_iH_2 < Δ_iH_3\). The sum of the first three ionization enthalpies is very high for every element of the group.

    4. Electronegativity#

    • Down the group, electronegativity first decreases from B to Al and then increases marginally. This is due to the irregular variation in atomic size of these elements.

    Physical Properties of Group 13 Elements#

    • Boron is non-metallic. It is an extremely hard, black coloured solid and exists in many allotropic forms.
    • Boron has an unusually high melting point due to its very strong crystalline lattice.
    • The rest of the members are soft metals with low melting points and high electrical conductivity.
    • Gallium has an unusually low melting point (303 K) and can exist as a liquid in summer. Its high boiling point (2676 K) makes it useful for measuring high temperatures.
    • Density increases down the group from boron to thallium.

    Questions#

    How would you explain the lower atomic radius of Ga as compared to Al?

    Gallium has 10 electrons in its inner 3d subshell, which aluminium does not have. d-electrons shield the outer electrons poorly from the nuclear charge. So the valence electrons of gallium feel a higher effective nuclear charge and are pulled closer to the nucleus. Hence the atomic radius of Ga (135 pm) is less than that of Al (143 pm).

    Why does gallium, a metal, remain liquid on a hot summer day, and why is it used in high temperature thermometers?

    Gallium has an unusually low melting point of 303 K, so it melts at summer temperatures. Its boiling point is very high (2676 K), so it stays liquid over a very wide range of temperature. This makes it useful for measuring high temperatures.

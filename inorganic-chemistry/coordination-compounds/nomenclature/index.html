    Coordination Compounds | IUPAC Nomenclature#

    The formulas and names of coordination entities are based on the recommendations of the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC). A systematic method is needed so that every formula and name describes exactly one compound, which is especially important when dealing with isomers.

    Writing Formulas of Mononuclear Coordination Entities#

    Mononuclear coordination entities contain a single central metal atom. The following rules are used while writing their formulas:

    1. The central atom is listed first.
    2. The ligands are then listed in alphabetical order. The placement of a ligand in the list does not depend on its charge.
    3. Polydentate ligands are also listed alphabetically. For an abbreviated ligand, the first letter of the abbreviation decides its position.
    4. The formula of the entire coordination entity, charged or not, is enclosed in square brackets. Polyatomic ligands and ligand abbreviations are enclosed in parentheses.
    5. There is no space between the ligands and the metal within a coordination sphere.
    6. When the formula of a charged coordination entity is written without the counter ion, the charge is shown outside the square bracket as a right superscript, with the number before the sign. Example: [Co(CN)6]3-, [Cr(H2O)6]3+.
    7. The charge of the cation(s) is balanced by the charge of the anion(s).

    Naming Mononuclear Coordination Compounds#

    Names are built using additive nomenclature: the groups around the central atom are written as prefixes to the name of the central atom, with suitable multipliers. The rules are:

    1. The cation is named first, whether the complex part is the cation or the anion.
    2. The ligands are named in alphabetical order before the name of the central atom (this is the reverse of writing the formula). Multiplying prefixes (di, tri, etc.) are ignored while alphabetising.
    3. Names of anionic ligands end in -o (the 2004 IUPAC recommendation uses -ido, so chloro becomes chlorido). Neutral and cationic ligands keep their usual names, except aqua for H2O, ammine for NH3, carbonyl for CO and nitrosyl for NO.
    4. Prefixes mono, di, tri, tetra, etc. show the number of each ligand. If the ligand name already contains a numerical prefix, the terms bis, tris, tetrakis are used and the ligand name is put in parentheses. Example: [NiCl2(PPh3)2] is dichloridobis(triphenylphosphine)nickel(II).
    5. The oxidation state of the metal is shown by a Roman numeral in parentheses after the metal name.
    6. If the complex ion is a cation, the metal is named as the element (Co - cobalt, Pt - platinum). If the complex ion is an anion, the metal name ends in -ate (Co in [Co(SCN)4]2- is cobaltate). For some metals, the Latin names are used in anions.
    7. A neutral complex molecule is named like a complex cation.
    8. The number of cations and anions in an ionic compound is never stated in the name.

    Worked Examples#

    1. [Cr(NH3)3(H2O)3]Cl3

    Name: triamminetriaquachromium(III) chloride

    • The complex ion inside the square bracket is a cation.

    • The ammine ligands are named before aqua ligands (alphabetical order).

    • There are three Cl- counter ions, so the complex ion has a charge of +3. All the ligands are neutral, so the oxidation number of Cr is +3.

    2. [Co(H2NCH2CH2NH2)3]2(SO4)3

    Name: tris(ethane-1,2-diamine)cobalt(III) sulphate

    • Three sulphate ions (total charge -6) balance two complex cations, so each complex cation has a charge of +3.

    • Ethane-1,2-diamine is neutral, so the oxidation number of Co is +3.

    • 'tris' is used because the ligand name already contains the prefix 'di'.

    3. [Ag(NH3)2][Ag(CN)2]

    Name: diamminesilver(I) dicyanoargentate(I)

    • Both the cation and the anion are complex ions. The cation is named first.

    • Silver in the anion is named argentate. Notice how the name of the metal differs in the cation and the anion even though both contain Ag+.

    Steps to Name a Complex#

    Steps

    1. Identify the cation and the anion; decide which one is the complex.

    2. Find the oxidation number of the metal from the charges.

    3. List ligand names alphabetically with multiplying prefixes.

    4. Add the metal name (with -ate if the complex is an anion) and the oxidation number in Roman numerals.

    5. Name the counter ion separately (cation first, then anion).

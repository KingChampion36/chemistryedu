# [![ChemistryEdu Logo](../../images/favicon.svg){ align=left, width=3.8% }](../../index.md)  Solid State | Introduction

Solid state is the state of matter in which the constituent particles (atoms, molecules or ions) have fixed positions and can only oscillate about their mean positions. This is why solids have a definite mass, volume and shape.

* Liquids and gases are called **fluids** because their particles are free to move about and they can flow.
* In solids, particles are held at fixed positions, which explains their rigidity.
* The properties of a solid depend on the nature of its constituent particles and the binding forces between them. This is why the study of the structure of solids is important for designing new materials like superconductors, magnetic materials and biodegradable polymers.

## General Characteristics of Solid State

* Whether a substance is a solid, liquid or gas at a given temperature and pressure depends on the balance between two opposing factors:
    * **Intermolecular forces** try to keep the particles close together.
    * **Thermal energy** tries to keep them apart by making them move faster.
* At sufficiently low temperature, the thermal energy is low and the intermolecular forces bring the particles so close that they occupy fixed positions. The substance then exists as a solid.

The characteristic properties of the solid state are:

1. They have definite mass, volume and shape.
2. Intermolecular distances are short.
3. Intermolecular forces are strong.
4. Their constituent particles have fixed positions and can only oscillate about their mean positions.
5. They are incompressible and rigid.

## Amorphous and Crystalline Solids

On the basis of the order present in the arrangement of their particles, solids are classified as **crystalline** or **amorphous**.

### 1. Crystalline Solids

* A crystalline solid usually consists of a large number of small crystals, each having a definite characteristic geometrical shape.
* The arrangement of particles is ordered and has **long range order**, i.e. a regular pattern of arrangement repeats itself periodically over the entire crystal.
* Examples: Sodium chloride, quartz, metals like iron and copper, sulphur, iodine, naphthalene.
* They have a **sharp melting point**.
* They are **anisotropic**: physical properties like electrical resistance or refractive index have different values when measured along different directions in the same crystal. This happens because the arrangement of particles is different along different directions.

### 2. Amorphous Solids

* An amorphous solid (Greek *amorphos* = no form) consists of particles of irregular shape.
* The arrangement has only **short range order**: a regular repeating pattern is seen only over short distances. Such regions are scattered and the arrangement between them is disordered.
* Examples: Glass, rubber, plastics, quartz glass. Their structure is similar to that of liquids.
* They soften over a range of temperature and can be moulded and blown into various shapes. On heating, they may become crystalline at some temperature. This is why some glass objects from ancient civilisations look milky.
* Like liquids, amorphous solids have a tendency to flow, though very slowly. Therefore they are called **pseudo solids** or **super cooled liquids**.

!!! example "Example"

    Glass panes fixed in windows of very old buildings are found to be slightly thicker at the bottom than at the top. This is because glass flows down very slowly over the years.

* They are **isotropic**: since there is no long range order and the arrangement is irregular in all directions, the value of any physical property is the same in every direction.
* Uses: Glass, rubber and plastics are used in daily life. Amorphous silicon is one of the best photovoltaic materials for converting sunlight into electricity.

### Distinction between Crystalline and Amorphous Solids

| Property | Crystalline solids | Amorphous solids |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Definite characteristic geometrical shape | Irregular shape |
| Melting point | Melt at a sharp and characteristic temperature | Gradually soften over a range of temperature |
| Cleavage property | When cut with a sharp edged tool, they split into two pieces with plain and smooth surfaces | When cut, they split into two pieces with irregular surfaces |
| Heat of fusion | Definite and characteristic heat of fusion | No definite heat of fusion |
| Anisotropy | Anisotropic | Isotropic |
| Nature | True solids | Pseudo solids or super cooled liquids |
| Order in arrangement | Long range order | Only short range order |

## Classification of Crystalline Solids

Crystalline solids are classified into four categories on the basis of the nature of intermolecular forces operating in them: molecular, ionic, metallic and covalent solids.

### 1. Molecular Solids

Molecules are the constituent particles of molecular solids. They are of three types:

#### (a) Non-polar Molecular Solids

* They consist of atoms (like argon, helium) or non-polar molecules (like H<sub>2</sub>, Cl<sub>2</sub>, I<sub>2</sub>).
* The particles are held by weak **dispersion forces** or **London forces**.
* They are soft, non-conductors of electricity, have low melting points and are usually liquids or gases at room temperature and pressure.

#### (b) Polar Molecular Solids

* They consist of molecules formed by polar covalent bonds, like HCl, SO<sub>2</sub>.
* The molecules are held by relatively stronger **dipole-dipole interactions**.
* They are soft and non-conductors of electricity. Their melting points are higher than those of non-polar molecular solids, but most of them are still gases or liquids at room temperature. Examples: Solid SO<sub>2</sub>, solid NH<sub>3</sub>.

#### (c) Hydrogen Bonded Molecular Solids

* Their molecules contain polar covalent bonds between H and F, O or N atoms, and are held together by strong **hydrogen bonds**. Example: H<sub>2</sub>O (ice).
* They are non-conductors of electricity and are generally volatile liquids or soft solids at room temperature.

### 2. Ionic Solids

* Ions are the constituent particles. Cations and anions are arranged in three dimensions and are bound by strong **coulombic (electrostatic) forces**.
* They are hard, brittle and have high melting and boiling points.
* In the solid state, ions are not free to move, so they are **insulators**. In the molten state or in aqueous solution, the ions become free to move and they **conduct electricity**.
* Examples: NaCl, MgO, ZnS, CaF<sub>2</sub>.

### 3. Metallic Solids

* Metals are an orderly collection of positive ions surrounded by and held together by a **sea of free electrons**. Each metal atom contributes one or more electrons to this sea.
* These mobile electrons are responsible for the high electrical and thermal conductivity of metals. When an electric field is applied, the electrons flow through the network of positive ions.
* The free electrons are also responsible for the lustre and colour of metals.
* Metals are highly malleable and ductile. Examples: Fe, Cu, Ag, Mg.

### 4. Covalent or Network Solids

* Non-metal atoms are joined by covalent bonds throughout the crystal. These are also called **giant molecules**.
* Covalent bonds are strong and directional, so the atoms are held very strongly at their positions.
* They are very hard and brittle, have extremely high melting points (and may even decompose before melting), and are insulators.
* Examples: Diamond, silicon carbide, SiO<sub>2</sub> (quartz), AlN.

!!! note "Graphite: An Exception"

    * Graphite is a covalent solid but it is soft and a good conductor of electricity.
    * In graphite, carbon atoms are arranged in layers. Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to three neighbouring atoms in the same layer.
    * The fourth valence electron of each atom is present between the layers and is free to move. These free electrons make graphite a good conductor.
    * The layers can slide over one another. This makes graphite soft and a good solid lubricant.

| Hydrogen bonded molecular | Molecules | Hydrogen bonding | H<sub>2</sub>O (ice) | Hard | Insulator | Low |

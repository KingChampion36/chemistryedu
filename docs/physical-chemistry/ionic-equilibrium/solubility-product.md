## Solubility of Ionic Salts

* The solubility of ionic solids in water varies a great deal. Some (like calcium chloride) are so soluble that they are hygroscopic and absorb water vapour from the air. Others (like lithium fluoride) have so little solubility that they are commonly called insoluble.
* Solubility mainly depends on the lattice enthalpy of the salt and the solvation enthalpy of its ions.
* To dissolve a salt, the strong attractive forces between its ions (lattice enthalpy) must be overcome by ion-solvent interactions. The solvation enthalpy is always negative, i.e., energy is released.
* In a non-polar (covalent) solvent, the solvation enthalpy is small and cannot overcome the lattice enthalpy. So ionic salts do not dissolve in non-polar solvents.

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    For a salt to dissolve in a solvent, its solvation enthalpy must be greater than its lattice enthalpy.

* Each salt has a characteristic solubility which depends on temperature. Salts are classified as:

Category     | Type               | Solubility
-------------|--------------------|-------------------------
Category I   | Soluble            | Solubility > 0.1 M
Category II  | Slightly soluble   | 0.01 M < Solubility < 0.1 M
Category III | Sparingly soluble  | Solubility < 0.01 M

## Solubility Product Constant (K<sub>sp</sub>)

* Consider solid barium sulphate in contact with its saturated aqueous solution. An equilibrium exists between the undissolved solid and its ions:

!!! tip ""

    $$BaSO_{4(s)} &#8652; Ba^{2+}_{(aq)} + SO^{2-}_{4(aq)}$$

    $$K = {[Ba^{2+}][SO_4^{2-}] \over [BaSO_4]}$$

* For a pure solid, the concentration remains constant, so it is included in the constant:

!!! tip ""

    $$K_{sp} = K[BaSO_4] = [Ba^{2+}][SO_4^{2-}]$$

* K<sub>sp</sub> is called the solubility product constant or simply solubility product.
* For BaSO<sub>4</sub>, K<sub>sp</sub> = 1.1 &times; 10<sup>-10</sup> at 298 K. If its molar solubility is S, then [Ba<sup>2+</sup>] = [SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup>] = S:

!!! tip ""

    $$1.1 \times 10^{-10} = (S)(S) = S^2$$

    $$S = 1.05 \times 10^{-5}\ mol\ L^{-1}$$

### Relation between solubility (S) and K<sub>sp</sub>

* A salt may give two or more ions with different charges. Example: zirconium phosphate, (Zr<sup>4+</sup>)<sub>3</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub><sup>3-</sup>)<sub>4</sub>, gives 3 Zr<sup>4+</sup> and 4 PO<sub>4</sub><sup>3-</sup> ions. If its molar solubility is S:

!!! tip ""

    $$[Zr^{4+}] = 3S,\ \ [PO_4^{3-}] = 4S$$

    $$K_{sp} = (3S)^3(4S)^4 = 6912\ S^7$$

    $$S = \left({K_{sp} \over 3^3 \times 4^4}\right)^{1/7} = \left({K_{sp} \over 6912}\right)^{1/7}$$

* In general, for a salt M<sub>x</sub>X<sub>y</sub> with molar solubility S:

!!! tip ""

    $$M_xX_{y(s)} &#8652; xM^{p+}_{(aq)} + yX^{q-}_{(aq)},\ \ (x \times p^+ = y \times q^-)$$

    $$K_{sp} = [M^{p+}]^x[X^{q-}]^y = (xS)^x(yS)^y = x^x\ y^y\ S^{(x+y)}$$

    $$S = \left({K_{sp} \over x^x\ y^y}\right)^{1/(x+y)}$$

Salt type                  | Example                                   | K<sub>sp</sub>
---------------------------|-------------------------------------------|----------------------
AB                         | AgCl, BaSO<sub>4</sub>                    | S<sup>2</sup>
AB<sub>2</sub> or A<sub>2</sub>B | CaF<sub>2</sub>, Ag<sub>2</sub>CrO<sub>4</sub> | 4S<sup>3</sup>
AB<sub>3</sub>             | Fe(OH)<sub>3</sub>, Al(OH)<sub>3</sub>    | 27S<sup>4</sup>
A<sub>2</sub>B<sub>3</sub> | A<sub>2</sub>X<sub>3</sub>                | 108S<sup>5</sup>
A<sub>3</sub>B<sub>4</sub> | Zr<sub>3</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>4</sub> | 6912S<sup>7</sup>

### Ionic product (Q<sub>sp</sub>) and precipitation

* When the concentrations are not equilibrium concentrations, the same product is called the ionic product, Q<sub>sp</sub> (it is the reaction quotient for the dissolution).
* At equilibrium (saturated solution), K<sub>sp</sub> = Q<sub>sp</sub>. Otherwise, comparing Q<sub>sp</sub> with K<sub>sp</sub> tells us whether precipitation or dissolution will occur:

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    * Q<sub>sp</sub> > K<sub>sp</sub>: the solution is supersaturated and precipitation occurs.

    * Q<sub>sp</sub> = K<sub>sp</sub>: the solution is saturated, at equilibrium.

    * Q<sub>sp</sub> < K<sub>sp</sub>: the solution is unsaturated, more salt can dissolve and no precipitate forms.

## Common Ion Effect on Solubility of Ionic Salts

* By Le Chatelier's principle, if the concentration of one of the ions is increased, it combines with the oppositely charged ion and some salt precipitates until once again K<sub>sp</sub> = Q<sub>sp</sub>.
* Similarly, if the concentration of one of the ions is decreased, more salt dissolves until K<sub>sp</sub> = Q<sub>sp</sub>.
* So the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt decreases in presence of a common ion.

!!! example "Applications"

    * **Purification of common salt:** When HCl gas is passed through a saturated solution of NaCl, pure NaCl precipitates due to the increased concentration (activity) of Cl<sup>-</sup> from HCl. Soluble impurities like sodium and magnesium sulphates stay in solution. (For soluble salts like NaCl, activities are used instead of molarities in Q<sub>sp</sub>.)

    * **Gravimetric analysis:** The common ion effect is used for almost complete precipitation of an ion as its sparingly soluble salt. Example: silver as AgCl, ferric ion as its hydroxide (hydrated ferric oxide) and barium as BaSO<sub>4</sub>.

## Effect of pH on Solubility

* The solubility of salts of weak acids (like phosphates) increases at lower pH. At lower pH, the anion X<sup>-</sup> gets protonated to HX, which lowers [X<sup>-</sup>]. To restore K<sub>sp</sub> = Q<sub>sp</sub>, more salt dissolves.
* Two equilibria must be satisfied together:

!!! tip ""

    $$K_{sp} = [M^+][X^-]$$

    $$HX_{(aq)} &#8652; H^+_{(aq)} + X^-_{(aq)},\ \ K_a = {[H^+][X^-] \over [HX]}$$

    $${[X^-] \over [HX]} = {K_a \over [H^+]}$$

    Taking inverse and adding 1 on both sides:

    $${[HX] \over [X^-]} + 1 = {[H^+] \over K_a} + 1$$

    $${[HX] + [X^-] \over [X^-]} = {[H^+] + K_a \over K_a}$$

    Taking inverse again, the fraction of the dissolved anion present as X<sup>-</sup> is:

    $$f = {[X^-] \over [X^-] + [HX]} = {K_a \over K_a + [H^+]}$$

* If S is the solubility of the salt at a given pH, then [M<sup>+</sup>] = S and [X<sup>-</sup>] = fS:

!!! tip ""

    $$K_{sp} = [S][fS] = S^2 \left({K_a \over K_a + [H^+]}\right)$$

    $$S = \sqrt{K_{sp}\left({[H^+] + K_a \over K_a}\right)}$$

* The fraction f decreases as pH decreases. So, solubility S increases with increase in [H<sup>+</sup>], i.e., with decrease in pH.

## Questions

!!! question "Calculate the solubility of A<sub>2</sub>X<sub>3</sub> in pure water, assuming that neither kind of ion reacts with water. The solubility product of A<sub>2</sub>X<sub>3</sub>, K<sub>sp</sub> = 1.1 &times; 10<sup>-23</sup>."

    $$A_2X_3 &rarr; 2A^{3+} + 3X^{2-}$$

    If S is the solubility, [A<sup>3+</sup>] = 2S and [X<sup>2-</sup>] = 3S.

    $$K_{sp} = [A^{3+}]^2[X^{2-}]^3 = (2S)^2(3S)^3 = 108S^5 = 1.1 \times 10^{-23}$$

    $$S^5 = 1.0 \times 10^{-25}$$

    $$S = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}\ mol/L$$

!!! question "The values of K<sub>sp</sub> of two sparingly soluble salts Ni(OH)<sub>2</sub> and AgCN are 2.0 &times; 10<sup>-15</sup> and 6 &times; 10<sup>-17</sup> respectively. Which salt is more soluble? Explain."

    $$AgCN &#8652; Ag^+ + CN^-,\ \ K_{sp} = [Ag^+][CN^-] = 6 \times 10^{-17}$$

    Let [Ag<sup>+</sup>] = [CN<sup>-</sup>] = S<sub>1</sub>:

    $$S_1^2 = 6 \times 10^{-17},\ \ S_1 = 7.8 \times 10^{-9}\ M$$

    $$Ni(OH)_2 &#8652; Ni^{2+} + 2OH^-,\ \ K_{sp} = [Ni^{2+}][OH^-]^2 = 2 \times 10^{-15}$$

    Let [Ni<sup>2+</sup>] = S<sub>2</sub>, then [OH<sup>-</sup>] = 2S<sub>2</sub>:

    $$(S_2)(2S_2)^2 = 4S_2^3 = 2 \times 10^{-15}$$

    $$S_2 = 7.9 \times 10^{-6}\ M$$

    S<sub>2</sub> > S<sub>1</sub>, so Ni(OH)<sub>2</sub> is more soluble than AgCN. Note that we compare solubilities, not K<sub>sp</sub> values directly, since the salts are of different types.

!!! question "Calculate the molar solubility of Ni(OH)<sub>2</sub> in 0.10 M NaOH. The ionic product of Ni(OH)<sub>2</sub> is 2.0 &times; 10<sup>-15</sup>."

    Let the solubility of Ni(OH)<sub>2</sub> be S. It gives S mol/L of Ni<sup>2+</sup> and 2S mol/L of OH<sup>-</sup>, but the solution already has 0.10 mol/L of OH<sup>-</sup> from NaOH.

    $$[OH^-] = (0.10 + 2S)\ mol/L$$

    $$K_{sp} = [Ni^{2+}][OH^-]^2 = (S)(0.10 + 2S)^2 = 2.0 \times 10^{-15}$$

    As K<sub>sp</sub> is small, 2S << 0.10, so (0.10 + 2S) &asymp; 0.10:

    $$2.0 \times 10^{-15} = S(0.10)^2$$

    $$S = 2.0 \times 10^{-13}\ M = [Ni^{2+}]$$

    Compare this with 7.9 &times; 10<sup>-6</sup> M in pure water: the common ion OH<sup>-</sup> reduces the solubility enormously.

!!! question "Determine the solubilities of silver chromate, barium chromate, ferric hydroxide, lead chloride and mercurous iodide at 298 K from their solubility product constants. Determine also the molarities of individual ions."

    **Silver chromate**, Ag<sub>2</sub>CrO<sub>4</sub> &#8652; 2Ag<sup>+</sup> + CrO<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup>, K<sub>sp</sub> = 1.1 &times; 10<sup>-12</sup>

    $$4S^3 = 1.1 \times 10^{-12},\ \ S = 6.5 \times 10^{-5}\ M$$

    $$[Ag^+] = 2S = 1.30 \times 10^{-4}\ M,\ \ [CrO_4^{2-}] = 6.5 \times 10^{-5}\ M$$

    **Barium chromate**, BaCrO<sub>4</sub> &#8652; Ba<sup>2+</sup> + CrO<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup>, K<sub>sp</sub> = 1.2 &times; 10<sup>-10</sup>

    $$S^2 = 1.2 \times 10^{-10},\ \ S = 1.1 \times 10^{-5}\ M = [Ba^{2+}] = [CrO_4^{2-}]$$

    **Ferric hydroxide**, Fe(OH)<sub>3</sub> &#8652; Fe<sup>3+</sup> + 3OH<sup>-</sup>, K<sub>sp</sub> = 1.0 &times; 10<sup>-38</sup>

    $$(S)(3S)^3 = 27S^4 = 1.0 \times 10^{-38},\ \ S = 1.39 \times 10^{-10}\ M$$

    $$[Fe^{3+}] = 1.39 \times 10^{-10}\ M,\ \ [OH^-] = 3S = 4.17 \times 10^{-10}\ M$$

    **Lead chloride**, PbCl<sub>2</sub> &#8652; Pb<sup>2+</sup> + 2Cl<sup>-</sup>, K<sub>sp</sub> = 1.6 &times; 10<sup>-5</sup>

    $$4S^3 = 1.6 \times 10^{-5},\ \ S = 1.59 \times 10^{-2}\ M$$

    $$[Pb^{2+}] = 1.59 \times 10^{-2}\ M,\ \ [Cl^-] = 3.18 \times 10^{-2}\ M$$

    **Mercurous iodide**, Hg<sub>2</sub>I<sub>2</sub> &#8652; Hg<sub>2</sub><sup>2+</sup> + 2I<sup>-</sup>, K<sub>sp</sub> = 4.5 &times; 10<sup>-29</sup>

    $$4S^3 = 4.5 \times 10^{-29},\ \ S = 2.24 \times 10^{-10}\ M$$

    $$[Hg_2^{2+}] = 2.24 \times 10^{-10}\ M,\ \ [I^-] = 4.48 \times 10^{-10}\ M$$

!!! question "The solubility product constant of Ag<sub>2</sub>CrO<sub>4</sub> and AgBr are 1.1 &times; 10<sup>-12</sup> and 5.0 &times; 10<sup>-13</sup> respectively. Calculate the ratio of the molarities of their saturated solutions."

    $$Ag_2CrO_4:\ 4S_1^3 = 1.1 \times 10^{-12},\ \ S_1 = 6.5 \times 10^{-5}\ M$$

    $$AgBr:\ S_2^2 = 5.0 \times 10^{-13},\ \ S_2 = 7.07 \times 10^{-7}\ M$$

    $${S_1 \over S_2} = {6.5 \times 10^{-5} \over 7.07 \times 10^{-7}} = 91.9$$

    Silver chromate is about 92 times more soluble, even though its K<sub>sp</sub> is only about twice that of AgBr, because the two salts are of different types.

!!! question "Equal volumes of 0.002 M solutions of sodium iodate and cupric chlorate are mixed together. Will it lead to precipitation of copper iodate? (For cupric iodate K<sub>sp</sub> = 7.4 &times; 10<sup>-8</sup>)"

    On mixing equal volumes, each concentration is halved:

    $$[Cu^{2+}] = [IO_3^-] = 0.001\ M$$

    $$Cu(IO_3)_2 &#8652; Cu^{2+} + 2IO_3^-$$

    $$Q_{sp} = [Cu^{2+}][IO_3^-]^2 = (10^{-3})(10^{-3})^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-9}$$

    Q<sub>sp</sub> (1.0 &times; 10<sup>-9</sup>) < K<sub>sp</sub> (7.4 &times; 10<sup>-8</sup>), so no precipitate of copper iodate is formed.

!!! question "The ionization constant of benzoic acid is 6.46 &times; 10<sup>-5</sup> and K<sub>sp</sub> for silver benzoate is 2.5 &times; 10<sup>-13</sup>. How many times is silver benzoate more soluble in a buffer of pH 3.19 compared to its solubility in pure water?"

    In pure water (benzoate hydrolysis neglected):

    $$S_0 = \sqrt{K_{sp}}$$

    In the buffer:

    $$[H^+] = 10^{-3.19} = 6.46 \times 10^{-4}\ M$$

    $$S = \sqrt{K_{sp}\left({[H^+] + K_a \over K_a}\right)}$$

    $${S \over S_0} = \sqrt{[H^+] + K_a \over K_a} = \sqrt{6.46 \times 10^{-4} + 6.46 \times 10^{-5} \over 6.46 \times 10^{-5}} = \sqrt{11} = 3.32$$

    Silver benzoate is about 3.32 times more soluble in the buffer of pH 3.19.

!!! question "What is the maximum concentration of equimolar solutions of ferrous sulphate and sodium sulphide so that when mixed in equal volumes, there is no precipitation of iron sulphide? (For iron sulphide, K<sub>sp</sub> = 6.3 &times; 10<sup>-18</sup>)"

    Let the concentration of each solution be c. After mixing equal volumes, [Fe<sup>2+</sup>] = [S<sup>2-</sup>] = c/2. For no precipitation, Q<sub>sp</sub> must not exceed K<sub>sp</sub>:

    $$\left({c \over 2}\right)^2 = 6.3 \times 10^{-18}$$

    $${c \over 2} = 2.5 \times 10^{-9}\ M$$

    So the concentration of each ion in the mixture can be at most 2.5 &times; 10<sup>-9</sup> M, i.e., each original solution can be at most 5.0 &times; 10<sup>-9</sup> M.

!!! question "What is the minimum volume of water required to dissolve 1 g of calcium sulphate at 298 K? (For calcium sulphate, K<sub>sp</sub> is 9.1 &times; 10<sup>-6</sup>)"

    $$CaSO_4 &#8652; Ca^{2+} + SO_4^{2-},\ \ S^2 = 9.1 \times 10^{-6}$$

    $$S = 3.02 \times 10^{-3}\ mol/L$$

    Molar mass of CaSO<sub>4</sub> = 136 g/mol

    $$Solubility\ in\ g/L = 3.02 \times 10^{-3} \times 136 = 0.41\ g/L$$

    $$Volume\ required\ for\ 1\ g = {1 \over 0.41} = 2.44\ L$$

!!! question "The concentration of sulphide ion in 0.1 M HCl solution saturated with hydrogen sulphide is 1.0 &times; 10<sup>-19</sup> M. If 10 mL of this is added to 5 mL of 0.04 M solution of the following: FeSO<sub>4</sub>, MnCl<sub>2</sub>, ZnCl<sub>2</sub> and CdCl<sub>2</sub>, in which of these solutions precipitation will take place?"

    Total volume after mixing = 15 mL.

    $$[S^{2-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-19} \times {10 \over 15} = 6.67 \times 10^{-20}\ M$$

    $$[M^{2+}] = 0.04 \times {5 \over 15} = 1.33 \times 10^{-2}\ M$$

    $$Q_{sp} = [M^{2+}][S^{2-}] = 1.33 \times 10^{-2} \times 6.67 \times 10^{-20} = 8.9 \times 10^{-22}$$

    Compare Q<sub>sp</sub> with K<sub>sp</sub> of each sulphide:

    Sulphide | K<sub>sp</sub>                  | Q<sub>sp</sub> > K<sub>sp</sub>?
    ---------|---------------------------------|---------------------------
    FeS      | 6.3 &times; 10<sup>-18</sup>    | No, no precipitate
    MnS      | 2.5 &times; 10<sup>-13</sup>    | No, no precipitate
    ZnS      | 1.6 &times; 10<sup>-24</sup>    | Yes, precipitate forms
    CdS      | 8.0 &times; 10<sup>-27</sup>    | Yes, precipitate forms

    So, using these K<sub>sp</sub> values, precipitation takes place in the ZnCl<sub>2</sub> and CdCl<sub>2</sub> solutions. CdS, with the smallest K<sub>sp</sub>, precipitates most readily.

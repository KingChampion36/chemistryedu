## Di- and Polybasic Acids

* Acids like oxalic acid, sulphuric acid and phosphoric acid have more than one ionizable proton per molecule. Such acids are known as polybasic or polyprotic acids.
* A polybasic acid ionizes in steps, losing one proton at a time. Each step has its own ionization constant.
* For a dibasic acid H<sub>2</sub>X:

!!! tip ""

    $$H_2X_{(aq)} &#8652; H^+_{(aq)} + HX^-_{(aq)},\ \ K_{a_1} = {[H^+][HX^-] \over [H_2X]}$$

    $$HX^-_{(aq)} &#8652; H^+_{(aq)} + X^{2-}_{(aq)},\ \ K_{a_2} = {[H^+][X^{2-}] \over [HX^-]}$$

* K<sub>a<sub>1</sub></sub> and K<sub>a<sub>2</sub></sub> are called the first and second ionization constants of H<sub>2</sub>X.
* Similarly, a tribasic acid like H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub> has three ionization constants: K<sub>a<sub>1</sub></sub>, K<sub>a<sub>2</sub></sub> and K<sub>a<sub>3</sub></sub>.

!!! example "Ionization of phosphoric acid"

    $$H_3PO_4 &#8652; H^+ + H_2PO_4^-,\ \ K_{a_1} = 7.5 \times 10^{-3}$$

    $$H_2PO_4^- &#8652; H^+ + HPO_4^{2-},\ \ K_{a_2} = 6.2 \times 10^{-8}$$

    $$HPO_4^{2-} &#8652; H^+ + PO_4^{3-},\ \ K_{a_3} = 4.2 \times 10^{-13}$$

* For a polybasic acid, the overall constant is the product of the step-wise constants: for H<sub>2</sub>X &#8652; 2H<sup>+</sup> + X<sup>2-</sup>, K = K<sub>a<sub>1</sub></sub> &times; K<sub>a<sub>2</sub></sub>.

### Why is K<sub>a<sub>1</sub></sub> > K<sub>a<sub>2</sub></sub> > K<sub>a<sub>3</sub></sub>?

* The higher order ionization constants are always smaller than the lower order ones.
* This is because it is more difficult to remove a positively charged proton from a negative ion, due to electrostatic attraction.
* Example: Removing a proton from uncharged H<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> is easier than removing one from negatively charged HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup>. Similarly, removing a proton from doubly charged HPO<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup> is harder than from H<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub><sup>-</sup>.

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    * A solution of a diprotic acid contains a mixture of H<sub>2</sub>A, HA<sup>-</sup> and A<sup>2-</sup>.

    * Since K<sub>a<sub>1</sub></sub> >> K<sub>a<sub>2</sub></sub>, the H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup> in the solution comes mainly from the first dissociation step. The primary reaction is the dissociation of H<sub>2</sub>A.

    * For a weak diprotic acid, the concentration of the dianion is approximately equal to K<sub>a<sub>2</sub></sub>: [A<sup>2-</sup>] &asymp; K<sub>a<sub>2</sub></sub> (when no other acid is present).

## Factors Affecting Acid Strength

* Broadly, the extent of dissociation of an acid H-A depends on the **strength** and **polarity** of the H-A bond.
* When the H-A bond strength decreases (less energy is needed to break it), HA becomes a stronger acid.
* When the H-A bond becomes more polar (greater electronegativity difference between H and A), there is more charge separation, the bond breaks more easily and acidity increases.

### 1. Elements in the same group: bond strength decides

* Down a group, the size of A increases and the H-A bond strength decreases. So acid strength increases down the group.
* Here bond strength is more important than polarity.

!!! tip ""

    $$Size\ of\ A\ increases\ &rarr;$$

    $$HF \ll HCl \ll HBr \ll HI$$

    $$Acid\ strength\ increases\ &rarr;$$

* Similarly, H<sub>2</sub>S is a stronger acid than H<sub>2</sub>O.

### 2. Elements in the same period: bond polarity decides

* Across a period, the electronegativity of A increases and the H-A bond becomes more polar. So acid strength increases across the period.

!!! tip ""

    $$Electronegativity\ of\ A\ increases\ &rarr;$$

    $$CH_4 < NH_3 < H_2O < HF$$

    $$Acid\ strength\ increases\ &rarr;$$

## Questions

!!! question "The first ionization constant of H<sub>2</sub>S is 9.1 &times; 10<sup>-8</sup>. Calculate the concentration of HS<sup>-</sup> ion in its 0.1 M solution. How will this concentration be affected if the solution is 0.1 M in HCl also? If the second dissociation constant of H<sub>2</sub>S is 1.2 &times; 10<sup>-13</sup>, calculate the concentration of S<sup>2-</sup> under both conditions."

    **(i) In 0.1 M H<sub>2</sub>S alone**

    $$H_2S &#8652; H^+ + HS^-,\ \ K_{a_1} = 9.1 \times 10^{-8}$$

    Let [H<sup>+</sup>] = [HS<sup>-</sup>] = x. Since K<sub>a<sub>1</sub></sub> is small, [H<sub>2</sub>S] &asymp; 0.1 M.

    $$K_{a_1} = {x^2 \over 0.1} = 9.1 \times 10^{-8}$$

    $$x = [HS^-] = \sqrt{9.1 \times 10^{-9}} = 9.54 \times 10^{-5}\ M$$

    For the second step, [H<sup>+</sup>] &asymp; [HS<sup>-</sup>], so they cancel:

    $$K_{a_2} = {[H^+][S^{2-}] \over [HS^-]},\ \ [S^{2-}] = K_{a_2} = 1.2 \times 10^{-13}\ M$$

    **(ii) In presence of 0.1 M HCl**

    HCl is a strong acid, so [H<sup>+</sup>] = 0.1 M. The common ion H<sup>+</sup> suppresses the ionization of H<sub>2</sub>S.

    $$[HS^-] = {K_{a_1}[H_2S] \over [H^+]} = {9.1 \times 10^{-8} \times 0.1 \over 0.1} = 9.1 \times 10^{-8}\ M$$

    For S<sup>2-</sup>, use the overall equilibrium H<sub>2</sub>S &#8652; 2H<sup>+</sup> + S<sup>2-</sup>:

    $$K_{a_1} \times K_{a_2} = {[H^+]^2[S^{2-}] \over [H_2S]}$$

    $$[S^{2-}] = {9.1 \times 10^{-8} \times 1.2 \times 10^{-13} \times 0.1 \over (0.1)^2} = 1.09 \times 10^{-19}\ M$$

    So in 0.1 M HCl, [HS<sup>-</sup>] falls from 9.54 &times; 10<sup>-5</sup> M to 9.1 &times; 10<sup>-8</sup> M, and [S<sup>2-</sup>] falls from 1.2 &times; 10<sup>-13</sup> M to 1.09 &times; 10<sup>-19</sup> M.

!!! question "Arrange HF, HCl, HBr and HI in increasing order of acid strength and explain."

    $$HF < HCl < HBr < HI$$

    These elements belong to the same group. Down the group the size of the halogen increases, so the H-X bond becomes longer and weaker. A weaker bond releases H<sup>+</sup> more easily, so acid strength increases from HF to HI, even though bond polarity decreases.

!!! question "Why is HF a stronger acid than H<sub>2</sub>O, while H<sub>2</sub>S is a stronger acid than H<sub>2</sub>O?"

    * F and O are in the same period. F is more electronegative than O, so the H-F bond is more polar than the H-O bond. Hence HF is a stronger acid than H<sub>2</sub>O.

    * O and S are in the same group. S is larger than O, so the H-S bond is weaker than the H-O bond. Hence H<sub>2</sub>S is a stronger acid than H<sub>2</sub>O.

!!! question "Why is the second ionization constant of carbonic acid (5.6 &times; 10<sup>-11</sup>) much smaller than its first ionization constant (4.3 &times; 10<sup>-7</sup>)?"

    In the first step, a proton is removed from the neutral H<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> molecule. In the second step, a proton must be removed from the negatively charged HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> ion. The negative charge holds the positive proton by electrostatic attraction, so the second ionization is much more difficult and K<sub>a<sub>2</sub></sub> << K<sub>a<sub>1</sub></sub>.

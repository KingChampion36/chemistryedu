## Proteins

* Proteins are the most abundant biomolecules of the living system. Chief sources are milk, cheese, pulses, peanuts, fish and meat.
* They occur in every part of the body, form the basis of its structure and functions, and are needed for growth and maintenance of the body.
* The word protein comes from the Greek *proteios*, meaning "primary" or "of prime importance".
* All proteins are polymers of **&alpha;-amino acids**.

## Amino Acids

* Amino acids contain an amino (-NH<sub>2</sub>) group and a carboxyl (-COOH) group.
* Depending on the position of the amino group relative to the carboxyl group, amino acids are classified as &alpha;, &beta;, &gamma;, &delta; and so on.
* Only **&alpha;-amino acids** (-NH<sub>2</sub> on the carbon next to -COOH) are obtained on hydrolysis of proteins. They may contain other functional groups too.

!!! tip "General structure of an &alpha;-amino acid"

    $$R-CH(NH_2)-COOH$$

    Here R is the side chain, which is different for each amino acid.

* All &alpha;-amino acids have trivial names that usually reflect a property or source. **Glycine** is named for its sweet taste (Greek *glykos* = sweet) and **tyrosine** was first obtained from cheese (Greek *tyros* = cheese).
* Amino acids are represented by a three-letter symbol, and sometimes by a one-letter code.

## Classification of Amino Acids

### On the basis of acidic/basic character

* **Neutral** amino acids: equal number of amino and carboxyl groups. Example: glycine, alanine.
* **Basic** amino acids: more amino groups than carboxyl groups. Example: lysine, arginine.
* **Acidic** amino acids: more carboxyl groups than amino groups. Example: glutamic acid, aspartic acid.

### On the basis of synthesis in the body

* **Non-essential amino acids** can be synthesised in the body. Examples: glycine, alanine.
* **Essential amino acids** cannot be synthesised in the body and must be obtained through the diet. Examples: valine, leucine (marked * in the table). Ten amino acids are essential.

## Properties of Amino Acids

* Amino acids are usually colourless, crystalline, water-soluble, high-melting solids.
* They behave like **salts** rather than like simple amines or carboxylic acids. This is because both an acidic (-COOH) and a basic (-NH<sub>2</sub>) group are present in the same molecule.

### Zwitter ion

* In aqueous solution, the carboxyl group loses a proton and the amino group accepts a proton. This gives a dipolar ion called a **zwitter ion**.
* It is neutral overall but contains both positive and negative charges.

!!! tip ""

    $$R-CH(NH_2)-COOH\ &#8652;\ \underset{Zwitter\ ion}{R-CH(\overset{+}{N}H_3)-COO^-}$$

* In zwitter ionic form, amino acids show **amphoteric** behaviour: they react with both acids and bases.
    * With acid, -COO<sup>-</sup> accepts a proton and the amino acid becomes a cation.
    * With base, -N<sup>+</sup>H<sub>3</sub> loses a proton and the amino acid becomes an anion.

### Optical activity

* Except glycine (where R = H), all naturally occurring &alpha;-amino acids are **optically active**, because the &alpha;-carbon is asymmetric.
* They exist in both D and L forms. Most naturally occurring amino acids have **L-configuration**. L-amino acids are drawn with the -NH<sub>2</sub> group on the **left** in the Fischer projection.

## Peptide Bond

* Proteins are polymers of &alpha;-amino acids connected by **peptide bonds** (peptide linkages).
* Chemically, a peptide linkage is an **amide** (-CO-NH-) formed between the -COOH group of one amino acid and the -NH<sub>2</sub> group of another, with elimination of a water molecule.
* The product of two amino acids is a **dipeptide**. For example, the carboxyl group of glycine combines with the amino group of alanine to give **glycylalanine (Gly-Ala)**:

!!! tip ""

    $$\underset{Glycine}{H_2N-CH_2-COOH} + \underset{Alanine}{H_2N-CH(CH_3)-COOH} \xrightarrow{-H_2O} \underset{Glycylalanine\ (Gly-Ala)}{H_2N-CH_2-\boxed{CO-NH}-CH(CH_3)-COOH}$$

* Three amino acids joined by two peptide linkages give a **tripeptide**. Similarly, four, five or six amino acids give a **tetrapeptide**, **pentapeptide** or **hexapeptide**.
* When more than ten amino acids are joined, the product is a **polypeptide**.
* A polypeptide with more than a hundred amino acid residues and molecular mass above 10,000 u is called a **protein**.
* The distinction is not sharp: a smaller polypeptide with a well-defined protein-like conformation is also called a protein. Example: **insulin**, which contains 51 amino acids.

## Classification of Proteins (by molecular shape)

### 1. Fibrous proteins

* The polypeptide chains run **parallel** and are held together by hydrogen and disulphide bonds, giving a fibre-like structure.
* They are generally **insoluble** in water.
* Examples: keratin (hair, wool, silk) and myosin (muscles).

### 2. Globular proteins

* The polypeptide chains **coil around** to give a spherical shape.
* They are usually **soluble** in water.
* Examples: insulin and albumins.

## Structure of Proteins

The structure and shape of proteins is studied at four levels, each more complex than the previous one.

### 1. Primary structure

* A protein may have one or more polypeptide chains. In each chain, the amino acids are linked in a **specific sequence**. This sequence is the primary structure of the protein.
* Any change in the sequence of amino acids creates a **different protein**.

### 2. Secondary structure

* It refers to the **shape** in which a long polypeptide chain can exist.
* It arises from regular folding of the polypeptide backbone due to **hydrogen bonding** between the >C=O and -NH- groups of the peptide bonds.
* There are two types:

#### (a) &alpha;-Helix

* The polypeptide chain forms all possible hydrogen bonds by twisting into a **right-handed screw** (helix).
* The -NH group of each amino acid residue is hydrogen-bonded to the >C=O of an adjacent turn of the helix (intramolecular hydrogen bonding).

#### (b) &beta;-Pleated sheet

* All the peptide chains are stretched out to nearly maximum extension and laid side by side.
* They are held together by **intermolecular** hydrogen bonds.
* The structure resembles the pleated folds of drapery, hence the name.

### 3. Tertiary structure

* It represents the **overall folding** of the polypeptide chains, i.e. further folding of the secondary structure.
* It gives rise to the two major molecular shapes: **fibrous** and **globular**.
* The main forces that stabilise the 2° and 3° structures are hydrogen bonds, disulphide linkages, van der Waals forces and electrostatic forces of attraction.

### 4. Quaternary structure

* Some proteins are made of two or more polypeptide chains called **sub-units**.
* The spatial arrangement of these sub-units with respect to each other is the quaternary structure. Example: haemoglobin.

## Denaturation of Proteins

* A protein in a biological system with a unique three-dimensional structure and biological activity is called a **native protein**.
* When a native protein is subjected to a **physical change** (like change in temperature) or a **chemical change** (like change in pH), its hydrogen bonds are disturbed.
* As a result, the globules unfold, the helices uncoil and the protein **loses its biological activity**. This is called **denaturation**.
* During denaturation, the **2° and 3° structures are destroyed** but the **1° structure remains intact**.

!!! example "Examples"

    * Coagulation of egg white on boiling.
    * Curdling of milk, caused by lactic acid formed by bacteria present in milk.

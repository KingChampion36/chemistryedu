## Introduction

Polymers are very large molecules of high molecular mass (10<sup>3</sup>-10<sup>7</sup> u), formed by joining a large number of repeating structural units. They are also called **macromolecules**.

* The word polymer comes from two Greek words: *poly* (many) and *mer* (unit or part).
* The repeating units are derived from simple, reactive molecules called **monomers**. Monomers in a polymer are joined to each other by **covalent bonds**.
* The process of forming a polymer from its monomers is called **polymerisation**.
* Polymers are the backbone of four major industries: plastics, elastomers (rubbers), fibres, and paints and varnishes. Buckets, toys, packaging bags, synthetic clothes, tyres, gears, seals and electrical insulation are all made of polymers.

!!! example "Examples"

    Ethene polymerises to polythene. Hexamethylene diamine and adipic acid react to form nylon 6,6. These are two different types of polymerisation reactions, as we will see below.

!!! tip ""

    $$n\ CH_2=CH_2 &rarr; -(CH_2-CH_2)_n-$$

    $$Ethene\ (monomer)\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ Polythene\ (polymer)$$

* **Functionality** of a monomer is the number of bonding sites (reactive functional groups) it has. Ethene has one double bond and can join to two other units, so it behaves as bi-functional. Ethylene glycol (two -OH groups) and adipic acid (two -COOH groups) are bi-functional; melamine is poly-functional.

## Classification of Polymers

Polymers can be classified in several ways depending on what we look at: where they come from, their structure, how they are made, the forces between their chains, and how the chain grows.

## 1. Classification Based on Source

### (a) Natural polymers

* Found in plants and animals.
* Examples: proteins, cellulose, starch, resins and rubber.

### (b) Semi-synthetic polymers

* Obtained by chemically modifying natural polymers.
* Examples: cellulose derivatives such as cellulose acetate (rayon) and cellulose nitrate.

### (c) Synthetic polymers

* Man-made polymers, widely used in daily life and industry.
* Examples: plastics (polythene), synthetic fibres (nylon 6,6) and synthetic rubbers (Buna-S).

## 2. Classification Based on Structure

### (a) Linear polymers

* Consist of long, straight chains lying side by side.
* Examples: high density polythene (HDP), polyvinyl chloride (PVC).

### (b) Branched chain polymers

* Linear chains carrying some side branches. The branches prevent close packing of chains.
* Example: low density polythene (LDP).

### (c) Cross linked or network polymers

* Usually formed from bi-functional and tri-functional monomers.
* Strong covalent bonds link the different linear chains together, giving a three-dimensional network.
* Examples: bakelite, melamine-formaldehyde polymer.

## 3. Classification Based on Mode of Polymerisation

### (a) Addition polymers

* Formed by repeated addition of monomer molecules that contain double or triple bonds. No molecule is lost in the process.
* Examples: polythene from ethene, polypropene from propene.
* An addition polymer made from a **single** kind of monomer is called a **homopolymer**, e.g. polythene.
* An addition polymer made from **two different** monomers is called a **copolymer**, e.g. Buna-S, Buna-N.

!!! tip ""

    $$n\ CH_2=CH_2 &rarr; -(CH_2-CH_2)_n-\ \ \ (Homopolymer)$$

    $$n\ CH_2=CH-CH=CH_2 + n\ C_6H_5CH=CH_2 &rarr; -(CH_2-CH=CH-CH_2-CH_2-CH(C_6H_5))_n-$$

    $$1,3-Butadiene\ +\ Styrene\ &rarr;\ Butadiene-styrene\ copolymer\ (Buna-S)$$

### (b) Condensation polymers

* Formed by repeated condensation reactions between two different bi-functional or tri-functional monomers.
* Each condensation step eliminates a small molecule such as water, alcohol or hydrogen chloride.
* Examples: terylene (dacron), nylon 6,6, nylon 6.

!!! tip ""

    $$n\ H_2N(CH_2)_6NH_2 + n\ HOOC(CH_2)_4COOH &rarr; -[NH(CH_2)_6NHCO(CH_2)_4CO]_n- + (2n-1)H_2O$$

    $$Hexamethylene\ diamine\ +\ Adipic\ acid\ &rarr;\ Nylon\ 6,6$$

!!! example "Example"

    Is -(CH<sub>2</sub>-CH(C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>5</sub>))<sub>n</sub>- a homopolymer or a copolymer?

    It is a **homopolymer**. Its only monomer is styrene, C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>5</sub>CH=CH<sub>2</sub>.

## 4. Classification Based on Molecular Forces

* Mechanical properties of a polymer, such as tensile strength, elasticity and toughness, depend on the intermolecular forces (van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonds) that hold the chains together.
* On the basis of the strength of these forces, polymers fall into four groups.

### (a) Elastomers

* Rubber-like solids with elastic properties.
* Chains are held by the **weakest** intermolecular forces, so the polymer can be stretched.
* A few cross links between chains pull the polymer back to its original shape when the force is released, as in vulcanised rubber.
* Examples: Buna-S, Buna-N, neoprene.

### (b) Fibres

* Thread-forming solids with high tensile strength and high modulus.
* Chains are held by **strong** forces such as hydrogen bonding. These forces also pack the chains closely, giving a crystalline nature.
* Examples: polyamides (nylon 6,6), polyesters (terylene).

### (c) Thermoplastic polymers

* Linear or slightly branched long-chain molecules.
* They soften on heating and harden on cooling, **repeatedly**, so they can be remoulded.
* Intermolecular forces are intermediate between those of elastomers and fibres.
* Examples: polythene, polystyrene, polyvinyls.

### (d) Thermosetting polymers

* Cross linked or heavily branched molecules.
* On heating in moulds they undergo extensive cross linking and become hard and **infusible**. They cannot be softened and reused.
* Examples: bakelite, urea-formaldehyde resins.

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    Order of intermolecular forces: Elastomers < Thermoplastics < Fibres. Thermosetting polymers owe their rigidity to a permanent covalent cross-linked network.

## 5. Classification Based on Growth Polymerisation

* Addition polymers are also called **chain growth polymers**, because the chain grows by successive addition of monomer units to a reactive (free radical or ionic) chain end.
* Condensation polymers are also called **step growth polymers**, because the chain grows through separate, independent condensation steps between functional groups.

# [![ChemistryEdu Logo](../../images/favicon.svg){ align=left, width=3.8% }](../../index.md)  Hydrocarbons | Alkenes

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond.

* With one double bond, an alkene has two hydrogen atoms less than the corresponding alkane. So the general formula is:

!!! tip ""

    $$C_nH_{2n}$$

* Alkenes are also called **olefins** (oil forming), because the first member, ethylene (C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>4</sub>), forms an oily liquid with chlorine.

## Structure of the Double Bond

* A C=C double bond consists of one strong &sigma; bond (bond enthalpy about 397 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>), formed by head-on overlap of sp<sup>2</sup> hybrid orbitals, and one weak &pi; bond (about 284 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>), formed by sideways overlap of the two unhybridised 2p orbitals.
* The C=C bond length (134 pm) is shorter than the C–C single bond (154 pm).
* In ethene, each carbon is sp<sup>2</sup> hybridised. The H–C–C angle is 121.7&deg;, the H–C–H angle is 116.6&deg; and the C–H bond length is 110 pm. The &pi; electron cloud lies above and below the plane of the molecule.
* The C=C bond enthalpy (681 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>) is greater than that of the C–C single bond in ethane (348 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>).

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    The &pi; bond is weak and its electrons are loosely held. So alkenes act as a source of mobile electrons and are easily attacked by **electrophilic reagents** (electron seeking species). This makes alkenes less stable than alkanes, and they readily add reagents to become single-bonded compounds.

## Nomenclature

* The longest chain **containing the double bond** is selected.
* Numbering starts from the end nearer to the double bond.
* The suffix '-ane' of the alkane is replaced by '-ene'.
* The first member, CH<sub>2</sub> (methene), has a very short life. The first stable member is C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>4</sub>, ethylene (common) or ethene (IUPAC).

## Isomerism in Alkenes

Alkenes show both structural isomerism and geometrical isomerism.

### 1. Structural Isomerism

Ethene and propene have only one structure each. C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>8</sub> can be written in three ways:

!!! example "Isomers of C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>8</sub>"

    $$I.\ CH_2=CH-CH_2-CH_3\ \ \ (But\text{-}1\text{-}ene)$$

    $$II.\ CH_3-CH=CH-CH_3\ \ \ (But\text{-}2\text{-}ene)$$

    $$III.\ CH_2=C(CH_3)-CH_3\ \ \ (2\text{-}Methylprop\text{-}1\text{-}ene)$$

* I and III, and II and III, are **chain isomers**.
* I and II are **position isomers** (they differ in the position of the double bond).

### 2. Geometrical Isomerism

* Rotation about a C=C bond is **restricted**, because rotating would break the &pi; bond.
* If each doubly bonded carbon carries two different atoms or groups (type YXC=CXY), two different spatial arrangements are possible. Such stereoisomers are called **geometrical isomers**.
* **Cis isomer:** identical atoms or groups lie on the **same side** of the double bond.
* **Trans isomer:** identical atoms or groups lie on **opposite sides** of the double bond.
* Cis and trans isomers have the same structure but different configuration, so they differ in m.p., b.p., dipole moment, solubility, etc.

!!! example "Example: But-2-ene"

    * cis-But-2-ene: both CH<sub>3</sub> groups on the same side; b.p. 277 K; dipole moment 0.33 D.
    * trans-But-2-ene: CH<sub>3</sub> groups on opposite sides; b.p. 274 K; dipole moment almost zero.

* The cis form is more polar. In the trans form, the two C–CH<sub>3</sub> bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel each other, making it non-polar.
* Among solids, the trans isomer has a higher melting point than the cis form (it packs better).
* Geometrical isomerism is also shown by alkenes of the types XYC=CXZ and XYC=CZW.

!!! note "Note"

    If either doubly bonded carbon carries two identical groups (for example (CH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>C=CH–C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>5</sub> or CH<sub>2</sub>=CBr<sub>2</sub>), cis-trans isomerism is **not** possible.

## Preparation of Alkenes

### 1. From Alkynes (Partial Reduction)

* Alkynes are partially reduced with a calculated amount of H<sub>2</sub> in the presence of palladised charcoal partially deactivated with poisons like sulphur compounds or quinoline. This catalyst is called **Lindlar's catalyst**. The alkene formed has **cis** geometry.
* Reduction with sodium in liquid ammonia gives **trans** alkenes.

!!! tip ""

    $$RC \equiv CR' + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd/C} cis\text{-}RCH=CHR'$$

    $$RC \equiv CR' + H_2 \xrightarrow{Na/liquid\ NH_3} trans\text{-}RCH=CHR'$$

    $$CH \equiv CH + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd/C} CH_2=CH_2$$

    $$CH_3-C \equiv CH + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd/C} CH_3-CH=CH_2$$

* Propene obtained this way does not show geometrical isomerism, because one of its doubly bonded carbons (=CH<sub>2</sub>) has two identical H atoms.

### 2. From Alkyl Halides (Dehydrohalogenation)

* Alkyl halides, on heating with alcoholic KOH, eliminate one molecule of halogen acid to form alkenes. This is called **dehydrohalogenation**.
* It is a **&beta;-elimination** reaction, because the H atom is removed from the &beta;-carbon (the carbon next to the one carrying the halogen).

!!! tip ""

    $$CH_3-CH_2X \xrightarrow[&Delta;]{alc.\ KOH} CH_2=CH_2 + HX\ \ \ (X = Cl,\ Br,\ I)$$

* Rate depends on the halogen and the alkyl group:

!!! tip ""

    $$Halogen:\ I > Br > Cl$$

    $$Alkyl\ group:\ tert > sec > primary$$

### 3. From Vicinal Dihalides (Dehalogenation)

Dihalides with the two halogens on adjacent carbons are called **vicinal dihalides**. On treatment with zinc metal they lose ZnX<sub>2</sub> to form alkenes. This is called **dehalogenation**.

!!! tip ""

    $$CH_2Br-CH_2Br + Zn &rarr; CH_2=CH_2 + ZnBr_2$$

    $$CH_3CHBr-CH_2Br + Zn &rarr; CH_3CH=CH_2 + ZnBr_2$$

### 4. From Alcohols (Acidic Dehydration)

Alcohols, on heating with concentrated sulphuric acid, lose a water molecule to form alkenes. This is also a &beta;-elimination, since the –OH group takes out one H atom from the &beta;-carbon.

!!! tip ""

    $$CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow[&Delta;]{Conc.\ H_2SO_4} CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O$$

## Physical Properties of Alkenes

* Alkenes resemble alkanes in physical properties, except in the types of isomerism and polar nature.
* The first three members are gases, the next fourteen are liquids and the higher ones are solids.
* Ethene is a colourless gas with a faint sweet smell. Other alkenes are colourless and odourless.
* They are insoluble in water but fairly soluble in non-polar solvents like benzene and petroleum ether.
* Boiling point rises regularly with size: every –CH<sub>2</sub> group added increases b.p. by 20–30 K. Straight chain alkenes boil higher than their branched isomers.

# [![ChemistryEdu Logo](../../images/favicon.svg){ align=left, width=3.8% }](../../index.md)  Basic Principles of Organic Chemistry | Methods of Purification

Once an organic compound is extracted from a natural source or synthesised in the laboratory, it must be purified. The method chosen depends on the nature of the compound and the impurity present in it.

The common techniques used for purification are:

1. Sublimation
2. Crystallisation
3. Distillation
4. Differential extraction
5. Chromatography

* The purity of a compound is checked by finding its **melting point or boiling point**. Most pure compounds have sharp melting and boiling points.
* Modern methods of checking purity are based on different chromatographic and spectroscopic techniques.

## 1. Sublimation

* Some solids change directly into vapour on heating, without passing through the liquid state. This is called sublimation.
* This principle is used to separate **sublimable compounds from non-sublimable impurities**. For example, camphor can be separated from calcium sulphate by sublimation.

## 2. Crystallisation

* This is one of the most commonly used techniques for purifying **solid** organic compounds.
* It is based on the **difference in solubilities** of the compound and the impurities in a suitable solvent.

### Procedure

* The impure compound is dissolved in a solvent in which it is **sparingly soluble at room temperature but appreciably soluble at higher temperature**.
* The solution is concentrated to get a nearly saturated solution.
* On cooling, the pure compound crystallises out and is removed by filtration.
* The filtrate (**mother liquor**) contains the impurities and a small quantity of the compound.

### Points to remember

* If the compound is highly soluble in one solvent and very little soluble in another, crystallisation can be done in a **mixture of these solvents**.
* Impurities which impart colour to the solution are removed by adsorbing them over **activated charcoal**.
* Repeated crystallisation is needed when the impurities have solubilities comparable to the compound.

## 3. Distillation

Distillation is used to separate (i) volatile liquids from non-volatile impurities and (ii) liquids having a sufficient difference in their boiling points.

### (a) Simple distillation

* Liquids having different boiling points vaporise at different temperatures. The vapours are cooled and the liquids so formed are collected separately.
* The mixture is taken in a round bottom flask and heated carefully. Vapours of the lower boiling component form first; they are condensed in a condenser and collected in a receiver. The higher boiling component vaporises later and is collected separately.
* Example: **Chloroform (b.p. 334 K) and aniline (b.p. 457 K)** are easily separated by simple distillation.

### (b) Fractional distillation

* If the difference in boiling points of two liquids is small, simple distillation cannot separate them, as both vaporise in the same temperature range.
* In fractional distillation, the vapours are passed through a **fractionating column** fitted over the mouth of the round bottom flask, before condensation.
* Vapours of the higher boiling liquid condense before those of the lower boiling liquid. So, the vapours rising up the column become richer in the more volatile component.
* The column provides many surfaces for heat exchange between the rising vapours and the descending condensed liquid. Some of the condensing liquid takes heat from the rising vapours and revaporises.
* At the top, the vapours are pure in the low boiling component; they pass into the condenser and are collected. After successive distillations, the liquid left in the flask is enriched in the high boiling component.
* Each successive condensation and vaporisation unit in the column is called a **theoretical plate**. Commercial columns have hundreds of plates.
* Application: separating different fractions of **crude oil in the petroleum industry**.

### (c) Distillation under reduced pressure (vacuum distillation)

* Used to purify liquids having **very high boiling points** and those which **decompose at or below their boiling points**.
* A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure. By reducing the pressure on its surface with a water pump or vacuum pump, the liquid boils at a temperature lower than its normal boiling point.
* Application: **glycerol is separated from spent-lye in the soap industry**.

### (d) Steam distillation

* Used to separate substances which are **steam volatile and immiscible with water**.
* Steam from a steam generator is passed through a heated flask containing the liquid. The mixture of steam and the volatile organic compound is condensed and collected. The compound is then separated from water using a separating funnel.
* The liquid boils when the sum of the vapour pressures of the organic liquid (p<sub>1</sub>) and of water (p<sub>2</sub>) becomes equal to the atmospheric pressure (p):

!!! tip ""

    $$p = p_1 + p_2$$

* Since p<sub>1</sub> is lower than p, the organic liquid vaporises **at a temperature lower than its boiling point**. A mixture of water and a water-insoluble substance boils close to, but below, 373 K.
* Example: **aniline is separated from an aniline-water mixture** by steam distillation.

## 4. Differential Extraction

* When an organic compound is present in an aqueous medium, it is separated by shaking it with an **organic solvent in which it is more soluble than in water**.
* The organic solvent and water must be **immiscible**, so that they form two distinct layers which can be separated using a **separatory funnel**.
* The organic solvent is then removed by distillation or evaporation to get back the compound.
* If the compound is only slightly soluble in the organic solvent, a very large quantity of solvent would be needed. In such cases, **continuous extraction** is used, in which the same solvent is used repeatedly.

## 5. Chromatography

* Chromatography (Greek *chroma* = colour, as it was first used for separating coloured plant substances) is used to separate mixtures into their components, purify compounds and test their purity.
* The mixture is applied onto a **stationary phase** (a solid or a liquid). A pure solvent, a mixture of solvents, or a gas (the **mobile phase**) is allowed to move slowly over it. The components get gradually separated from one another.
* Based on the principle involved, chromatography is classified as:
    * Adsorption chromatography
    * Partition chromatography

### (a) Adsorption chromatography

* It is based on the fact that **different compounds are adsorbed on an adsorbent to different degrees**. Commonly used adsorbents are **silica gel and alumina**.
* As the mobile phase moves over the stationary phase (adsorbent), the components of the mixture move by different distances.
* Two main types are column chromatography and thin layer chromatography.

#### Column chromatography

* The mixture is separated over a column of adsorbent (stationary phase) packed in a glass tube fitted with a stopcock at its lower end.
* The mixture adsorbed on the adsorbent is placed on top of the column, and a suitable **eluant** (a liquid or a mixture of liquids) is allowed to flow down slowly.
* The **most readily adsorbed substances are retained near the top**, and the others come down to various distances in the column, giving complete separation.

#### Thin layer chromatography (TLC)

* A thin layer (about **0.2 mm** thick) of adsorbent (silica gel or alumina) is spread over a glass plate. This is called a **TLC plate or chromaplate**.
* The mixture is applied as a small spot about 2 cm above one end of the plate. The plate is placed in a closed jar containing the eluant.
* As the eluant rises up the plate, the components move up to different distances depending on their degree of adsorption.
* The relative adsorption of each component is expressed by its **retardation factor (R<sub>f</sub> value)**:

!!! tip ""

    $$R_f = {Distance\ moved\ by\ the\ substance\ from\ base\ line\ (x) \over Distance\ moved\ by\ the\ solvent\ from\ base\ line\ (y)}$$

* **Detecting spots:**
    * Coloured compounds are visible due to their own colour.
    * Colourless compounds which fluoresce are seen under **ultraviolet light**.
    * The plate may be placed in a covered jar containing a few crystals of **iodine**; compounds which adsorb iodine show up as brown spots.
    * A suitable reagent may be sprayed on the plate. For example, **amino acids are detected by spraying ninhydrin** solution.

### (b) Partition chromatography

* It is based on **continuous differential partitioning** of the components of a mixture between the stationary and mobile phases.
* **Paper chromatography** is a type of partition chromatography. A special chromatography paper is used, which contains **water trapped in it; this water acts as the stationary phase**.
* A strip of the paper, spotted at the base with the mixture, is suspended in a suitable solvent or mixture of solvents (the mobile phase).
* The solvent rises up the paper by **capillary action** and flows over the spot. The paper selectively retains different components according to their differing partition between the two phases.
* The developed paper strip is called a **chromatogram**. Coloured components are seen as spots at different heights; colourless ones are detected under UV light or with a spray reagent, as in TLC.

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    * Sublimation: sublimable solid + non-sublimable impurity.
    * Crystallisation: solids, based on difference in solubility.
    * Simple distillation: liquids with large difference in boiling points.
    * Fractional distillation: liquids with small difference in boiling points.
    * Vacuum distillation: high boiling liquids or liquids that decompose at their boiling point.
    * Steam distillation: steam volatile liquids immiscible with water.
    * Chromatography: the best and latest technique for isolation, purification and separation of organic compounds.

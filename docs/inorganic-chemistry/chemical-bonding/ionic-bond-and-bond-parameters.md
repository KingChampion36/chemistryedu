## Ionic or Electrovalent Bond

An ionic or electrovalent bond is formed by the electrostatic attraction between positive and negative ions, produced by transfer of electrons from one atom to another.

* The formation of an ionic compound depends mainly on:
    * The ease of formation of the positive and negative ions from the neutral atoms.
    * The arrangement of the ions in the solid, that is, the lattice of the crystalline compound.
* Formation of a positive ion involves **ionization** (removal of electrons), and formation of a negative ion involves **addition of electrons**.

!!! tip ""

    $$M_{(g)} &rarr; M^+_{(g)} + e^- \qquad (Ionization\ enthalpy)$$

    $$X_{(g)} + e^- &rarr; X^-_{(g)} \qquad (Electron\ gain\ enthalpy)$$

    $$M^+_{(g)} + X^-_{(g)} &rarr; MX_{(s)}$$

* The **electron gain enthalpy**, &Delta;<sub>eg</sub>H, is the enthalpy change when a gaseous atom in its ground state gains an electron. It may be exothermic or endothermic.
* Ionization is always endothermic.
* **Electron affinity** is the negative of the energy change accompanying electron gain.

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    Ionic bonds are formed more easily between elements with comparatively **low ionization enthalpies** and elements with comparatively **high negative electron gain enthalpy**.

* Most ionic compounds have cations from metals and anions from non-metals. The ammonium ion, NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup> (made of two non-metals) is an exception; it is the cation of many ionic compounds.
* In the crystalline state, ionic compounds are orderly three-dimensional arrangements of cations and anions held together by coulombic forces. Example: NaCl has the rock salt structure.

### Why ionic compounds are stable

* The sum of ionization enthalpy and electron gain enthalpy may be **positive**, yet the crystal is stable because of the energy released when the crystal lattice forms.

!!! example "Energetics of NaCl"

    $$Na_{(g)} &rarr; Na^+_{(g)} + e^-;\ \ &Delta;H = +495.8\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

    $$Cl_{(g)} + e^- &rarr; Cl^-_{(g)};\ \ &Delta;H = -348.7\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

    $$Sum = +147.1\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

    This is more than compensated by the enthalpy of lattice formation of NaCl<sub>(s)</sub> (-788 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>). So the energy released is more than the energy absorbed.

* Thus, the stability of an ionic compound is measured by its **enthalpy of lattice formation**, and not simply by achieving an octet around the ions in the gaseous state.

## Lattice Enthalpy

The lattice enthalpy of an ionic solid is defined as the energy required to completely separate one mole of a solid ionic compound into gaseous constituent ions.

* Example: The lattice enthalpy of NaCl is 788 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>. This means 788 kJ of energy is needed to separate one mole of solid NaCl into one mole of Na<sup>+</sup><sub>(g)</sub> and one mole of Cl<sup>-</sup><sub>(g)</sub> to an infinite distance.
* This involves both attraction between opposite charges and repulsion between like charges. As the crystal is three-dimensional, lattice enthalpy cannot be calculated from these forces alone; the crystal geometry must also be taken into account.

## Bond Parameters

### 1. Bond Length

Bond length is defined as the equilibrium distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms in a molecule.

* It is measured by spectroscopic, X-ray diffraction and electron-diffraction methods.
* In a covalent bond A-B, each atom contributes its **covalent radius** to the bond length:

!!! tip ""

    $$R = r_A + r_B$$

    Where, R = bond length and r<sub>A</sub>, r<sub>B</sub> = covalent radii of A and B.

* **Covalent radius** is roughly the radius of an atom's core which is in contact with the core of an adjacent atom in a bonded situation. It is half the distance between two similar atoms joined by a covalent bond.
* **van der Waals radius** is the overall size of the atom, including its valence shell, in a non-bonded situation. It is half the distance between two similar atoms in separate molecules in a solid.
* For chlorine, covalent radius = 99 pm (Cl-Cl bond length = 198 pm) and van der Waals radius = 180 pm.

### 2. Bond Angle

Bond angle is defined as the angle between the orbitals containing bonding electron pairs around the central atom in a molecule or complex ion.

* It is expressed in degrees and determined by spectroscopic methods.
* It gives an idea of the distribution of orbitals around the central atom and hence helps to find the **shape** of the molecule. Example: the H-O-H bond angle in water is 104.5&deg;.

### 3. Bond Enthalpy

Bond enthalpy is defined as the amount of energy required to break one mole of bonds of a particular type between two atoms in the gaseous state. Its unit is kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>.

!!! tip ""

    $$H_{2(g)} &rarr; H_{(g)} + H_{(g)};\ \ &Delta;_aH^0 = 435.8\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

    $$O_{2(g)} &rarr; O_{(g)} + O_{(g)};\ \ &Delta;_aH^0 = 498\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

    $$N_{2(g)} &rarr; N_{(g)} + N_{(g)};\ \ &Delta;_aH^0 = 946.0\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

    $$HCl_{(g)} &rarr; H_{(g)} + Cl_{(g)};\ \ &Delta;_aH^0 = 431.0\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

* Larger the bond dissociation enthalpy, stronger is the bond.
* In polyatomic molecules, the same type of bond may need different energies to break. In H<sub>2</sub>O, the second O-H bond is in a changed chemical environment:

!!! tip ""

    $$H_2O_{(g)} &rarr; H_{(g)} + OH_{(g)};\ \ &Delta;_aH_1^0 = 502\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

    $$OH_{(g)} &rarr; H_{(g)} + O_{(g)};\ \ &Delta;_aH_2^0 = 427\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

* So, for polyatomic molecules we use the **mean or average bond enthalpy**, obtained by dividing the total bond dissociation enthalpy by the number of bonds broken:

!!! tip ""

    $$Average\ bond\ enthalpy\ of\ O-H = {502 + 427 \over 2} = 464.5\ kJ\ mol^{-1}$$

### 4. Bond Order

In the Lewis description of a covalent bond, the bond order is given by the number of bonds between the two atoms in a molecule.

* Bond order of H<sub>2</sub> = 1, O<sub>2</sub> = 2, N<sub>2</sub> = 3 and CO = 3.
* Isoelectronic molecules and ions have identical bond orders. Example: F<sub>2</sub> and O<sub>2</sub><sup>2-</sup> have bond order 1, while N<sub>2</sub>, CO and NO<sup>+</sup> have bond order 3.

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    With increase in bond order, bond enthalpy increases and bond length decreases.

### 5. Resonance Structures

Whenever a single Lewis structure cannot describe a molecule accurately, a number of structures with similar energy, positions of nuclei, bonding and non-bonding pairs of electrons are taken as the canonical structures of the hybrid, which describes the molecule accurately.

* **Example: Ozone, O<sub>3</sub>.** A single Lewis structure shows one O-O single bond (148 pm) and one O=O double bond (121 pm). But experimentally, both O-O bonds in O<sub>3</sub> are equal (128 pm), which is between a single and a double bond.
* So O<sub>3</sub> is represented by two **canonical (resonance) structures**, and the actual molecule is their **resonance hybrid**. Resonance is shown by a double-headed arrow.

!!! tip ""

    $$O=O^+-O^- \longleftrightarrow\ ^-O-O^+=O$$

* **Carbonate ion, CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2-</sup>:** A single structure with two C-O single bonds and one C=O double bond gives unequal bonds, but experimentally all C-O bonds are equivalent. So CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2-</sup> is a resonance hybrid of three canonical forms, with the double bond on each of the three O atoms in turn.
* **Carbon dioxide, CO<sub>2</sub>:** The experimental C-O bond length is 115 pm, which lies between C=O (121 pm) and C&equiv;O (110 pm). So CO<sub>2</sub> is a hybrid of three canonical forms:

!!! tip ""

    $$O=C=O \longleftrightarrow\ ^-O-C\equiv O^+ \longleftrightarrow\ ^+O\equiv C-O^-$$

!!! note "Important points about resonance"

    * Resonance **stabilises** the molecule, because the energy of the resonance hybrid is less than that of any single canonical structure.
    * Resonance **averages** the bond characteristics as a whole.
    * The canonical forms have **no real existence**.
    * The molecule does not exist in one canonical form for some fraction of time and in other forms for other fractions of time.
    * There is no equilibrium between canonical forms like there is between keto and enol forms in tautomerism.
    * The molecule has a single structure, the resonance hybrid, which cannot be shown by a single Lewis structure.

## Questions

!!! question "Write the favourable factors for the formation of an ionic bond."

    * **Low ionization enthalpy** of the metal atom, so that it easily loses electrons to form a cation.
    * **High negative electron gain enthalpy** of the non-metal atom, so that it easily gains electrons to form an anion.
    * **High lattice enthalpy** of the resulting solid, so that the energy released in forming the crystal lattice more than compensates for the energy needed to form the gaseous ions.

!!! question "How do you express the bond strength in terms of bond order?"

    * Bond strength (bond enthalpy) increases as bond order increases, while bond length decreases.
    * Example: N<sub>2</sub> (bond order 3) has bond enthalpy 946 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>, O<sub>2</sub> (bond order 2) has 498 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>, and H<sub>2</sub> (bond order 1) has 435.8 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>.

!!! question "Explain the structure of the CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2-</sup> ion in terms of resonance."

    * A single Lewis structure with two C-O single bonds and one C=O double bond would give unequal bonds.
    * Experimentally, all three carbon-oxygen bonds in CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2-</sup> are equivalent.
    * So the carbonate ion is a resonance hybrid of three canonical forms. In each form the double bond is on a different O atom and the other two O atoms carry a negative charge each:

    $$O=C(O^-)_2\ \longleftrightarrow\ ^-O-C(=O)-O^-\ \longleftrightarrow\ (^-O)_2C=O$$

    * In the hybrid, each C-O bond has a bond order of about 1.33 and the negative charge is spread over all three O atoms.

!!! question "H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>3</sub> can be drawn as (1) with one H bonded directly to P and two P-OH groups, or (2) with three P-OH groups. Can these be taken as canonical forms of a resonance hybrid?"

    * No. Canonical structures must have the **same positions of nuclei** and differ only in the arrangement of electrons.
    * In structure (1), one H atom is attached to P, while in structure (2), that H atom is attached to O. The positions of atoms are different.
    * So these are two different structures, not resonance forms of H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>3</sub>.

!!! question "Write the resonance structures for SO<sub>3</sub>, NO<sub>2</sub> and NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup>."

    * **SO<sub>3</sub>:** The S=O double bond can be with any of the three O atoms (the other two being S-O single bonds with formal charges), giving three equivalent canonical forms.

    $$O=S^{2+}(O^-)_2\ \longleftrightarrow\ ^-O-S^{2+}(=O)-O^-\ \longleftrightarrow\ (^-O)_2S^{2+}=O$$

    * **NO<sub>2</sub>:** The double bond can be with either O atom; the odd electron stays on N.

    $$O=\dot{N}^+-O^-\ \longleftrightarrow\ ^-O-\dot{N}^+=O$$

    * **NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup>:** Like carbonate, the N=O double bond can be with any of the three O atoms.

    $$O=N^+(O^-)_2\ \longleftrightarrow\ ^-O-N^+(=O)-O^-\ \longleftrightarrow\ (^-O)_2N^+=O$$

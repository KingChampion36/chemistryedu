# [![ChemistryEdu Logo](../../images/favicon.svg){ align=left, width=3.8% }](../../index.md)  Chemical Bonding | Kössel-Lewis Approach

The attractive force which holds the constituents (atoms, ions, etc.) together in different chemical species is called a **chemical bond**.

* Apart from noble gases, no element normally exists as a free, independent atom. Atoms group together to form molecules.
* Atoms combine because bonding lowers the energy of the system, and a system with lower energy is more stable.
* Different theories have been put forward to explain why atoms combine and why molecules have definite shapes: the Kössel-Lewis approach, VSEPR theory, Valence Bond (VB) theory and Molecular Orbital (MO) theory.

## Kössel-Lewis Approach

In 1916, Kössel and Lewis independently explained chemical bonding on the basis of the inertness of noble gases.

* Lewis pictured an atom as a positively charged **kernel** (nucleus + inner electrons) surrounded by an outer shell that can hold a maximum of eight electrons.
* An outer shell with eight electrons (an **octet**) is a particularly stable arrangement.
* Lewis proposed that atoms achieve a stable octet when they are linked by chemical bonds, either by **transfer** of electrons (as in NaCl) or by **sharing** a pair of electrons (as in Cl<sub>2</sub>, H<sub>2</sub>, F<sub>2</sub>).

### Lewis Symbols

* Only the outer shell (valence) electrons take part in chemical combination. These are called **valence electrons**.
* In a Lewis symbol, the valence electrons are shown as dots around the symbol of the element.
* For second period elements: Li has 1 dot, Be has 2, B has 3, C has 4, N has 5, O has 6, F has 7 and Ne has 8 dots.

!!! tip ""

    $$Li\cdot \qquad \cdot Be \cdot \qquad \cdot \dot{B} \cdot \qquad \cdot \dot{C} \cdot \qquad \cdot \ddot{N} \cdot \qquad :\ddot{O} \cdot \qquad :\ddot{F} \cdot \qquad :\ddot{Ne}:$$

* **Significance:** The number of dots gives the number of valence electrons, which helps to find the **group valence** of an element.
* The group valence is generally either equal to the number of dots or 8 minus the number of dots.

### Kössel's Postulates (Electrovalent Bond)

* In the periodic table, the highly electronegative halogens and the highly electropositive alkali metals are separated by the noble gases.
* A negative ion is formed when a halogen atom gains an electron, and a positive ion is formed when an alkali metal atom loses an electron.
* The ions formed attain the stable noble gas configuration, ns<sup>2</sup>np<sup>6</sup> (helium has a duplet, 1s<sup>2</sup>).
* The positive and negative ions are held together by electrostatic attraction.

!!! example "Formation of NaCl and CaF<sub>2</sub>"

    $$Na &rarr; Na^+ + e^- \qquad [Ne]3s^1 &rarr; [Ne]$$

    $$Cl + e^- &rarr; Cl^- \qquad [Ne]3s^2 3p^5 &rarr; [Ne]3s^2 3p^6\ or\ [Ar]$$

    $$Na^+ + Cl^- &rarr; NaCl\ or\ Na^+Cl^-$$

    $$Ca &rarr; Ca^{2+} + 2e^- \qquad [Ar]4s^2 &rarr; [Ar]$$

    $$F + e^- &rarr; F^- \qquad [He]2s^2 2p^5 &rarr; [He]2s^2 2p^6\ or\ [Ne]$$

    $$Ca^{2+} + 2F^- &rarr; CaF_2\ or\ Ca^{2+}(F^-)_2$$

* The bond formed by electrostatic attraction between positive and negative ions is called an **electrovalent bond**.
* The **electrovalence** is equal to the number of unit charges on the ion. So calcium has an electrovalence of +2 and chlorine has an electrovalence of -1.

## Octet Rule

According to the electronic theory of chemical bonding (Kössel and Lewis, 1916), atoms combine either by transfer of valence electrons from one atom to another or by sharing of valence electrons, in order to have an octet in their valence shells. This is known as the **octet rule**.

## Covalent Bond

* Langmuir (1919) refined Lewis's ideas and introduced the term **covalent bond**.
* In Cl<sub>2</sub>, each Cl atom ([Ne]3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>5</sup>) is one electron short of the argon configuration. Each Cl contributes one electron to a shared pair, and both atoms attain an octet.

!!! tip ""

    $$:\ddot{\underset{..}{Cl}}\cdot + \cdot\ddot{\underset{..}{Cl}}: &rarr; :\ddot{\underset{..}{Cl}}:\ddot{\underset{..}{Cl}}:\ \ or\ \ Cl-Cl$$

* Conditions for writing Lewis dot structures of covalent molecules:
    * Each bond is formed by sharing one electron pair between the atoms.
    * Each combining atom contributes at least one electron to the shared pair.
    * The combining atoms attain the outer shell noble gas configuration by sharing electrons.
* In H<sub>2</sub>O, each H atom attains a duplet and O attains an octet. In CCl<sub>4</sub>, C and all four Cl atoms attain an octet.

### Single, Double and Triple Bonds

* When two atoms share **one** electron pair, they are joined by a **single covalent bond**. Example: Cl-Cl, H-H.
* When two atoms share **two** electron pairs, a **double bond** is formed. Example: O=C=O in CO<sub>2</sub> and C=C in ethene (C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>4</sub>).
* When two atoms share **three** electron pairs, a **triple bond** is formed. Example: N&equiv;N in N<sub>2</sub> and H-C&equiv;C-H in ethyne (C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>2</sub>).

## Lewis Structures

Lewis dot structures show the bonding in molecules and ions in terms of shared pairs of electrons and the octet rule. They can be written using these steps:

### 1. Count the total electrons

* Add the valence electrons of all the combining atoms. Example: CH<sub>4</sub> has 4 (from C) + 4 &times; 1 (from H) = 8 valence electrons.
* For an **anion**, add one electron for each negative charge. CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2-</sup> has 2 extra electrons.
* For a **cation**, subtract one electron for each positive charge. NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup> has 1 electron less.

### 2. Write the skeletal structure

* The least electronegative atom usually occupies the central position. In NF<sub>3</sub> and CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2-</sup>, N and C are central, while F and O are terminal.

### 3. Distribute the electrons

* First place one shared pair for each single bond.
* Use the remaining electron pairs either for multiple bonds or as **lone pairs**, so that each bonded atom gets an octet (H gets a duplet).

!!! example "Lewis structures of some molecules"

    $$H_2:\quad H:H\quad or\quad H-H$$

    $$O_2:\quad \ddot{O}::\ddot{O}\quad or\quad \ddot{O}=\ddot{O}$$

    $$N_2:\quad :N:::N:\quad or\quad :N\equiv N:$$

    $$NF_3:\quad F-\underset{\underset{F}{|}}{\ddot{N}}-F\quad (each\ F\ carries\ three\ lone\ pairs)$$

    $$CO_3^{2-}:\quad \left[O=\underset{\underset{O}{|}}{C}-O\right]^{2-}$$

## Formal Charge

* In a polyatomic ion, the net charge belongs to the ion as a whole, not to a particular atom. However, it is useful to assign a **formal charge** to each atom.
* The formal charge of an atom is the difference between the number of valence electrons in the free atom and the number of electrons assigned to it in the Lewis structure.

!!! tip ""

    $$Formal\ charge\ (F.C.) = V - L - {1 \over 2}S$$

    Where, V = total valence electrons in the free atom, L = total non-bonding (lone pair) electrons and S = total bonding (shared) electrons.

* The counting assumes that the atom owns one electron of each shared pair and both electrons of a lone pair.

!!! example "Formal charges in ozone, O<sub>3</sub>"

    In the Lewis structure of O<sub>3</sub>, the central O (1) has one lone pair, one double bond and one single bond. The end O (2) is double bonded with two lone pairs and the end O (3) is single bonded with three lone pairs.

    $$Central\ O\ (1):\ 6 - 2 - {1 \over 2}(6) = +1$$

    $$End\ O\ (2):\ 6 - 4 - {1 \over 2}(4) = 0$$

    $$End\ O\ (3):\ 6 - 6 - {1 \over 2}(2) = -1$$

    So O<sub>3</sub> is written as O=O<sup>+</sup>-O<sup>-</sup>.

* Formal charges do **not** indicate real charge separation in the molecule. They only help keep track of valence electrons.
* Formal charges help to select the best Lewis structure: **generally the lowest energy structure is the one with the smallest formal charges on the atoms.**

## Limitations of the Octet Rule

The octet rule is useful, especially for organic compounds and second period elements, but it is not universal. There are three types of exceptions.

### 1. Incomplete octet of the central atom

* In some compounds the central atom has fewer than eight electrons. This is common for elements with fewer than four valence electrons.
* Examples: LiCl (Li has 2 electrons around it), BeH<sub>2</sub> (Be has 4) and BCl<sub>3</sub> (B has 6). AlCl<sub>3</sub> and BF<sub>3</sub> are similar.

### 2. Odd-electron molecules

* Molecules with an odd number of electrons, like nitric oxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO<sub>2</sub>), cannot satisfy the octet rule for all atoms.

### 3. Expanded octet

* Elements of the third period and beyond have 3d orbitals available (besides 3s and 3p) for bonding. So they can have more than eight electrons around the central atom.
* Examples: PF<sub>5</sub> (10 electrons around P), SF<sub>6</sub> (12 electrons around S) and H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> (12 electrons around S), and many coordination compounds.
* Interestingly, sulphur also forms compounds that obey the octet rule. In SCl<sub>2</sub>, S has an octet around it.

### Other drawbacks of the octet theory

* The octet rule is based on the inertness of noble gases, but some noble gases (like xenon and krypton) do form compounds such as XeF<sub>2</sub>, KrF<sub>2</sub> and XeOF<sub>2</sub>.
* It does not explain the **shape** of molecules.
* It says nothing about the **energy** of a molecule, so it cannot explain the relative stability of molecules.

## Questions

!!! question "Explain the formation of a chemical bond."

    * Atoms combine to attain a stable noble gas configuration (octet, or duplet for H) and to lower their energy.
    * They do this either by **transfer** of electrons, forming ions held by electrostatic attraction (ionic bond, e.g. NaCl), or by **sharing** electron pairs (covalent bond, e.g. Cl<sub>2</sub>).
    * The attractive force that holds the atoms or ions together in the resulting species is the chemical bond.

!!! question "Write Lewis dot symbols for atoms of the following elements: Mg, Na, B, O, N, Br."

    * The number of dots equals the number of valence electrons.

    $$Mg:\ \cdot Mg \cdot \quad (2) \qquad Na:\ Na\cdot \quad (1) \qquad B:\ \cdot\dot{B}\cdot \quad (3)$$

    $$O:\ :\ddot{O}\cdot \quad (6) \qquad N:\ \cdot\ddot{N}\cdot \quad (5) \qquad Br:\ :\ddot{\underset{..}{Br}}\cdot \quad (7)$$

!!! question "Write Lewis symbols for the following atoms and ions: S and S<sup>2-</sup>; Al and Al<sup>3+</sup>; H and H<sup>-</sup>."

    $$S:\ :\ddot{S}\cdot \qquad S^{2-}:\ \left[:\ddot{\underset{..}{S}}:\right]^{2-}$$

    $$Al:\ \cdot\dot{Al}\cdot \qquad Al^{3+}:\ [Al]^{3+}$$

    $$H:\ H\cdot \qquad H^-:\ [H:]^-$$

!!! question "Define octet rule. Write its significance and limitations."

    * **Octet rule:** Atoms combine by transfer or sharing of valence electrons so as to have eight electrons (an octet) in their valence shells.
    * **Significance:** It explains why atoms combine, and helps to write the structures of most organic compounds and compounds of second period elements.
    * **Limitations:** It cannot explain compounds with an incomplete octet (BeH<sub>2</sub>, BCl<sub>3</sub>), odd-electron molecules (NO, NO<sub>2</sub>) and expanded octets (PF<sub>5</sub>, SF<sub>6</sub>). It also fails for compounds of noble gases (XeF<sub>2</sub>), and says nothing about the shapes of molecules or their relative stability.

!!! question "Use Lewis symbols to show electron transfer between the following atoms to form cations and anions: (a) K and S (b) Ca and O (c) Al and N."

    $$(a)\ 2K\cdot + \cdot\ddot{S}: &rarr; 2K^+ + [:\ddot{\underset{..}{S}}:]^{2-}\ \ or\ \ K_2S$$

    $$(b)\ Ca: + \cdot\ddot{O}: &rarr; Ca^{2+} + [:\ddot{\underset{..}{O}}:]^{2-}\ \ or\ \ CaO$$

    $$(c)\ \cdot\dot{Al}\cdot + \cdot\ddot{N}\cdot &rarr; Al^{3+} + [:\ddot{\underset{..}{N}}:]^{3-}\ \ or\ \ AlN$$

!!! question "Write the correct Lewis structure for acetic acid, CH<sub>3</sub>COOH."

    * Acetic acid has 24 valence electrons (2 C &times; 4 + 4 H &times; 1 + 2 O &times; 6).
    * The methyl carbon forms four single bonds (three C-H and one C-C). There is no C=C double bond.
    * The carboxyl carbon forms one C=O double bond and one C-O single bond. Each O atom has two lone pairs.

    $$H_3C-\underset{\underset{:O:}{||}}{C}-\ddot{O}-H$$

!!! question "Write the Lewis dot structure of the CO molecule."

    * **Step 1:** Valence shell configurations are C: 2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>2</sup> and O: 2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>4</sup>.

    $$Total\ valence\ electrons = 4 + 6 = 10$$

    * **Step 2:** The skeletal structure is C O.

    * **Step 3:** A single bond between C and O, with the octet of O completed, leaves two electrons as a lone pair on C. Then carbon has only 4 electrons, so its octet is incomplete.

    $$:C-\ddot{\underset{..}{O}}:\ \ (octet\ of\ C\ incomplete)$$

    * So we need a triple bond between C and O, which completes the octet of both atoms:

    $$:C\equiv O:$$

!!! question "Write the Lewis structure of the nitrite ion, NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>."

    * **Step 1:** Count valence electrons of N (2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>3</sup>), two O (2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>4</sup>) and one extra electron for the negative charge.

    $$Total = 5 + (2 \times 6) + 1 = 18\ electrons$$

    * **Step 2:** The skeletal structure is O N O.

    * **Step 3:** With two N-O single bonds and the octets of both O atoms completed, only two electrons remain as a lone pair on N. So N does not get an octet.

    * Hence a double bond is required between N and one of the O atoms. This gives two equivalent Lewis structures:

    $$\left[\ddot{O}=\ddot{N}-\ddot{\underset{..}{O}}:\right]^- \quad or \quad \left[:\ddot{\underset{..}{O}}-\ddot{N}=\ddot{O}\right]^-$$

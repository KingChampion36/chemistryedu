## Bonding in Coordination Compounds

Werner was the first to describe bonding in coordination compounds, but his theory could not answer some basic questions:

* Why do only certain elements form coordination compounds?
* Why do the bonds in coordination compounds have directional properties?
* Why do coordination compounds have characteristic magnetic and optical properties?

Several theories explain bonding in coordination compounds - Valence Bond Theory (VBT), Crystal Field Theory (CFT), Ligand Field Theory (LFT) and Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT). Here we will study VBT; CFT is discussed in the next article.

## Valence Bond Theory

* According to VBT, the metal atom or ion, under the influence of ligands, uses its (n-1)d, ns, np or ns, np, nd orbitals for **hybridisation**.
* This gives a set of equivalent hybrid orbitals of definite geometry such as octahedral, tetrahedral or square planar.
* These hybrid orbitals overlap with ligand orbitals that donate electron pairs, forming coordinate bonds.

Coordination number | Type of hybridisation | Distribution of hybrid orbitals in space
--------------------|-----------------------|-----------------------------------------
4 | sp<sup>3</sup> | Tetrahedral
4 | dsp<sup>2</sup> | Square planar
5 | sp<sup>3</sup>d | Trigonal bipyramidal
6 | sp<sup>3</sup>d<sup>2</sup> | Octahedral
6 | d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> | Octahedral

* It is usually possible to predict the geometry of a complex if its magnetic behaviour is known.
* In the orbital diagrams below, each column is one orbital, &uarr;&darr; are the metal's own electrons, and **xx** is an electron pair donated by a ligand.

## Octahedral Complexes

### 1. [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> - inner orbital complex

* Cobalt is in the +3 oxidation state. Co<sup>3+</sup> has the configuration 3d<sup>6</sup>.
* NH<sub>3</sub> causes the 3d electrons to pair up, leaving two 3d orbitals empty. These two 3d orbitals, one 4s and three 4p orbitals hybridise to give six d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> hybrid orbitals.

Species | 3d | 3d | 3d | 3d | 3d | 4s | 4p | 4p | 4p
--------|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----
Co<sup>3+</sup> ion | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | | | |
d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> hybridised Co<sup>3+</sup> | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | | | | | |
[Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | xx | xx | xx | xx | xx | xx

* Six pairs of electrons, one from each NH<sub>3</sub>, occupy the six hybrid orbitals. The complex is **octahedral** and **diamagnetic** (no unpaired electron).
* Since the inner d orbitals (3d) are used in hybridisation, [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> is called an **inner orbital** or **low spin** or **spin paired** complex.

### 2. [CoF<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> - outer orbital complex

* Co<sup>3+</sup> is again 3d<sup>6</sup>, but F<sup>-</sup> does not cause pairing of the 3d electrons.
* The outer 4d orbitals are used: one 4s, three 4p and two 4d orbitals give six sp<sup>3</sup>d<sup>2</sup> hybrid orbitals.

Species | 3d | 3d | 3d | 3d | 3d | 4s | 4p | 4p | 4p | 4d | 4d
--------|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----
Co<sup>3+</sup> ion | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | | | | | |
[CoF<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | xx | xx | xx | xx | xx | xx

* The complex is octahedral and **paramagnetic** with four unpaired electrons.
* Since the outer d orbitals (4d) are used, [CoF<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> is called an **outer orbital** or **high spin** or **spin free** complex.

!!! tip "Inner vs Outer Orbital Complexes"

    * **Inner orbital (low spin):** d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> hybridisation using (n-1)d orbitals; electrons paired as far as possible. Example: [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup>.

    * **Outer orbital (high spin):** sp<sup>3</sup>d<sup>2</sup> hybridisation using nd orbitals; maximum number of unpaired electrons. Example: [CoF<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup>.

## Tetrahedral Complexes

### [NiCl<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup>

* In tetrahedral complexes, one s and three p orbitals hybridise to give four sp<sup>3</sup> orbitals oriented tetrahedrally.
* Nickel is in the +2 oxidation state with configuration 3d<sup>8</sup>.

Species | 3d | 3d | 3d | 3d | 3d | 4s | 4p | 4p | 4p
--------|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----
Ni<sup>2+</sup> ion | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | | | |
[NiCl<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup> | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | xx | xx | xx | xx

* Each Cl<sup>-</sup> donates one electron pair. The complex is tetrahedral and **paramagnetic** with two unpaired electrons (high spin).
* Similarly, [Ni(CO)<sub>4</sub>] is tetrahedral, but it is **diamagnetic** because nickel is in the zero oxidation state (3d<sup>10</sup> after rearrangement of the 4s electrons) and has no unpaired electron.

## Square Planar Complexes

### [Ni(CN)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup>

* In square planar complexes, the hybridisation is dsp<sup>2</sup> (one 3d, one 4s and two 4p orbitals).
* Nickel is in the +2 oxidation state (3d<sup>8</sup>). CN<sup>-</sup> forces the two unpaired 3d electrons to pair up, making one 3d orbital empty.

Species | 3d | 3d | 3d | 3d | 3d | 4s | 4p | 4p | 4p
--------|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----
Ni<sup>2+</sup> ion | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr; | &uarr; | | | |
dsp<sup>2</sup> hybridised Ni<sup>2+</sup> | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | | | | |
[Ni(CN)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup> | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | &uarr;&darr; | xx | xx | xx | xx |

* Each hybrid orbital receives an electron pair from a cyanide ion. The complex is **diamagnetic** (low spin).

!!! note "Note"

    Hybrid orbitals do not actually exist. Hybridisation is only a mathematical manipulation of the wave equation for the atomic orbitals involved.

## Magnetic Properties of Coordination Compounds

* The magnetic moment of a coordination compound can be measured by magnetic susceptibility experiments. The results tell us about the structure of the complex.
* The spin-only magnetic moment is related to the number of unpaired electrons (n):

!!! tip ""

    $$&mu; = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ BM$$

Unpaired electrons (n) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5
-----------------------|---|---|---|---|---
&mu; (BM) | 1.73 | 2.83 | 3.87 | 4.90 | 5.92

### Magnetic behaviour and the number of d electrons

* For metal ions with up to three 3d electrons, like Ti<sup>3+</sup> (d<sup>1</sup>), V<sup>3+</sup> (d<sup>2</sup>) and Cr<sup>3+</sup> (d<sup>3</sup>), two 3d orbitals are already vacant for d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> hybridisation. Their free ions and complexes have similar magnetic behaviour.
* When more than three 3d electrons are present, a vacant pair of 3d orbitals is obtained only by pairing electrons against Hund's rule. For d<sup>4</sup> (Cr<sup>2+</sup>, Mn<sup>3+</sup>), d<sup>5</sup> (Mn<sup>2+</sup>, Fe<sup>3+</sup>) and d<sup>6</sup> (Fe<sup>2+</sup>, Co<sup>3+</sup>), this pairing leaves two, one and zero unpaired electrons respectively.
* The magnetic data agree with maximum spin pairing in many cases, especially for d<sup>6</sup> ions. But for d<sup>4</sup> and d<sup>5</sup> ions, there are complications, which VBT explains using inner and outer orbital complexes:

Complex | Metal ion | Hybridisation | Type | Unpaired electrons
--------|-----------|---------------|------|-------------------
[Mn(CN)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> | Mn<sup>3+</sup> (d<sup>4</sup>) | d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> | Inner orbital | 2
[MnCl<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> | Mn<sup>3+</sup> (d<sup>4</sup>) | sp<sup>3</sup>d<sup>2</sup> | Outer orbital | 4
[Fe(CN)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> | Fe<sup>3+</sup> (d<sup>5</sup>) | d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> | Inner orbital | 1
[FeF<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> | Fe<sup>3+</sup> (d<sup>5</sup>) | sp<sup>3</sup>d<sup>2</sup> | Outer orbital | 5
[Co(C<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>)<sub>3</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> | Co<sup>3+</sup> (d<sup>6</sup>) | d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> | Inner orbital | 0 (diamagnetic)
[CoF<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> | Co<sup>3+</sup> (d<sup>6</sup>) | sp<sup>3</sup>d<sup>2</sup> | Outer orbital | 4

## Limitations of Valence Bond Theory

VBT explains the formation, structures and magnetic behaviour of coordination compounds to a large extent, but it has the following shortcomings:

1. It involves a number of assumptions.
2. It does not give a quantitative interpretation of magnetic data.
3. It does not explain the colour exhibited by coordination compounds.
4. It does not give a quantitative interpretation of the thermodynamic or kinetic stabilities of coordination compounds.
5. It does not make exact predictions about tetrahedral and square planar structures of 4-coordinate complexes.
6. It does not distinguish between weak and strong ligands.

## Questions

!!! question "The spin only magnetic moment of [MnBr<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup> is 5.9 BM. Predict the geometry of the complex ion."

    * The coordination number of Mn<sup>2+</sup> is 4, so the complex is either tetrahedral (sp<sup>3</sup>) or square planar (dsp<sup>2</sup>).

    $$5.9 = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ &rArr;\ n = 5$$

    * Mn<sup>2+</sup> is 3d<sup>5</sup>, and all five electrons remain unpaired. No 3d orbital is vacant for dsp<sup>2</sup> hybridisation, so sp<sup>3</sup> hybridisation occurs.

    Hence, [MnBr<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup> is **tetrahedral**.

!!! question "Explain on the basis of valence bond theory that [Ni(CN)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup> ion with square planar structure is diamagnetic and the [NiCl<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup> ion with tetrahedral geometry is paramagnetic."

    In both complexes, Ni is in the +2 state with 3d<sup>8</sup> configuration (two unpaired electrons).

    * CN<sup>-</sup> is a strong ligand. It pairs up the two unpaired 3d electrons, leaving one 3d orbital empty. dsp<sup>2</sup> hybridisation gives a square planar complex with no unpaired electrons - diamagnetic.

    * Cl<sup>-</sup> is a weak ligand and cannot pair up the 3d electrons. sp<sup>3</sup> hybridisation (4s + 4p) gives a tetrahedral complex with two unpaired electrons - paramagnetic.

!!! question "[NiCl<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup> is paramagnetic while [Ni(CO)<sub>4</sub>] is diamagnetic though both are tetrahedral. Why?"

    * In [Ni(CO)<sub>4</sub>], Ni is in the zero oxidation state (3d<sup>8</sup>4s<sup>2</sup>). In the presence of CO, the 4s electrons move into 3d and all d electrons pair up (3d<sup>10</sup>). sp<sup>3</sup> hybridisation gives a tetrahedral complex with no unpaired electron - diamagnetic.

    * In [NiCl<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup>, Ni is in the +2 state (3d<sup>8</sup>). Cl<sup>-</sup> is a weak ligand and cannot pair up the two unpaired electrons - paramagnetic.

!!! question "[Fe(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> is strongly paramagnetic whereas [Fe(CN)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3-</sup> is weakly paramagnetic. Explain."

    In both, Fe is in the +3 state (3d<sup>5</sup>).

    * CN<sup>-</sup> is a strong ligand. The 3d electrons pair up, leaving only **one** unpaired electron. d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> hybridisation gives an inner orbital complex - weakly paramagnetic.

    * H<sub>2</sub>O is a weak ligand. The 3d electrons do not pair up. sp<sup>3</sup>d<sup>2</sup> hybridisation gives an outer orbital complex with **five** unpaired electrons - strongly paramagnetic.

!!! question "Explain why [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> is an inner orbital complex whereas [Ni(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> is an outer orbital complex."

    * In [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup>, Co<sup>3+</sup> is 3d<sup>6</sup>. In the presence of NH<sub>3</sub>, the 3d electrons pair up, leaving two 3d orbitals empty for d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> hybridisation. So it is an inner orbital complex.

    * In [Ni(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2+</sup>, Ni<sup>2+</sup> is 3d<sup>8</sup>. Even after pairing, only one 3d orbital can be emptied, so two vacant 3d orbitals are not available. The outer 4d orbitals are used, giving sp<sup>3</sup>d<sup>2</sup> hybridisation. So it is an outer orbital complex.

!!! question "Predict the number of unpaired electrons in the square planar [Pt(CN)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup> ion."

    Pt<sup>2+</sup> has the configuration 5d<sup>8</sup>. For a square planar shape, the hybridisation is dsp<sup>2</sup>. The two unpaired electrons in 5d pair up to make one 5d orbital empty for dsp<sup>2</sup> hybridisation.

    Hence, there is **no unpaired electron** in [Pt(CN)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup>.

!!! question "[Cr(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> is paramagnetic while [Ni(CN)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup> is diamagnetic. Explain why."

    * Cr<sup>3+</sup> is 3d<sup>3</sup>. Two 3d orbitals are already empty, so d<sup>2</sup>sp<sup>3</sup> hybridisation takes place without any pairing. The three unpaired electrons remain, so [Cr(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> is paramagnetic.

    $$&mu; = \sqrt{3(3+2)} = 3.87\ BM$$

    * Ni<sup>2+</sup> is 3d<sup>8</sup>. CN<sup>-</sup> pairs up the two unpaired electrons, and dsp<sup>2</sup> hybridisation gives a square planar complex with no unpaired electrons - diamagnetic.

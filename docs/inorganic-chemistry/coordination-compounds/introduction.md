# [![ChemistryEdu Logo](../../images/favicon.svg){ align=left, width=3.8% }](../../index.md)  Coordination Compounds | Introduction and Werner's Theory

Coordination compounds are compounds in which a central metal atom or ion is bonded to a fixed number of anions or neutral molecules. They were earlier called complex compounds.

## Introduction

* Transition metals form a large number of complex compounds in which the metal atom is bound to a number of anions or neutral molecules. In modern terminology, these are called **coordination compounds**.
* Coordination compounds are the backbone of modern inorganic chemistry, bio-inorganic chemistry and the chemical industry.
* Many vital components of biological systems are coordination compounds:
    * **Chlorophyll** - coordination compound of magnesium
    * **Haemoglobin** - coordination compound of iron
    * **Vitamin B<sub>12</sub>** - coordination compound of cobalt
* They are also used in metallurgical processes, industrial catalysts, analytical reagents, electroplating, textile dyeing and medicinal chemistry.

## Werner's Experiments

* Alfred Werner (1866-1919), a Swiss chemist, was the first to explain the structures of coordination compounds. He prepared and studied a large number of them using simple experimental techniques.
* Werner proposed that a metal ion shows two kinds of valence - a **primary valence** and a **secondary valence**.
* Binary compounds such as CrCl<sub>3</sub>, CoCl<sub>2</sub> and PdCl<sub>2</sub> have a primary valence of 3, 2 and 2 respectively.
* Werner studied a series of compounds of cobalt(III) chloride with ammonia. On adding excess silver nitrate solution in cold, only some of the chloride ions were precipitated as AgCl; the rest remained in solution.

Compound (1 mol)                    | Colour  | Moles of AgCl precipitated
------------------------------------|---------|---------------------------
CoCl<sub>3</sub>.6NH<sub>3</sub>    | Yellow  | 3
CoCl<sub>3</sub>.5NH<sub>3</sub>    | Purple  | 2
CoCl<sub>3</sub>.4NH<sub>3</sub>    | Green   | 1
CoCl<sub>3</sub>.4NH<sub>3</sub>    | Violet  | 1

* These observations, together with conductivity measurements, can be explained if:
    1. Six groups in all (chloride ions, ammonia molecules or both) remain bonded to the cobalt ion during the reaction.
    2. The compounds are formulated with the bonded groups inside a square bracket. The atoms within the square bracket form a single entity which does not dissociate under the reaction conditions.

Colour  | Formula                                                                       | Conductivity corresponds to
--------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------
Yellow  | [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> 3Cl<sup>-</sup>                 | 1:3 electrolyte
Purple  | [CoCl(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>5</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> 2Cl<sup>-</sup>               | 1:2 electrolyte
Green   | [CoCl<sub>2</sub>(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>+</sup> Cl<sup>-</sup>     | 1:1 electrolyte
Violet  | [CoCl<sub>2</sub>(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>+</sup> Cl<sup>-</sup>     | 1:1 electrolyte

* Only the chloride ions outside the square bracket are free to precipitate as AgCl.
* The number of groups bound directly to the metal ion was termed the **secondary valence** by Werner. In all the above compounds, the secondary valence of cobalt is 6.
* The last two compounds have the same empirical formula, CoCl<sub>3</sub>.4NH<sub>3</sub>, but different properties (green and violet). Such compounds are called **isomers**.

## Postulates of Werner's Theory

Werner put forward his theory of coordination compounds in 1898. Its main postulates are:

### 1. Two types of valences

* In coordination compounds, metals show two types of linkages (valences) - primary and secondary.

### 2. Primary valence

* Primary valences are normally ionisable.
* They are satisfied by negative ions.
* In modern terms, the primary valence corresponds to the oxidation state of the metal.

### 3. Secondary valence

* Secondary valences are non-ionisable.
* They are satisfied by neutral molecules or negative ions.
* The secondary valence is equal to the coordination number and is fixed for a metal.

### 4. Spatial arrangement

* The ions or groups bound to the metal by secondary linkages have characteristic spatial arrangements corresponding to different coordination numbers.
* In modern language, such spatial arrangements are called **coordination polyhedra**.
* The species inside the square bracket is called the **coordination entity** or **complex**, and the ions outside the square bracket are called **counter ions**.
* Werner further proposed that octahedral, tetrahedral and square planar shapes are the most common in coordination compounds of transition metals.

!!! example "Example"

    * Octahedral: [Co(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup>, [CoCl(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>5</sub>]<sup>2+</sup>, [CoCl<sub>2</sub>(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>+</sup>

    * Tetrahedral: [Ni(CO)<sub>4</sub>]

    * Square planar: [PtCl<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2-</sup>

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    In modern language, Werner's primary and secondary linkages are recognised as the ionisable (ionic) and non-ionisable (covalent) bonds respectively.

## Difference between a Double Salt and a Complex

* Both double salts and complexes are formed by combining two or more stable compounds in a fixed (stoichiometric) ratio.
* **Double salts** dissociate completely into simple ions when dissolved in water. Examples: carnallite, KCl.MgCl<sub>2</sub>.6H<sub>2</sub>O; Mohr's salt, FeSO<sub>4</sub>.(NH<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>.6H<sub>2</sub>O; potash alum, KAl(SO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>.12H<sub>2</sub>O.
* **Complexes** do not dissociate completely. For example, the complex ion [Fe(CN)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>4-</sup> of K<sub>4</sub>[Fe(CN)<sub>6</sub>] does not dissociate into Fe<sup>2+</sup> and CN<sup>-</sup> ions.

!!! tip ""

    $$Mohr's\ salt:\ FeSO_4.(NH_4)_2SO_4.6H_2O_{(aq)} &rarr; Fe^{2+} + 2NH_4^+ + 2SO_4^{2-} + 6H_2O$$

    $$K_4[Fe(CN)_6]_{(aq)} &rarr; 4K^+ + [Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$$

## Questions

!!! question "On the basis of the following observations made with aqueous solutions, assign secondary valences to the metals: <br>(i) PdCl<sub>2</sub>.4NH<sub>3</sub> - 2 mol AgCl <br>(ii) NiCl<sub>2</sub>.6H<sub>2</sub>O - 2 mol AgCl <br>(iii) PtCl<sub>4</sub>.2HCl - 0 mol AgCl <br>(iv) CoCl<sub>3</sub>.4NH<sub>3</sub> - 1 mol AgCl <br>(v) PtCl<sub>2</sub>.2NH<sub>3</sub> - 0 mol AgCl <br>(moles of AgCl precipitated per mole of compound with excess AgNO<sub>3</sub>)"

    The chlorides that precipitate are outside the coordination sphere. The remaining chlorides and the neutral molecules are bonded to the metal, and their total gives the secondary valence.

    Compound | Formulation | Secondary valence
    ---------|-------------|------------------
    PdCl<sub>2</sub>.4NH<sub>3</sub> | [Pd(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>]Cl<sub>2</sub> | 4
    NiCl<sub>2</sub>.6H<sub>2</sub>O | [Ni(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub>]Cl<sub>2</sub> | 6
    PtCl<sub>4</sub>.2HCl | H<sub>2</sub>[PtCl<sub>6</sub>] | 6
    CoCl<sub>3</sub>.4NH<sub>3</sub> | [CoCl<sub>2</sub>(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>4</sub>]Cl | 6
    PtCl<sub>2</sub>.2NH<sub>3</sub> | [PtCl<sub>2</sub>(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>] | 4

!!! question "FeSO<sub>4</sub> solution mixed with (NH<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> solution in 1:1 molar ratio gives the test of Fe<sup>2+</sup> ion, but CuSO<sub>4</sub> solution mixed with aqueous ammonia in 1:4 molar ratio does not give the test of Cu<sup>2+</sup> ion. Explain why."

    * FeSO<sub>4</sub> and (NH<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> in 1:1 ratio form a double salt, Mohr's salt (FeSO<sub>4</sub>.(NH<sub>4</sub>)<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>.6H<sub>2</sub>O), which dissociates completely in water. So free Fe<sup>2+</sup> ions are present.

    * CuSO<sub>4</sub> and NH<sub>3</sub> in 1:4 ratio form a complex which does not dissociate into Cu<sup>2+</sup> ions:

    $$CuSO_4 + 4NH_3 &rarr; [Cu(NH_3)_4]SO_4$$

    Hence, free Cu<sup>2+</sup> ions are not available and the test for Cu<sup>2+</sup> is negative.

# [![ChemistryEdu Logo](../../images/favicon.svg){ align=left, width=3.8% }](../../index.md)  Hydrogen | Introduction

Hydrogen is the first element of the periodic table and has the simplest atomic structure of all elements: one proton and one electron. In elemental form it exists as a diatomic molecule, H<sub>2</sub>, called **dihydrogen**.

* Hydrogen forms more compounds than any other element.
* It is the most abundant element in the universe and the third most abundant element on the surface of the earth.
* Since it burns cleanly and releases a lot of energy, hydrogen is seen as a major future source of energy.

In this article, we will discuss the position of hydrogen in the periodic table, its occurrence and its isotopes.

## Position of Hydrogen in the Periodic Table

* Electronic configuration of hydrogen is 1s<sup>1</sup>.
* Hydrogen shows resemblance with both alkali metals (group 1) and halogens (group 17). At the same time it differs from both of them. That is why its position in the periodic table has long been debated.

### 1. Resemblance with alkali metals

* Its electronic configuration (1s<sup>1</sup>) is similar to the outer electronic configuration of alkali metals (ns<sup>1</sup>).
* Like alkali metals, it can lose one electron to form a unipositive ion, H<sup>+</sup>.
* Like alkali metals, it forms oxides, halides and sulphides.

### 2. Resemblance with halogens

* Like halogens (ns<sup>2</sup>np<sup>5</sup>), it is short of just one electron to attain the configuration of the nearest noble gas, helium (1s<sup>2</sup>).
* Like halogens, it can gain one electron to form a uninegative ion, H<sup>-</sup>.
* Like halogens, it forms a diatomic molecule and combines with elements to form hydrides and a large number of covalent compounds.
* In terms of ionization enthalpy, hydrogen resembles halogens more than alkali metals:

Element            | Li  | H    | F    |
-------------------|-----|------|------|
&Delta;<sub>i</sub>H (kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>) | 520 | 1312 | 1680 |

### 3. Differences from alkali metals and halogens

* Unlike alkali metals, hydrogen has a very high ionization enthalpy and shows no metallic character under normal conditions.
* Unlike halogens, its reactivity is very low.
* Loss of the electron leaves only a bare nucleus (H<sup>+</sup>) of size about 1.5 &times; 10<sup>-3</sup> pm. This is extremely small compared to normal atomic and ionic sizes of 50 to 200 pm. As a result, H<sup>+</sup> never exists freely and is always associated with other atoms or molecules.

!!! tip "Conclusion"

    Hydrogen is unique in its behaviour. Therefore, it is best placed separately in the periodic table.

## Occurrence of Dihydrogen

* Dihydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe (about 70% of the total mass of the universe) and is the principal element in the solar atmosphere.
* The giant planets Jupiter and Saturn consist mostly of hydrogen.
* Being very light, it is much less abundant (0.15% by mass) in the earth's atmosphere.
* In the combined form, it makes up 15.4% of the earth's crust and the oceans.
* Besides water, it occurs in plant and animal tissues, carbohydrates, proteins, hydrides including hydrocarbons and many other compounds.

## Isotopes of Hydrogen

Hydrogen has three isotopes:

!!! tip ""

    $$Protium:\ {}^1_1H$$

    $$Deuterium:\ {}^2_1H\ or\ D$$

    $$Tritium:\ {}^3_1H\ or\ T$$

* The isotopes differ only in the number of neutrons in the nucleus. Protium has no neutron, deuterium (also called **heavy hydrogen**) has one neutron and tritium has two neutrons.
* Protium is the predominant form. Terrestrial hydrogen contains 0.0156% of deuterium, mostly in the form of HD.
* Tritium is present only to the extent of about one atom per 10<sup>18</sup> atoms of protium.
* Only tritium is radioactive. It emits low energy &beta;<sup>-</sup> particles with a half-life of 12.33 years.
* In 1934, Harold C. Urey received the Nobel Prize for separating the hydrogen isotope of mass number 2 (deuterium) by physical methods.

!!! note "Note"

    Since the isotopes have the same electronic configuration, they have almost the same chemical properties. They differ only in their **rates of reaction**, mainly because of their different bond dissociation enthalpies. Their physical properties, however, differ considerably due to the large differences in their masses.

## Questions

!!! question "Justify the position of hydrogen in the periodic table on the basis of its electronic configuration."

    Hydrogen has the configuration 1s<sup>1</sup>.

    * Like alkali metals (ns<sup>1</sup>), it has one electron in its valence shell and can lose it to form H<sup>+</sup>. So, it can be placed in group 1.
    * Like halogens (ns<sup>2</sup>np<sup>5</sup>), it is one electron short of the noble gas configuration (He, 1s<sup>2</sup>) and can gain one electron to form H<sup>-</sup>. So, it can also be placed in group 17.

    However, it has a much higher ionization enthalpy than alkali metals, is non-metallic, and is much less reactive than halogens. Moreover, H<sup>+</sup> is extremely small and cannot exist freely. Because of this unique behaviour, hydrogen is best placed separately in the periodic table.

!!! question "Write the names of isotopes of hydrogen. What is the mass ratio of these isotopes?"

    The isotopes of hydrogen are protium (<sup>1</sup>H), deuterium (<sup>2</sup>H or D) and tritium (<sup>3</sup>H or T).

    Their mass numbers are 1, 2 and 3. Hence:

    $$Mass\ ratio\ (H : D : T) = 1 : 2 : 3$$

!!! question "Why does hydrogen occur in a diatomic form rather than in a monoatomic form under normal conditions?"

    A hydrogen atom has only one electron (1s<sup>1</sup>), so its valence shell is incomplete. By sharing its electron with another hydrogen atom, each atom attains the stable helium configuration (1s<sup>2</sup>). The H-H bond formed is very strong (435.88 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>), so the H<sub>2</sub> molecule is far more stable than free H atoms. Therefore, hydrogen exists as H<sub>2</sub> under normal conditions.

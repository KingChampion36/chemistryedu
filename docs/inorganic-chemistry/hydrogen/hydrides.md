# [![ChemistryEdu Logo](../../images/favicon.svg){ align=left, width=3.8% }](../../index.md)  Hydrogen | Hydrides

Under suitable conditions, dihydrogen combines with almost all elements except noble gases to form binary compounds called **hydrides**. If E is the symbol of an element, its hydride can be written as EH<sub>x</sub> (e.g., MgH<sub>2</sub>) or E<sub>m</sub>H<sub>n</sub> (e.g., B<sub>2</sub>H<sub>6</sub>).

Hydrides are classified into three categories:

* Ionic or saline or saltlike hydrides
* Covalent or molecular hydrides
* Metallic or non-stoichiometric hydrides

## 1. Ionic or Saline Hydrides

* These are stoichiometric compounds of hydrogen formed with most of the s-block elements, which are highly electropositive.
* The lighter metal hydrides such as LiH, BeH<sub>2</sub> and MgH<sub>2</sub> show significant covalent character. In fact, BeH<sub>2</sub> and MgH<sub>2</sub> are polymeric in structure.
* Ionic hydrides are crystalline, non-volatile and non-conducting in the solid state.
* Their melts conduct electricity, and on electrolysis liberate dihydrogen at the anode. This confirms the presence of the H<sup>-</sup> ion.

!!! tip ""

    $$2H^-_{(melt)} \xrightarrow{anode} H_{2(g)} + 2e^-$$

* Saline hydrides react violently with water, producing dihydrogen gas.

!!! tip ""

    $$NaH_{(s)} + H_2O_{(aq)} &rarr; NaOH_{(aq)} + H_{2(g)}$$

* Lithium hydride is rather unreactive towards O<sub>2</sub> or Cl<sub>2</sub> at moderate temperatures. It is therefore used to synthesise other useful hydrides.

!!! tip ""

    $$8LiH + Al_2Cl_6 &rarr; 2LiAlH_4 + 6LiCl$$

    $$2LiH + B_2H_6 &rarr; 2LiBH_4$$

## 2. Covalent or Molecular Hydrides

* Dihydrogen forms molecular compounds with most p-block elements. Familiar examples are CH<sub>4</sub>, NH<sub>3</sub>, H<sub>2</sub>O and HF.
* Being covalent, they are volatile compounds.
* Based on the relative number of electrons and bonds in their Lewis structures, molecular hydrides are classified into three types:

### (a) Electron-deficient hydrides

* These have too few electrons to write a conventional Lewis structure.
* Example: diborane (B<sub>2</sub>H<sub>6</sub>). In fact, all elements of group 13 form electron-deficient compounds.
* They act as **Lewis acids**, i.e., electron acceptors.

### (b) Electron-precise hydrides

* These have exactly the required number of electrons to write conventional Lewis structures.
* All elements of group 14 form such compounds (e.g., CH<sub>4</sub>). They are tetrahedral in geometry.

### (c) Electron-rich hydrides

* These have excess electrons, present as lone pairs.
* Elements of groups 15-17 form such compounds. NH<sub>3</sub> has 1 lone pair, H<sub>2</sub>O has 2 and HF has 3 lone pairs.
* They behave as **Lewis bases**, i.e., electron donors.
* Lone pairs on highly electronegative atoms like N, O and F lead to hydrogen bonding between molecules, which causes association of molecules.

Type              | Group | Example                                   | Behaviour   |
------------------|-------|-------------------------------------------|-------------|
Electron-deficient | 13   | B<sub>2</sub>H<sub>6</sub>                 | Lewis acid  |
Electron-precise  | 14    | CH<sub>4</sub>                            | Neither (tetrahedral) |
Electron-rich     | 15-17 | NH<sub>3</sub>, H<sub>2</sub>O, HF        | Lewis base  |

## 3. Metallic or Non-stoichiometric (Interstitial) Hydrides

* These are formed by many d-block and f-block elements.
* Metals of groups 7, 8 and 9 do not form hydrides. From group 6, only chromium forms a hydride (CrH).
* They conduct heat and electricity, though not as efficiently as their parent metals.
* Unlike saline hydrides, they are almost always **non-stoichiometric**, being deficient in hydrogen. For these hydrides, the law of constant composition does not hold.

!!! example "Examples"

    LaH<sub>2.87</sub>, YbH<sub>2.55</sub>, TiH<sub>1.5-1.8</sub>, ZrH<sub>1.3-1.75</sub>, VH<sub>0.56</sub>, NiH<sub>0.6-0.7</sub>, PdH<sub>0.6-0.8</sub>

* Earlier it was believed that hydrogen occupies interstices in the metal lattice, causing distortion without changing the lattice type. So they were called **interstitial hydrides**.
* Recent studies show that, except for hydrides of Ni, Pd, Ce and Ac, the other hydrides of this class have a lattice different from that of the parent metal.
* The ability of transition metals to absorb hydrogen is widely used in catalytic reduction and hydrogenation reactions.
* Some metals (e.g., Pd, Pt) can absorb a very large volume of hydrogen. So, they can be used as hydrogen storage media. This gives them high potential for **hydrogen storage** and as a **source of energy**.

## Questions

!!! question "Would you expect the hydrides of N, O and F to have lower boiling points than the hydrides of their subsequent group members? Give reasons."

    On the basis of molecular masses alone, the boiling points of NH<sub>3</sub>, H<sub>2</sub>O and HF should be lower than those of the hydrides of the next members of their groups (PH<sub>3</sub>, H<sub>2</sub>S, HCl).

    However, N, O and F are highly electronegative, so extensive hydrogen bonding exists in their hydrides. Extra energy is needed to break these hydrogen bonds. Hence, the boiling points of NH<sub>3</sub>, H<sub>2</sub>O and HF are actually **higher** than those of the hydrides of their subsequent group members.

!!! question "Can phosphorus with outer electronic configuration 3s<sup>2</sup>3p<sup>3</sup> form PH<sub>5</sub>?"

    Although phosphorus shows +3 and +5 oxidation states, it cannot form PH<sub>5</sub>. Apart from other factors, the high &Delta;<sub>a</sub>H (atomisation enthalpy) of dihydrogen and the &Delta;<sub>eg</sub>H (electron gain enthalpy) of hydrogen do not favour phosphorus showing its highest oxidation state with hydrogen. Hence PH<sub>5</sub> is not formed.

!!! question "What do you understand by (i) electron-deficient, (ii) electron-precise, and (iii) electron-rich compounds of hydrogen? Provide justification with suitable examples."

    (i) **Electron-deficient** hydrides have too few electrons to write a conventional Lewis structure. Example: B<sub>2</sub>H<sub>6</sub>. Boron has only 3 valence electrons, so B<sub>2</sub>H<sub>6</sub> has 12 electrons, which are not enough for 8 normal two-electron bonds.

    (ii) **Electron-precise** hydrides have exactly enough electrons for a conventional Lewis structure. Example: CH<sub>4</sub>. Carbon's 4 valence electrons form 4 C-H bonds with no lone pair left.

    (iii) **Electron-rich** hydrides have extra electrons present as lone pairs. Examples: NH<sub>3</sub> (1 lone pair), H<sub>2</sub>O (2 lone pairs), HF (3 lone pairs).

!!! question "What characteristics do you expect from an electron-deficient hydride with respect to its structure and chemical reactions?"

    * **Structure:** Since there are not enough electrons for normal two-centre two-electron bonds, such hydrides exist in associated forms with bridging hydrogen atoms (e.g., B<sub>2</sub>H<sub>6</sub> instead of BH<sub>3</sub>).
    * **Reactions:** They are electron acceptors, so they act as Lewis acids and readily react with Lewis bases (electron donors) such as NH<sub>3</sub>.

!!! question "Do you expect the carbon hydrides of the type C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub> to act as 'Lewis' acid or base? Justify your answer."

    No. C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub> hydrides are electron-precise. All the valence electrons of carbon are used in bonding and there are neither vacant orbitals nor lone pairs. So, they can neither accept nor donate electron pairs, and do not act as Lewis acids or bases.

!!! question "What do you understand by the term 'non-stoichiometric hydrides'? Do you expect this type of hydrides to be formed by alkali metals? Justify your answer."

    Non-stoichiometric hydrides are hydrides in which the ratio of metal to hydrogen is not a simple whole number and can vary, e.g., LaH<sub>2.87</sub>, TiH<sub>1.5-1.8</sub>. They are formed by d- and f-block elements and are deficient in hydrogen.

    Alkali metals do not form such hydrides. Alkali metals are highly electropositive and transfer their electron completely to hydrogen, forming ionic hydrides (M<sup>+</sup>H<sup>-</sup>) of fixed, stoichiometric composition.

!!! question "How do you expect the metallic hydrides to be useful for hydrogen storage? Explain."

    In metallic hydrides, hydrogen is absorbed into the metal lattice. Some metals such as Pd and Pt can accommodate a very large volume of hydrogen. On heating, the absorbed hydrogen is released. Hence, these metals and their alloys can store hydrogen compactly and release it when needed, making them useful as hydrogen storage media.

!!! question "Saline hydrides are known to react with water violently producing fire. Can CO<sub>2</sub>, a well known fire extinguisher, be used in this case? Explain."

    No. Saline hydrides like NaH react with water to give H<sub>2</sub>, which catches fire due to the heat of the reaction. Hot metal hydrides (and the metals formed) are strong reducing agents and can reduce CO<sub>2</sub>, so the fire would not be put out. For example:

    $$NaH + CO_2 &rarr; HCOONa$$

    So, CO<sub>2</sub> cannot be used. Such fires are extinguished with dry sand.

!!! question "How can saline hydrides remove traces of water from organic compounds?"

    Saline hydrides react with water to form the metal hydroxide and dihydrogen gas. The H<sub>2</sub> escapes and the hydroxide can be separated, leaving the organic compound dry.

    $$NaH_{(s)} + H_2O_{(l)} &rarr; NaOH_{(aq)} + H_{2(g)}$$

!!! question "Arrange the following:<br>(i) CaH<sub>2</sub>, BeH<sub>2</sub> and TiH<sub>2</sub> in order of increasing electrical conductance.<br>(ii) LiH, NaH and CsH in order of increasing ionic character.<br>(iii) H-H, D-D and F-F in order of increasing bond dissociation enthalpy.<br>(iv) NaH, MgH<sub>2</sub> and H<sub>2</sub>O in order of increasing reducing property."

    (i) BeH<sub>2</sub> is covalent and non-conducting, CaH<sub>2</sub> is ionic (conducts only when molten) and TiH<sub>2</sub> is metallic.

    $$BeH_2 < CaH_2 < TiH_2$$

    (ii) Ionic character increases as electropositive character of the metal increases down the group.

    $$LiH < NaH < CsH$$

    (iii) F-F bond is weak due to repulsion between lone pairs; D-D is slightly stronger than H-H.

    $$F-F < H-H < D-D$$

    (iv) Ionic hydrides are the strongest reducing agents; H<sub>2</sub>O is a very weak one.

    $$H_2O < MgH_2 < NaH$$

!!! question "What do you expect the nature of hydrides to be, if formed by elements of atomic numbers 15, 19, 23 and 44 with dihydrogen? Compare their behaviour towards water."

    * Z = 15 (P): forms covalent hydride PH<sub>3</sub>. It does not react appreciably with water.
    * Z = 19 (K): forms ionic hydride KH. It reacts violently with water.

    $$KH_{(s)} + H_2O_{(l)} &rarr; KOH_{(aq)} + H_{2(g)}$$

    * Z = 23 (V): forms metallic (non-stoichiometric) hydride, e.g., VH<sub>0.56</sub>. It does not react with water.
    * Z = 44 (Ru): belongs to group 8, which does not form hydrides.
